Few commands are easier to applaud than the care of widows and orphans. __“Pure religion and undefiled,”__ says James, is __“to visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction”__ (James 1:27); even the godless agree, funding the work through the state. It is a good thing, and we are commanded to it. But it is so plainly good that we read past it — and we read past it assuming one thing: that we stand among the givers, never the widow, never the orphan. The symbolic language overturns the assumption.

[discrete]
=== We Are the Widow

A widow is a woman whose husband is dead or has put her away; the fatherless are children left without their father. Now hear how God speaks of His own people when they break covenant. He hands them a bill of divorce:

[quote.scripture, Isaiah 50:1]
____
…Where is the bill of your mother’s *divorcement*, whom I have *put away*?… for your iniquities have ye sold yourselves…
____

And the city, cut off from her Husband, sits alone: __“how is she become as a widow!”__ (Lamentations 1:1). Her children take up the cry: __“We are orphans and fatherless, our mothers are as widows”__ (Lamentations 5:3). This is the turn. The widow and the fatherless are not only the poor down the street; they are the covenant people themselves, severed from the God who was their Husband and their Father. We are the widow; we are the orphans.

[discrete]
=== Thy Maker Is Thine Husband

Which is why God’s fierce defense of the widow and the fatherless is no generic charity — it is a Husband and a Father moving toward His own:

[quote.scripture, Isaiah 54:5]
____
For thy *Maker* is thine *husband*; the LORD of hosts is his name; and thy *Redeemer* the Holy One of Israel…
____

He __“doth execute the judgment of the fatherless and widow, and loveth the stranger, in giving him food and raiment”__ (Deuteronomy 10:18). And the corner of the field left unreaped from the last chapter was reserved for exactly these: __“for the stranger, for the fatherless, and for the widow”__ (Deuteronomy 24:19). The widowed remnant gleans the edge, as Ruth did, and is brought home by the Redeemer.

[discrete]
=== The Stranger

The third name in that gleaning law is a defined symbol, and Scripture fixes its meaning by setting it against its exact opposite:

[quote.scripture, Ephesians 2:19]
____
Now therefore ye are *no more strangers and foreigners*, but *fellowcitizens* with the saints, and of the household of God.
____

The opposite of a stranger is a _fellowcitizen_; so a stranger is a non-citizen — one who belongs to another commonwealth than the one he stands in. And Paul names the commonwealth in the verse just before: the Gentiles had been __“aliens from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers from the covenants of promise, having no hope, and without God”__ (Ephesians 2:12). The covenant is the commonwealth, and to be a stranger is to hold no citizenship in it.

That key turns both ways. Outside the covenant, a man is a stranger to God’s commonwealth — and the law leaves the field’s corner to draw him in. The gleaning stranger is the _ger_, the sojourner who has left his own people to dwell among God’s; Israel had been one in Egypt — __“ye were strangers in the land of Egypt”__ (Leviticus 19:34) — so they must love him, and bring him toward citizenship: let him keep the Passover, and __“he shall be as one that is born in the land”__ (Exodus 12:48). This is the whole of Ruth, a Moabite stranger who gleans the corner and is grafted into the line of the Redeemer, until the far-off are __“no more strangers… but fellowcitizens.”__

And once a man is made a citizen of God’s commonwealth, the symbol inverts: he becomes a stranger to the _world_. The patriarchs __“confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth,”__ and Hebrews gives the reason — __“they seek a country… an heavenly”__ (Hebrews 11:13-16); __“our conversation”__ — the word is _citizenship_ — __“is in heaven”__ (Philippians 3:20); even in the land God says, __“ye are strangers and sojourners with me”__ (Leviticus 25:23). The stranger to the world keeps the one law that is now his: __“I am a stranger in the earth: hide not thy commandments from me”__ (Psalm 119:19). The covenant that ends a man’s estrangement from God begins his estrangement from the world.

[discrete]
=== Food and Raiment

He gives the widow __“food and raiment”__ (Deuteronomy 10:18) — and by now neither word is bare. Scripture makes bread the word of God — __“man doth not live by bread only, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of the LORD”__ (Deuteronomy 8:3) — and the garment, the last chapters showed, is righteousness. So to feed and clothe His widowed people is to give them the word and the covenant covering. And the worst famine is not of bread:

[quote.scripture, Amos 8:11]
____
…I will send a famine in the land, not a famine of bread… but of *hearing the words of the LORD*.
____

This is why the charge to the shepherd is __“Feed my sheep”__ (John 21:17); why the faithful servant is the one who gives the household __“meat in due season”__ (Matthew 24:45); why the commission is to teach the nations __“to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded”__ (Matthew 28:20). To feed is to teach the commandments; to clothe is to robe in righteousness. And the covenant itself is taken the same way — by eating His flesh and drinking His blood: __“the bread that I will give is my flesh”__ (John 6:51), and __“the words that I speak unto you, they are spirit, and they are life”__ (John 6:63). His body is the bread, the word to be eaten and kept; His blood is the covering that washes clean. The crowd could not bear it — __“from that time many of his disciples went back, and walked no more with him”__ (John 6:66) — because they took the symbol for the flesh.

[discrete]
=== Five Loaves and Two Fishes

With the symbols in hand, the feeding of the multitude reads as the lived parable it is. A boy has __“five barley loaves, and two small fishes”__ (John 6:9); from them a multitude is fed, and __“they took up of the fragments that remained twelve baskets full”__ (Matthew 14:20). Five loaves: the five books of the Law, the bread of the word. Two fishes: witnesses, for the Lord sent His disciples __“by two and two”__ (Mark 6:7) to be fishers of men. The bread is blessed, broken, and handed to the disciples, and the disciples to the people — and as the word is shared it multiplies, until the hungry are filled and twelve baskets remain, one for each tribe. The Law, carried by witnesses, feeds the famished, and there is more left at the end than was held at the start.

So the command to care for the widow and the fatherless reaches all the way down. They are God’s own people, cut off and gleaning at the edge; to feed them is to bring them the word, to clothe them is to robe them in righteousness, and to gather them is the work of the Husband who will not leave them widowed. The next time Scripture says _feed the hungry_, hear what is handed across: the bread of His word, to a people hungrier than they know.
