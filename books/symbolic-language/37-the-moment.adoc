Babylon fell in the middle of a boast.
__“I sit a queen, and am no sym:sym-widow[widow], and shall see no sorrow”__ (Revelation 18:7) — and the verse that supplies her exact words, seven centuries earlier, supplies the timing of her answer too:

[quote.scripture, Isaiah 47:9]
____
But these two things shall come to thee *in a moment* in one day, the loss of children, and sym:sym-widow[widowhood]: they shall come upon thee in their perfection…
____

__“In a moment.”__
Not a slow decline, not a siege that could be watched approaching for years — a moment, and both children and husband are gone.
This book has followed one Hebrew word into a dozen chapters now; this one is smaller and sharper than any of them, and once you have it, an entire vocabulary of sudden things in Scripture turns out to be one thing.

[discrete]
=== The Wink of an Eye

The Hebrew behind __“moment”__ is _rega_, and its root meaning is exact: to wink, to blink — the very motion of an eyelid.
It is the shortest interval Scripture has a word for, and it never describes a process.

God tells Moses what would happen if He walked with a stiff-necked people even one stage of the journey: __“I will come up into the midst of thee in a moment, and consume thee”__ (Exodus 33:5).
When Korah’s company rose up against Moses, God did not say _I will judge them in time_; He said, __“Separate yourselves from among this sym:sym-assembly[congregation], that I may consume them in a moment”__ (Numbers 16:21) — and when the plague broke out among the people themselves, the same word is used again: __“that I may consume them *as in a moment*”__ (Numbers 16:45).
Job draws the general rule from such scenes: __“In a moment shall they die, and the people shall be troubled at midnight, and pass away: and the mighty shall be taken away *without hand*”__ (Job 34:20) — no visible weapon, no army, no hand at all; the moment does the work alone.

Scripture makes sure you cannot mistake this for a merely human turn of phrase.
The Greek New Testament reaches for the identical image, unprompted, to describe the resurrection:

[quote.scripture, 1 Corinthians 15:52]
____
In a *moment*, in the *twinkling of an eye*, at the last trump: for the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised incorruptible…
____

__“Moment”__ here is _atomos_ — literally an _uncut_ thing, an atom of time, indivisible — paired with __“the twinkling of an eye,”__ the eye’s own glance.
Two languages, centuries apart, reach for the same picture: the blink of an eye is Scripture’s unit for _instantaneous_, whether the news is a resurrection or a ruin.
The moment is not vague; it is the shortest thing a human body can do.

[discrete]
=== Suddenly

Beside _rega_ stands its constant companion, _pithom_ — __“suddenly”__ — and Strong’s traces it back to the same family of roots: a wink, an unguarded instant, __“used only adverbially, quickly or unexpectedly.”__
The two words travel together so often that they read as one idea wearing two names.
Watch them arrive in the same verse over the ruin of a land: __“suddenly are my tents spoiled, and my curtains *in a moment*”__ (Jeremiah 4:20).
And watch the word do its full work on Babylon herself:

[quote.scripture, Jeremiah 51:8]
____
*Babylon is suddenly fallen* and destroyed: howl for her; take balm for her pain, if so be she may be healed.
____

No warning shot, no visible siege in the sentence — she is simply, suddenly, fallen.
Revelation counts the same fall by the clock rather than the eye, and repeats the number until it cannot be missed: __“for in *one hour* is thy judgment come”__ (Revelation 18:10); __“for in *one hour* so great riches is come to nought”__ (18:17); __“for in *one hour* is she made desolate”__ (18:19).
Three times in one chapter, the mourners themselves keep saying the word — because the thing that stuns them most is not that Babylon fell, but how fast.

The same suddenness that ends the wicked announces the LORD Himself: __“the Lord, whom ye seek, shall *suddenly* come to his temple”__ (Malachi 3:1) — the true King’s arrival keeps the same timing as the false queen’s fall.
Neither is a process you can watch coming.
Both are a wink.

[discrete]
=== No Remedy in the Wink

Scripture is careful to note what the moment does not allow: appeal, delay, or repair.

[quote.scripture, Proverbs 29:1]
____
He, that being often reproved hardeneth his neck, shall *suddenly* be destroyed, and that *without remedy*.
____

The warnings can run for years; the destruction, when it lands, allows none.
Paul names the same collapse waiting behind the world’s most confident sentence:

[quote.scripture, 1 Thessalonians 5:3]
____
For when they shall say, sym:sym-peace[Peace] *and safety*; then *sudden destruction* cometh upon them, as *travail upon a woman with child*; and they shall not escape.
____

The image is deliberately double-edged, and worth holding next to Babylon’s own boast of childlessness (Isaiah 47:8-9): travail is sudden, unstoppable once it starts, and ends in a birth the mother cannot delay — but here it ends the one who is unprepared, __“as a thief in the night”__ (1 Thessalonians 5:2; 2 Peter 3:10).
Jesus warns His own not to be caught the same way — __“lest… that day come upon you *unawares*”__ (Luke 21:34), the Greek _aiphnidios_, the exact cousin of the __“suddenly”__ that fells Babylon.
The moment does not ask whether you are ready.

[discrete]
=== The Moment That Blesses

The shortness of the word is not only a threat, and Scripture will not let the reader forget it.
The same _rega_ that consumes Korah’s company also measures God’s own anger toward His covenant people — and measures it as mercy:

[quote.scripture, Isaiah 54:7-8]
____
For a *small moment* have I forsaken thee; but with great mercies will I *gather thee*.
In a little wrath I hid my face from thee *for a moment*; but with *everlasting kindness* will I have mercy on thee…
____

The wrath is a moment; the kindness is everlasting — the same asymmetry David sings: __“his anger endureth but a *moment*; in his favour is life: weeping may endure for a night, but joy cometh in the morning”__ (Psalm 30:5).
And Paul, under the weight of real suffering, reaches for the identical word to shrink it: __“our light affliction, which is but *for a moment*, worketh for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory”__ (2 Corinthians 4:17).
The moment cuts both ways because it is not itself good or evil; it is simply the unit in which God acts — decisively, without process, whether the act is a judgment or a rescue.

Even the devil's temptation borrows the word, and gives away more than he intends by doing so.
He takes Jesus __“up into an *exceeding high mountain*”__ (Matthew 4:8) and shows Him __“all the kingdoms of the world *in a moment of time*”__ (Luke 4:5) — and by now every term of the offer reads.
The sym:sym-mountain[mountain] is an empire, and Scripture knows one mountain that link:/books/symbolic-language/mountain/[holds all the kingdoms at once]: the destroying mountain that destroys all the earth (Jeremiah 51:25) — sym:sym-babel[Babylon], the composite kingdom.footnote:fn31mountain[The mountain-as-kingdom derivation — Jeremiah 51:25 beside Daniel 2 and Revelation 17 — is made in the link:/books/symbolic-language/mountain/[Mountain] chapter.]
And the offer arrives priced in Babylon's own doom-unit: he shows the kingdoms in a *moment* — the very measure by which they fall.
What the devil could display in a _rega_, God can end in one; the Lord declined the mountain that falls in a moment.

What changes hands in that moment is not what the devil was holding.
The trumpet that raises the dead __“*in a moment*, in the twinkling of an eye, at the *last trump*”__ (1 Corinthians 15:52) is the seventh, at which __“The kingdoms of this world are become the kingdoms of our Lord, and of his Christ”__ (Revelation 11:15) — and the next breath tells how: __“the *nations were angry*, and *thy wrath is come*”__ (Revelation 11:18).
The world's kingdoms are not transferred; they fall — Daniel's sym:sym-stone[stone] grinds them to sym:sym-chaff[chaff] and the sym:sym-wind[wind] carries them off, no place found for them (Daniel 2:35) — and what is given to the Son is another kingdom entirely, the kingdom the God of heaven sets up to break and consume all these (Daniel 2:44): __“there was *given him* dominion, and glory, and a kingdom”__ (Daniel 7:14).
The devil offered the mountains as they stood — __“that is *delivered unto me*,”__ he claimed (Luke 4:6); the Father's grant arrives only over their dust.
In the moment Babylon falls, the nations of the world fall with her — and the kingdom is given to the Son of man.

Take the phrase as a marker wherever you meet it: *in a moment*, *suddenly* — when Scripture hangs those words on a kingdom, it is speaking the judgment of Babylon, or a similitude of it.

[discrete]
=== The Sign That Was Not Sudden

Set against all this, one long-fused word makes its point by contrast.
The vision that seems slow is not therefore false: __“the vision is yet for an *appointed time*, but at the end it shall speak, and not lie: though it tarry, *wait for it*; because it will *surely come*, it will not tarry”__ (Habakkuk 2:3).
God’s patience and God’s suddenness are not opposites; they are the same character seen at two distances.
He can hold back for centuries and then close the account in a single wink, because the tarrying was never uncertainty — it was the appointed time, running its full course before the moment falls.

[discrete]
=== The Queen’s Clock Is Not Her Own

Return, then, to the queen who claimed she would never be a widow.
The moment is not a separate judgment laid on top of Babylon’s fall; it is that judgment’s own timer.
The wife of the covenant is warned, pleaded with, given generation after generation to return — the long, patient _tarrying_ of Habakkuk’s vision — but when the sentence at last falls, it falls the only way Scripture ever lets it fall on the unrepentant: __“in a moment, in one day”__ (Isaiah 47:9), __“in one hour”__ (Revelation 18:10, 17, 19), __“suddenly”__ (Jeremiah 51:8).
The years of warning and the instant of ruin are not in tension; they are the same pattern this book keeps meeting — in the sym:sym-remnant[remnant] of Part Two, in the sym:sym-shadow[shadow] beside it — every symbol that turns out to hinge on one small, decisive word.
God is not slow, and He is not sudden by accident.
He simply does not need more than a wink — and neither, Scripture keeps insisting, will the trumpet that raises the dead.
And if the account can close in a wink, the vocabulary had best be in hand before it does.
