Every definition in this book was derived the same way: Scripture compared with Scripture, the clearer uses defining the harder ones, tested until the meaning held everywhere it appears.
No reading was taken from a commentary, ancient or modern; the working library was deliberately small.
Only after the glossary was finished did the search turn outward — first to what is taught today, which the badges measure, and then backward, through the church fathers, the targums, the rabbis, and the oldest commentaries on record, asking one question: had anyone read these words this way before?

The answer changes the complexion of the book's boldest claims.
Of the forty readings the modern consensus does not teach, eleven turned out to be things the ancient interpreters already held — not influences, for the derivations were finished before the search began, but witnesses: independent readers, most of them fifteen centuries closer to the language, landing where the texts land.
Those eleven carry the ANCIENT badge in the Glossary, each entry naming its witnesses.
This chapter lets them speak.

[discrete]
=== The Lights

The link:/books/symbolic-language/sun-moon-and-stars/[Sun, Moon, and Stars] chapter argued that the moon is the faithful witness — the reflecting, submissive partner of a relation, bright only with another's light.
Augustine says it without hedging: __“the moon is understood to be the Church, because she has no light of her own, but is *lighted by the only-begotten Son of God*, who in many places of holy Scripture is allegorically called the Sun”__ (Exposition on Psalm 11).
Ambrose calls the moon a figure of the Church for the same reason in his Hexaemeron, and Origen had already drawn the pairing in his homilies on Genesis: Christ the sun, the Church the moon, the faithful her stars.
The relational reading — the light borrowed, the witness faithful — was the church's first instinct, not this book's invention.

The oil of the ten virgins belongs beside it.
Today's pulpit almost universally makes that oil the Holy Spirit; the search found *no ancient interpreter who read it so*.
What the ancients said, in a single voice, is what the link:/books/symbolic-language/the-remnant/[parable's own economy] demands: Chrysostom — __“by oil, *humanity, almsgiving, succor* to them that are in need”__ (Homily 78 on Matthew); Hilary — __“the oil is the *fruit of good works*, the vessels are the human bodies in whose inward parts the treasure of a good conscience is to be laid up”__; Jerome — the wise virgins are they who, __“besides their faith, have the *ornament of good works*.”__
Oil as stored works is not a revision of the old teaching.
It is the old teaching.

[discrete]
=== The Coin and the Fish

The link:/books/symbolic-language/the-coin/[Coin] chapter read the coin as a person: stamped with an owner's image, assigned a value, sought when lost.
Tertullian preached the decode in the 3rd century: render __“the image of Caesar, which is on the coin, to Caesar, and the *image of God, which is in man, to God* — money to Caesar, *yourself* to God”__ (On Idolatry 15).
Gregory the Great read the lost drachma the same way — the coin bearing the image is man, made in the image of his Maker and hunted through the swept house — and Gregory of Nyssa found the royal image hidden under the filth, waiting to be uncovered.

The Great Fish of Jonah's sign fared the same in Jewish antiquity.
Where the modern reader sees only a miracle-whale, the Targum of Isaiah 27:1 decodes the sea-monsters as *kings* — __“the king who magnified himself as Pharaoh… and the king who was strong as the *dragon that is in the sea*”__ — and Exodus Rabbah says the rod became a serpent-monster before Pharaoh __“because Pharaoh was likened to a serpent, as it is stated: the great dragon that lies in the midst of his rivers”__ (Ezekiel 29:3).
A great ruler among the sea of peoples, who swallows and is made to give back: the synagogue knew the figure before the church forgot it.

[discrete]
=== The Vessel on the Sea

The link:/books/symbolic-language/the-ship/[Ship] chapter has already called its ancient witness: Hilary of Poitiers, reading the Lord's boat-sermon — __“the ship offers a type of the Church, within which the word of life is placed, and is preached to those without.”__
He does not stand alone.
Hippolytus drew the whole rigging: __“the sea is the world, in which the Church is set, like a ship tossed in the deep but not destroyed, for she has with her the skilled *Pilot*, Christ”__ — the mast her cross, her two rudders the two Testaments.
Tertullian read the storm-tossed boat of Matthew 8 as the church in the world's waves; Augustine called Noah's ark __“a figure of the *city* of God sojourning in this world.”__
A governed vessel, crewed and piloted, riding the sea of the peoples: the ancients held the type and named one vessel; Scripture's own usage — Tyre, Egypt, Babylon's lament — supplies the register this book restored.

Victorinus of Pettau, who wrote the earliest surviving commentary on Revelation, belongs in this company for a different identification: on the great city __“spiritually called Sodom and Egypt, where also our Lord was crucified”__ (Revelation 11:8), he answers plainly — Jerusalem.
The link:/books/symbolic-language/heaven-and-hell/[covenant city in bondage] wearing Egypt's name was the third century's reading before it was this book's.

[discrete]
=== The Week, the Way, and the Truth

That _sabbath_ names the whole seven was the working assumption of antiquity.
The Septuagint translates Leviticus' __“seven sabbaths”__ as *seven weeks* outright; the Talmud argues the identification in so many words (Menachot 65a); the Didache dates fasts to __“the second and the fifth day *of the sabbath*”__ — the week's days numbered inside the unit, exactly as the Gospels count them.
The link:/books/symbolic-language/the-sabbath/[Sabbath] chapter derived the unit from the law's own grammar; the oldest translators and the rabbis had already banked it.

The way and the truth read the same.
The oldest church manual opens: __“There are *two ways*, one of life and one of death”__ — and the way of life that follows is nothing but commanded conduct, the commandments expounded for the walk (Didache 1; so also the Epistle of Barnabas).
The Talmud parses Exodus 18:20's __“the way wherein they must walk”__ clause by clause into covenant deeds (Bava Metzia 30b).
And for the truth, the identification could not be more direct: __“*Truth* — this is Torah”__ (Berakhot 5b), the rabbis citing the same proverb this book stands on, as the psalmist had already written: __“thy law is the truth.”__

=== The West, the Sign, and Righteousness

Scripture's compass faces east, and this book read the west — the _hinder_ sea — as where a course ends.
An ancient midrash makes the same move on the same letters: on Moses shown the land __“unto the hinder sea”__ (Deuteronomy 34:2), the Sifre teaches — __“do not read *the last sea* (ha-yam ha-acharon) but *the last day* (ha-yom ha-acharon): He showed him the whole world from the day it was created until the day the dead live”__ — the westward limit standing for the end of the course of history.
Rashi set that reading in his commentary, where it has stood for nine centuries.

The sign as an enacted portent — a similitude lived in advance, whose fulfillment testifies — is the rabbinic principle _ma'aseh avot siman le-vanim_: the deeds of the fathers are a *sign* to the sons (Midrash Tanchuma, carried by Ramban at Genesis 12:6).
And righteousness as right-living was simply the ancient definition of the word: Chrysostom glosses the hunger for righteousness as the hunger for __“the whole of *virtue*”__ (Homily 15 on Matthew), and the Septuagint renders the righteousness of the kept commandment as mercy done (Deuteronomy 6:25).
The forensic abstraction this book declines to import is younger than every witness in this chapter.

[discrete]
=== What the Search Did Not Find

The record deserves to be read whole, so the misses stand beside the hits.
For the renewed moon at full, the located ancient voices — Philo, the Talmud's reading of the covered throne-day — stand with the very traditions this book contests; the case for the full moon rests on Scripture and the sky, not on the fathers, and the calendar chapters argue it on that evidence alone.
The pearl the ancients gave to Christ and the kingdom, not to the moon; the sapphire they gave to the sky-blue of the throne; the palm of the hand, as a word distinct from the hand itself, none of them isolated at all.
Nobody before the modern era placed Jerusalem under the harlot of Revelation 17 in any source this search reached — though Origen read Ezekiel's harlot as the unfaithful covenant bride, and the equation the chapters build stands on Scripture's own repeated usage.

That is the honest shape of it: the ancients are witnesses here, not judges.
Where they agree, they testify to how much the modern pulpit has let fall; where they differ or fall silent, the derivations stand where they always stood — on the texts, which any reader can check.
Ask for the old paths; but walk them because they are *good*, proven from the mouth of two or three witnesses of Scripture itself.

The pattern of this book has been the same from the first chapter: a vocabulary defined by Scripture, tested against every occurrence, and found already whole — the exam of the parables, the doctrine of the gospel and the law, the calendar of the appointed times, the prophecy of the mountains and the sea, and the studies that filled the vocabulary out.
The words were never new.
They were waiting to be heard again.
