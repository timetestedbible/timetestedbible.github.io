Peninim: Why Pearl Fits Better Than Ruby

Six times the Hebrew Bible uses peninim (H6443). Six times the King James Version prints rubies. That familiar English word feels settled until the Strong’s panel is opened: its own definition is “probably a pearl (as round).” Which object did the writers mean—ruby, coral, pearl, or merely some precious material?

The short answer: the surviving evidence does not permit absolute certainty, but it does make pearl a responsible and probably preferable rendering. Coral is the strongest competing identification. Ruby is a traditional English choice, not the conclusion demanded by the Hebrew or by the ancient translations. The Time Tested Bible therefore proposes pearl in all six passages and leaves the evidence attached to the change.

Begin With Every Occurrence

A rare word cannot be defined by one convenient verse. Its complete biblical corpus is the proper starting place. Five occurrences use peninim as a measure of exceptional worth. The sixth, Lamentations 4:7, places it inside a portrait of white, red, polished, and black appearances.

The five value texts do not distinguish pearl from ruby or coral; all three could be costly. Lamentations supplies the only physical comparison, so any proposed identification must answer it rather than quietly omit it.

What the Lexicons Actually Say

Strong’s H6443 defines the word as “probably a pearl (as round)”, then lists ruby as the KJV rendering. Those are not the same claim. The first attempts to identify the Hebrew object; the second reports what one English translation printed.

Brown–Driver–Briggs prefers corals, apparently connecting the word with branching, but its entry also records pearls as the alternative defended by earlier authorities. Modern translations display the same uncertainty with corals, jewels, pearls, or a general phrase such as precious stones. Lexicography does not present ruby as an uncontested result.

What this proves: “pearl” is not an invented symbolic substitution. It begins as a published lexical identification of the literal object. The symbolic argument comes later and must still be tested from Scripture.

The Ancient Translations Do Not Settle on Ruby

Ancient translators often refused to identify a specific mineral. The Greek Septuagint renders Proverbs 3:15 with precious stones and Proverbs 8:11 with costly stones. The Syriac Peshitta and Aramaic Targum to Proverbs 3:15 similarly read good stones. These witnesses preserve great value but do not identify a ruby.

The Aramaic Targum to Job 28:18 is more specific. It renders the comparison with margalyan, an ordinary Aramaic word for pearls. That does not prove what the original Hebrew author pictured, but it proves that “pearl” is an old reading of this very word in this very verse—not a modern rescue devised for the moon argument.

The witnesses therefore divide in a useful way: some preserve only “valuable stones,” one identifies pearls, and none independently requires the modern gemstone called ruby.

Why “Ruby” Is More Precise Than the Evidence

Modern readers hear ruby and picture red corundum with a definite chemical composition. Historical writers did not use the category so precisely. The Gemological Institute of America notes that ruby and red spinel were not scientifically distinguished until 1783. Famous “rubies” later proved to be spinels. An English Bible printed in 1611 could therefore say ruby without identifying the mineral a modern jeweler means by the word.

That history does not make the KJV dishonest. It means ruby functioned as a broad traditional jewel-name. It cannot be carried backward as though the Hebrew lexeme had already passed a modern mineralogical test.

The Hard Verse: Redder Than Peninim

Her consecrated ones were purer than snow, they were whiter than milk; they were more ruddy in body than peninim, their polished form was like sapphire. Their appearance became blacker than soot. Lamentations 4:7–8, with the disputed noun left untranslated

This is the best argument for coral or a red stone. The bodies are compared with peninim precisely where the poem says ruddy. Any pearl proposal that insists pearls can only be white has failed its hardest test.

But pearls are not only white. Pliny the Elder, writing in the first century, says pearls could redden in the sun; in the same description he names brilliance, roundness, smoothness, size, and weight among their prized qualities. The public-domain Book of the Pearl records naturally rose-tinted and reddish Pinna pearls and notes an older proposal that the reddish pearl of the Red Sea was the biblical peninim. Its shell has a nacreous interior described as white tinged with red.

This evidence does not turn Lamentations into a dictionary saying “pearl.” It does something narrower and necessary: it defeats the claim that the verse’s red comparison makes pearl impossible. Pearls known around the biblical world could be white, rose, red, dark, polished, and round—the very range that makes the passage’s movement from snow and milk, through ruddy peninim and sapphire polish, to blackness intelligible.

Coral remains possible. Its red color gives it a straightforward reading in Lamentations, and BDB’s proposal deserves to be seen. But pearl explains the lexicon’s roundness note, has a specific Aramaic translation behind it, remains fully compatible with the red witness, and preserves a coherent object through every occurrence. On balance, pearl is the better rendering.

How Time Tested Bible Handles the Difference

The base translation remains visible. In the Bible reader, all six H6443 occurrences carry a proposed translation patch from rubies to pearls. Tapping or hovering over the patched word shows the reason, permits the reader to restore the traditional wording, and links back to this complete case. That keeps the translation decision inspectable instead of silently building it into the argument.

The proposed readings are:

  • Job 28:18 — the acquisition of wisdom is above pearls.
  • Proverbs 3:15 — Wisdom is more precious than pearls.

Translation First, Symbol Second

This article asks a lexical question: What object did peninim probably name? The Pearl symbol study deliberately approaches from the other direction. It temporarily sets the lexicons aside, gathers every biblical pearl passage, and asks what properties Scripture itself assigns to the object: great value; singular wholeness; brightness and polish; white, ruddy, and dark appearances; bridal association; Wisdom; trade; adornment; and gates.

Only after those independent paths meet should a larger symbolic claim be considered. The chapter The Pearls of Wisdom in MEAT The Bible’s Symbolic Language follows that pattern into the bride, the twelve pearl-gates, and the moon. The book does not need to reproduce this lexical investigation. It needs only to state the minimum fact—Strong’s says peninim is probably a pearl—and send the reader here to inspect the receipts.
