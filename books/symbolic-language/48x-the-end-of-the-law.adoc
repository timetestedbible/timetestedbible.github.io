Paul wrote 1 sentence that has been made to carry a conclusion he spends whole chapters forbidding:

[quote.scripture, Romans 10:4]
____
For Christ is the *end of the law* for righteousness to every one that believeth.
____

Read _end_ as termination, and Christ is where the law stops.
Read it as purpose, and Christ is where the law was going.
The English word can carry both senses, and so can the Greek beneath it.
The sentence cannot be settled by the word _end_ alone.
Paul must be permitted to define Paul.

[discrete]
=== The End of the Commandment

The Greek word is _telos_: an end, outcome, destination, or goal.
Paul uses it again where no one mistakes it for abolition:

[quote.scripture, 1 Timothy 1:5, 8, NKJV]
____
Now the *purpose of the commandment is* sym:sym-love[love] from a pure heart, from a good conscience, and from sincere faith…
But we know that the *law is good* if one uses it lawfully.
____

Love did not terminate the commandment.
Love is what the commandment was designed to produce — __“love is the fulfilling of the law”__ (Romans 13:10).
The same paragraph warns of men who desire to teach the law while understanding neither what they say nor what they affirm; Paul's answer is not that the law is gone, but that it must be used lawfully (1 Timothy 1:7-8).
His next sentence says __“the law is not made for a righteous man, but for the lawless and disobedient”__ before listing murderers, whoremongers, menstealers, liars, and perjurers (1 Timothy 1:9-10).
The criminal code is laid against the criminal; the righteous man does not need its sentence aimed at him because he is not doing what it condemns.
The verse limits the law's prosecuting office, not its definition of right-living.

_Telos_ can name a resulting destination as well: freed from sin and become servants to God, __“ye have your sym:sym-fruit[fruit] unto holiness, and the *end everlasting* sym:sym-life[life]”__ (Romans 6:22).
Everlasting life does not abolish the fruit that reaches it.
It is the end toward which that fruit runs.

Romans 10 supplies the same direction.
Israel, Paul says, went about __“to establish their *own* sym:sym-righteousness[righteousness]”__ and did not submit to __“the righteousness of God”__ (Romans 10:3).
Christ is then the _telos_ __“for righteousness to every one that believeth”__ — not the end of righteousness, but its embodied aim: God's sym:sym-word[word] believed, obeyed, and lived without defect.
Paul had already closed the abolition reading in the same letter: __“Do we then make void the law through faith? God forbid: yea, we *establish the law*”__ (Romans 3:31).

Christ is not where the law failed and stopped.
He is the sym:sym-word[word] made flesh, the law's right-living purpose made visible, and the pattern into which His people are formed.

[discrete]
=== Paul Does Not Mean One Thing by Law

_Law_ is not a magic word with 1 referent in every sentence.
Paul regularly attaches a name to it, and the name controls the meaning:

.Paul's named laws and commands
[.prevalence-table%unbreakable, frame=topbot, grid=none, cols="3,3,5"]
|===
| *Phrase* | *Reference* | *What governs*
| law of God | Romans 7:22, 25; 8:7 | God's instruction, delighted in and served by the inward man
| law of sin | Romans 7:23, 25 | sym:sym-sin[sin] ruling the flesh
| law of the Spirit of life | Romans 8:2 | the Spirit's sym:sym-life[life]-giving rule in Christ
| law of faith | Romans 3:27 | the principle of sym:sym-faith[faith] that excludes boasting
| law of Christ | Galatians 6:2 | burdens borne in fulfillment of Christ's command
| commandments of God | 1 Corinthians 7:19 | what remains when circumcision and uncircumcision are set aside
| commandments of men | Colossians 2:22; Titus 1:14 | human rules that turn from the sym:sym-truth[truth]
|===

The noun _law_ does not erase its modifier.
Freedom from __“the law of sin and death”__ is not freedom from __“the law of God”__ (Romans 8:2; 7:22).
Nor does a human command become disobedience merely because a man gave it.
Paul commands submission to civil rulers (Romans 13:1-7), children to parents, and servants to masters acting within their proper bounds (Ephesians 6:1-8).
A human command becomes a rival command when it conflicts with, replaces, or nullifies what God commanded; then the order is fixed: __“We ought to obey God rather than men”__ (Acts 5:29).

Paul has clear vocabulary for human rules that rival God's command: __“traditions of my fathers”__ (Galatians 1:14), __“tradition of men”__ (Colossians 2:8), __“commandments and doctrines of men”__ (Colossians 2:22), and __“Jewish fables”__ joined to __“commandments of men”__ (Titus 1:14).
When Paul means God's standard, he is equally plain: __“the law is holy, and the commandment holy, and just, and good”__ (Romans 7:12), __“the law is spiritual”__ (Romans 7:14), and __“I delight in the law of God after the inward man”__ (Romans 7:22).

Any reading that pours all those laws into 1 bucket will make Paul contradict himself before the page turns.

[discrete]
=== Work Is Not Obedience

A sym:sym-works[work] is a deed.
_Obedience_ names the deed's relation to a legitimate command.
The 2 can meet in 1 act without becoming synonyms.

A son who carries his father's message has performed a work and obeyed his father.
The same son may perform the same outward work for his employer without disobeying God; the authorities do not conflict.
Disobedience begins when a command or deed crosses God's command — when a tradition is kept by rejecting what God said (Mark 7:9).

Paul's famous contrast in Romans 4 is not faith against obedience.
He defines the work by its economic claim:

[quote.scripture, Romans 4:4-5]
____
Now to him that *worketh* is the reward not reckoned of grace, but of *debt*.
But to him that worketh not, but sym:sym-belief[believeth] on him that justifieth the ungodly, his sym:sym-faith[faith] is counted for sym:sym-righteousness[righteousness].
____

The picture is a laborer presenting work for wages, making the payer a debtor.
Jesus rules out that claim even after complete obedience: __“when ye shall have done *all those things which are commanded you*, say, We are unprofitable servants: we have done that which was our *duty* to do”__ (Luke 17:10).
Perfect obedience would still create no surplus with which to purchase forgiveness.
The servant has done his duty; the Master owes no wage.

Abraham's faith was not a substitute for obedience.
__“By faith Abraham, when he was called… *obeyed*”__ (Hebrews 11:8), and James says his faith __“wrought with his works”__ and __“by works was faith made perfect”__ (James 2:22).
His work made faith visible; it did not turn duty into wages.

This is why Paul can write __“not of works”__ and, in the next breath, say we are __“created in Christ Jesus *unto good works*, which God hath before ordained that we should *walk in them*”__ (Ephesians 2:9-10).
Works are excluded as the source and purchase price of the gift, then restored as the life the gift creates.

Paul's other __“not of works”__ sentences keep the same boundary.
Election cannot be earned by children __“not yet born, neither having done any good or evil”__ (Romans 9:11); calling cannot be earned when its purpose and grace were given __“before the world began”__ (2 Timothy 1:9); salvation is __“not by works of righteousness which we have done”__ in the letter that commands believers to __“maintain good works”__ (Titus 3:5, 8).
The excluded works either do not exist yet or cannot purchase the gift; the commanded works follow the gift and show what it produced.

[discrete]
=== Every Work of the Law in Its Place

The exact Greek phrase _erga nomou_, sym:sym-works-of-law[“works of the law,”] does not carry 1 hidden code through every occurrence.
Sometimes the context explicitly identifies God's law.
Sometimes the controversy is circumcision and enforced separation.
Sometimes the disputed deed is not named.

Romans 9:32 carries a textual wrinkle worth keeping visible.
The Received Text behind the KJV reads __“works of the law.”__
The critical Greek text reads simply __“as if by works,”__ without __“of the law,”__ placing still more weight on the attempted manner rather than on a different code.

The Galatian controversy cannot be reduced to __“God's commandments are bad works.”__
Circumcision was a God-given sign, but the circumcision party turned it into a universal entry requirement and ground of standing.
Paul separates the boundary mark from obedience in 1 sentence: __“Circumcision is nothing, and uncircumcision is nothing, but the *keeping of the commandments of God*”__ (1 Corinthians 7:19).
Galatians 5 makes the disputed use explicit: the man receiving circumcision is seeking to be __“justified by the law”__ (Galatians 5:2-4).
Circumcision itself cannot be the evil, for Paul circumcised Timothy in the next chapter of Acts (Acts 16:3).
The error is demanding the sign as a purchase price and covenant gate God did not set for the nations.

Nor can every occurrence be renamed a commandment of men.
Romans 3 and Galatians 3:10 explicitly identify God's law.
The uniform error lies not in the identity of every deed but in what men demand from it: justification, the Spirit, inheritance, or a boast as though God owed payment.
The law identifies sym:sym-sin[sin] and defines sym:sym-righteousness[right-living]; it cannot ransom the man who already broke it, and obedience after the breach cannot turn duty into overpayment.

[discrete]
=== Not Under, Dead To, and Delivered From

__“Ye are not under the law, but under grace”__ is usually quoted with the next sentence missing.
Paul supplies the forbidden inference himself:

[quote.scripture, Romans 6:14-16]
____
For sin shall not have dominion over you: for ye are not *under the law*, but under sym:sym-grace[grace].
What then? shall we *sin*, because we are not under the law, but under grace? *God forbid.*
Know ye not, that to whom ye yield yourselves servants to *obey*, his servants ye are to whom ye obey; whether of sin unto death, or of *obedience unto righteousness*?
____

Whatever __“not under the law”__ means, Paul expressly says it cannot mean permission to sin.
The paragraph is about dominion and masters: sin no longer rules; grace receives the servant; obedience runs unto righteousness.
Grace does not leave the man masterless.

Romans 7 uses sym:sym-marriage[marriage] to make the same point.
Paul says _the believer_ becomes __“dead to the law by the body of Christ”__ so that he may be __“married to another… that we should bring forth sym:sym-fruit[fruit] unto God”__ (Romans 7:4).
The man dies and changes covenant standing; Paul does not say the law died.
His next question closes that escape: __“Is the law sin? God forbid”__ — then he quotes the tenth commandment, __“Thou shalt not covet”__ (Romans 7:7).
The sequence ends with __“the law is holy,”__ __“the law is spiritual,”__ and __“I delight in the law of God”__ (Romans 7:12, 14, 22).

Romans 8 names the weakness accurately: the law was weak __“through the flesh,”__ so God condemned sin in the flesh __“that the *righteousness of the law might be fulfilled in us*, who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit”__ (Romans 8:3-4).
The Spirit cures the failed walker; He does not erase the right walk.

Galatians says likewise, __“if ye be led of the Spirit, ye are not under the law”__ (Galatians 5:18), inside a passage that names adultery, idolatry, hatred, murder, and drunkenness as works of the flesh that exclude from the kingdom (Galatians 5:19-21).
The chapter has just said __“all the law is fulfilled in 1 word… Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself”__ (Galatians 5:14), and it closes the Spirit's fruit with __“against such there is no law”__ (Galatians 5:23).
Led by the Spirit does not mean led into what the law condemns.

[discrete]
=== The Curse, the Schoolmaster, and the Letter

Christ redeemed us __“from the *curse* of the law”__ (Galatians 3:13).
The curse is not the law itself; it is the sentence the law pronounces on the transgressor.
Paul quotes the sentence immediately: __“Cursed is every one that *continueth not* in all things which are written in the book of the law *to do them*”__ (Galatians 3:10).
The failure is not obedience but its absence.
Canceling a prisoner's sentence does not repeal the statute he broke.

The law cannot give sym:sym-life[life] to the already dead: __“if there had been a law given which could have given life, verily righteousness should have been by the law”__ (Galatians 3:21).
Instruction can identify the right sym:sym-way[road] and convict the man who left it; it cannot raise him from death or pay his debt.
Life remains gift and promise.

Paul then calls the law a _paidagōgos_, the household custodian who guarded a child until maturity: __“the law was our schoolmaster unto Christ… but after that faith is come, we are no longer under a schoolmaster”__ (Galatians 3:24-25).
A son who comes of age is no longer under the custodian's hand; he does not therefore become free to steal from his father, dishonor his mother, or worship another god.
The guardian's administration reaches its _telos_ when the Father's instruction is received inwardly and lived by faith.

__“The letter killeth, but the spirit giveth life”__ is read with the same flattening.
Second Corinthians 3 does not call God's command evil; it contrasts 2 ministrations — __“the ministration of condemnation”__ and __“the ministration of righteousness”__ (2 Corinthians 3:9).
The chapter opens with the Spirit writing, __“not in tables of stone, but in fleshy tables of the heart”__ (2 Corinthians 3:3).
The new covenant does not celebrate an empty heart.
It moves the writing there.

Paul had already asked whether the good commandment was made death to him and answered __“God forbid.”__
Sin worked death in him __“by that which is good,”__ so that sin might appear exceeding sinful (Romans 7:13).
The letter names and sentences the breach; the Spirit forgives, writes, and empowers the right-living the letter could command but never create.

Hebrews uses the same distinctions in its priesthood argument.
__“A change also of the law”__ is required because __“the priesthood”__ has changed (Hebrews 7:12); the commandment disannulled is the preceding commandment governing mortal Levitical priests, replaced by the power of an endless life (Hebrews 7:16-18).
The meats, drinks, washings, and carnal ordinances imposed until reformation belong to the tabernacle service the chapter is describing (Hebrews 9:1-10).
The new covenant does not leave the heart blank: __“I will put my laws into their mind, and write them in their hearts”__ (Hebrews 8:10).
A changed priesthood and an inward writing cannot honestly be summarized as the end of every law.

[discrete]
=== Ordinances, Days, and Paul's Verdict

Ephesians says Christ abolished __“the enmity, even the law of commandments contained in ordinances”__ (Ephesians 2:15).
The paragraph names its subject before the disputed phrase: circumcision and uncircumcision, aliens and citizens, the __“middle wall of partition,”__ and the making of both peoples into __“1 new man”__ (Ephesians 2:11-19).
Whatever the ordinances include, the thing Christ kills is the enmity that keeps the nations apart.
The same letter later quotes the fifth commandment — __“Honour thy father and mother”__ — and calls it __“the first commandment with promise”__ (Ephesians 6:2).
Paul cannot mean that every commandment was abolished in chapter 2 and binding in chapter 6.

Colossians does not say the Torah was nailed to the cross.
It says God forgave trespasses by blotting out a singular _cheirographon_, a handwritten debt-record __“that was against us”__ (Colossians 2:13-14).
The hostile document matches the debt canceled; _nomos_, law, never appears in the sentence.
When the chapter later identifies the ordinances to which the Colossians must not submit, it quotes them — __“Touch not; taste not; handle not”__ — and names their source: __“the commandments and doctrines of *men*”__ (Colossians 2:20-22).

The command concerning feast, new moon, and sym:sym-sabbath[sabbath] is not _stop keeping them_ but __“let no man therefore *judge you*”__ (Colossians 2:16).
Paul calls them things which __“are a sym:sym-shadow[shadow] of things to come”__ — present shadow, coming referent — and a shadow is not an enemy of its body but the outline cast by it (Colossians 2:17).
The verse may govern how the days are judged; it does not announce their abolition.

Galatians 4 is equally specific about the road being retraced.
Its readers formerly __“did service unto them which by nature are no gods,”__ and Paul asks why they turn __“again to the weak and beggarly elements”__ before naming __“days, and months, and times, and years”__ (Galatians 4:8-10).
Gentiles cannot return to a Mosaic calendar they never kept.
At minimum, the verse cannot bear the claim that every appointed time of God has ended while its own word __“again”__ points backward to pagan bondage.

Romans 14 names neither sabbath nor unclean animals.
Its dispute is between one who eats all things and a weak brother who __“eateth herbs,”__ and it later adds wine (Romans 14:2, 21).
The word translated __“unclean”__ in verse 14 is _koinos_, common, not _akathartos_, the law's word for an unclean animal.
The chapter governs doubtful disputes, private esteem, and care not to destroy a brother over food; an unnamed day and a vegetarian's conscience cannot repeal a named command.

Acts 15 likewise begins with the stated claim __“Except ye be circumcised… ye cannot be saved”__ (Acts 15:1).
The council refuses circumcision as the purchase price of covenant entry, gives the nations 4 necessary commands drawn from the law, and then observes that Moses __“is read in the synagogues every sabbath day”__ (Acts 15:20-21).
Paul afterward circumcises Timothy for the Jews, hastens to be at Jerusalem for Pentecost, and publicly demonstrates that the report he teaches Jews to forsake Moses is false: __“thou thyself also walkest orderly, and keepest the law”__ (Acts 16:3; 20:16; 21:21-24).
Those acts do not settle every command's application to every covenant member, but they forbid turning Paul into a witness that God's law was evil or dead.

Paul's own verdict remains the safest reading rule:

[quote.scripture, Romans 3:31]
____
Do we then make void the law through faith? *God forbid*: yea, we *establish the law*.
____

The end of the law is sym:sym-love[love] from a pure heart, sym:sym-righteousness[right-living] formed in those who walk after the Spirit, and Christ Himself — the Word believed, the command obeyed, the Father's character made visible.
He is not the law's funeral.
He is its purpose.
