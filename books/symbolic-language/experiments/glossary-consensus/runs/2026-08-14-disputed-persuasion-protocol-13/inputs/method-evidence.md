Shortly after feeding the five thousand, Jesus gave a hard teaching in the synagogue at Capernaum (John 6:59): “Except ye eat the flesh of the Son of man, and drink his blood, ye have no life in you… He that eateth my flesh, and drinketh my blood, dwelleth in me, and I in him” (John 6:53, 56).
After He said this, many of His disciples murmured — “This is an hard saying; who can hear it?” — and “from that time many of his disciples went back, and walked no more with him” (John 6:60, 66).
It was so bad that Jesus turned to the twelve and asked whether they would also go away.

It was a hard saying because those who heard it did not know the symbolic language of the Scriptures, and took it over-literally, saying: “How can this man give us his flesh to eat?” (John 6:52).
Which prompts the question: why would Jesus speak in a manner that drives people away?
Why not speak plainly?

He did it to fulfill the Scriptures, which prophesied that He would speak in parables.
When he spoke publicly he always used parables — “without a parable spake he not unto them” (Matthew 13:34) — and only with his closest disciples did he explain their meaning.
Even so, when he gave the parables of the kingdom his own disciples did not understand, and Jesus rebuked them for it. The rebuke was fair because the keys were already in the Old Testament: the “dark sayings of old” that the psalm said he would utter (Mark 4:13; Psalm 78:2-3; Matthew 13:35). That exam waits a chapter ahead: The Parables of the Kingdom. [Footnote: The Parables of the Kingdom chapter derives each parable of Matthew 13 from the Old Testament alone.]

In other words, Scripture prophesied that the message would be encrypted in parables and decoded with the Old Testament.
If you think you can read the New Testament without an intimate understanding of the Old, chances are you will walk away like the masses — either by interpreting it in a way you reject, or by misinterpreting it in a way you can accept, and facing the same consequences either way.

The first hearers of the apostles knew better.
When Paul preached in Berea, Luke recorded how the hearers received him — and graded them for it:

These were more noble than those in Thessalonica, in that they received the word with all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so.

They tested Paul against the Old Testament, and Scripture calls that nobility.
How many today search the Old Testament to see whether their understanding of Paul is true?
The order has quietly reversed: Paul, as we understand him, has become the judge of the very Scriptures he was judged by.
And the test is not for Paul only — your understanding of any verse must be tested against every other verse, for “at the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall the matter be established” (Deuteronomy 19:15): one verse cannot make a doctrine.

The Bible provides two symbolic words, meat and milk, to describe its teachings, and if you do not do the work to churn the milk into meat, you will remain immature at best — and at worst turn away entirely, like the crowds in Jesus’ day.

The purpose of this book is to teach you how I learned to chew the word of God and test everything.
It is a repeatable, reproducible process that has yielded more pearls of wisdom — and more appreciation for the supernatural structure of the word of God — than I could possibly fit in one book.
We will go through the process systematically and apply it to dozens of symbolic words, starting with meat and milk.
But first we must prepare the ground by explaining the difference between a word and a symbol.

At the end of the seven parables of the kingdom, Jesus gave His hearers an exam of one question, and they passed it: “Yea, Lord.”
It had not started that way.
At the first parable — the key to all of them — His own disciples stumbled, and He rebuked them: “Know ye not this parable? and how then will ye know all parables?” (Mark 4:13).

Stop and weigh that rebuke.
A teacher may not be astonished that students fail an exam on material they were never given; His astonishment is only just if the answers were already on the table.
They were, and He said where.

The psalm He fulfills by teaching in parables locates the material in the past, not the future: “I will open my mouth in a parable: I will utter dark sayings of old: which we have heard and known, and our fathers have told us” (Psalm 78:2-3; Matthew 13:35).
And the Scriptures the disciples grew up on do not merely contain parables — they decode them in front of the reader and expect him to grade himself.

Nathan told David a story about a ewe lamb; David judged its villain to death without seeing his own face — “the man that hath done this thing shall surely die” (2 Samuel 12:5) — and Nathan handed him the key in four words: “Thou art the man” (2 Samuel 12:7).
Isaiah sang a parable of a vineyard, then handed over the key: “the vineyard of the LORD of hosts is the house of Israel” (Isaiah 5:7).
Ezekiel told a riddle of two eagles and a vine, and God's next word is the very question Jesus would ask on the boat: “Know ye not what these things mean?” — followed by the answer, “the king of Babylon is come to Jerusalem” (Ezekiel 17:2, 12).

The exam is closed-book in one direction only: everything needed sits in the Law, the Prophets, and the Psalms.
Take each parable, then, the way a disciple on the shore had to take it — derive the meaning from the Scriptures he already had, write it down, and only then let the Teacher grade the work.
That is how this chapter will read them, and it will keep score.
More broadly, it will establish the principle of how to decode all Scripture using the full body of the word.

Before the first parable, fix the procedure, because it is this book's whole method in miniature.
Take the parable's own words — sower, seed, ground, thorns, fowls — and search the Scriptures for the same words and phrases, everywhere they fall.
Where Scripture has already defined a term out loud — Isaiah's own key, above: the vineyard is the house of Israel — that definition is not local color for one passage; it holds, and it holds uniformly, or parables could not be decoded at all.

Next, weigh what you gathered before you lean on it.
One verse makes a candidate, not a key: a symbol is established when the pattern spans Moses and the prophets and the psalms — independent witnesses carrying the same figure, with no counter-text standing against them.
That standard is what makes the rebuke in the boat just, and it measures its weight: Jesus did not upbraid the twelve for missing an obscurity; seed and ground and thorns and fowls were established patterns, laid down across the whole of the Scriptures read to them every sabbath since childhood.
The better attested the pattern, the more accountable the hearer.

The difference between their seat and yours matters.
The disciple on the shore had his memory and the weekly reading; you have the whole text searchable in moments — a concordance, a search bar, and tools that can gather every use of a word while you frame your next question.
Recall was never the point of the exam, and now it is not even the obstacle: the test is not whether you remember the matches, but whether you go look for them.

“Behold, a sower went forth to sow” (Matthew 13:3).
Close the Gospels; what could a hearer of Moses and the prophets make of that?
Start with the seed, which Isaiah had defined in one breath with the rain:

For as the rain cometh down… that it may give seed to the sower, and bread to the eater: so shall my word be that goeth forth out of my mouth…

The word is the seed to the sower and the bread to the eater — one verse, both figures.
And Moses had sung the same rain long before: “My doctrine shall drop as the rain, my speech shall distil as the dew” (Deuteronomy 32:2) — what falls from heaven to make the ground bear is teaching.

Next the ground.
Jeremiah had already preached this parable as a command: “Break up your fallow ground, and sow not among thorns” — and his very next sentence names the soil: “take away the foreskins of your heart” (Jeremiah 4:3-4) — plowing and circumcising, one operation in two figures: remove the blocking flesh so the seed can enter.
Hosea gives the same order in the same words: “break up your fallow ground: for it is time to seek the LORD” (Hosea 10:12).
The ground is the heart, and the thorns were choking Judah's heart seven centuries before the sermon by the sea — and the thorns have a pedigree older still, for they are the curse-ground's own crop: “thorns also and thistles shall it (the ground) bring forth to thee” (Genesis 3:18), what the cursed earth grows where food should stand.

Now derive what the thorns mean, for the Old Testament assigns that too.
Read the sentence they grow in: the same curse that gives the ground its thorns sentences the man to anxious toil for his bread — “in sorrow shalt thou eat of it… in the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread” (Genesis 3:17, 19) — the thorns spring up in the field of the struggle for a living.
And that pair of weeds — thorn and thistle together — returns exactly once more in the Hebrew Scriptures, and Hosea builds the whole indictment around it:

Israel is an empty vine, he bringeth forth fruit unto himself: according to the multitude of his fruit he hath increased the altars; according to the goodness of his land they have made goodly images.
Their heart is divided; now shall they be found faulty…

The high places also of Aven, the sin of Israel, shall be destroyed: the thorn and the thistle shall come up on their altars; and they shall say to the mountains, Cover us; and to the hills, Fall on us.

Increase, a divided heart, and the curse-ground's own crop sprouting on the altars: riches choking the heart is Hosea's picture before it is the parable's.

Isaiah's vineyard tells the same story, and he graded it himself.
The vineyard planted with the choicest vine gave wild grapes instead, and the sentence fell:

And I will lay it waste: it shall not be pruned, nor digged; but there shall come up briers and thorns: I will also command the clouds that they rain no rain upon it.

Isaiah then hands over the key:

For the vineyard of the LORD of hosts is the house of Israel, and the men of Judah his pleasant plant: and he looked for judgment, but behold oppression; for righteousness, but behold a cry.

The vineyard is Israel, and its briers and thorns are men — the woes that follow name them, person by person:

Woe unto them that join house to house, that lay field to field, till there be no place, that they may be placed alone in the midst of the earth!

Here is the monopoly-builder — buying house after house and field after field until no one else has a place, alone in the middle of everything he has swallowed.

Woe unto them that rise up early in the morning, that they may follow strong drink; that continue until night, till wine inflame them!
And the harp, and the viol, the tabret, and pipe, and wine, are in their feasts: but they regard not the work of the LORD, neither consider the operation of his hands.

Here is the pleasure-lover — feasting from morning drink to evening music, past all notice of God.
Riches and pleasures came up where fruit should have stood: Isaiah graded the thorns seven centuries before the sermon by the sea.

Even the stony ground was on record, for the heart God promises to replace is “the stony heart” (Ezekiel 36:26).
And why is the exchange a heart of flesh for a heart of stone?
Because flesh is ground: adam was formed “of the dust of the ground,” adamah (Genesis 2:7) — the man is named for the soil he is — and he was sent forth “to till the ground from whence he was taken” (Genesis 3:23), so the Sower's field was staked out in Eden.
No one plows rock — “will one plow there with oxen?” (Amos 6:12) — and the very oracle that promises the heart of flesh promises its plowing: “I am for you… and ye shall be tilled and sown” (Ezekiel 36:9).
The seed that found “no deepness of earth” on stone takes root in flesh: the law once carved on “tables of stone” written now “in fleshy tables of the heart” (2 Corinthians 3:3) — “ye are God's husbandry” (1 Corinthians 3:9).

What does ground bring forth when the seed takes?
Grass — “all flesh is grass” (Isaiah 40:6) — a symbol with a chapter of its own.

What of the fowls that devour the wayside seed?
The first time birds descend on a covenant in Scripture, they come as devourers: “when the fowls came down upon the carcases, Abram drove them away” (Genesis 15:11) — uninvited, snatching at the covenant sacrifice, driven off by the covenant man.
In Egypt, Joseph heard a dream of birds eating baked bread out of a basket and graded it without hesitation as a sentence of death (Genesis 40:17-19).
Birds that snatch what was given out of its place are the enemy's agents; a boy raised on Genesis could have written that down.

Four for four — Isaiah's answer, Jeremiah's, Abram's, Hosea's — and not one of the four needed a word Jesus had not yet spoken.
His astonishment in Mark 4:13 was just.

Yet let me be fair, for I would have fared no better.
Handed the parable with no Bible in reach, I would have failed it as completely as any man on the shore.
With a Bible and a printed concordance — and the assurance that the answer was in there somewhere — the derivation above might have cost me days, or months.
It is only with modern tools that I can derive the interpretation independently at all, without leaning on any man's commentary.

Daniel was told to seal the words “even to the time of the end,” when “many shall run to and fro, and knowledge shall be increased” (Daniel 12:4).
The running to and fro is through the Scriptures themselves — and the knowledge increased is this.
