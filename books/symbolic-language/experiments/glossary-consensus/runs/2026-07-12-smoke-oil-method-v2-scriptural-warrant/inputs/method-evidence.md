# The book's method and its internal validation

This protocol evidence is supplied unchanged in every persuasion-stage review.
It explains the kind of inference the book claims to make; it does not establish
that any particular glossary conclusion is correct.

## The stated method

Source: `books/symbolic-language/01-introduction.adoc`, opening argument, and
`books/symbolic-language/02-the-parables-of-the-kingdom.adoc`, opening argument

Matthew first establishes that parable was not occasional decoration in Jesus's
public teaching: “without a parable spake he not unto them” (Matthew 13:34).
Matthew says this fulfilled the prophet: “I will open my mouth in parables”
(Matthew 13:35). The prophecy he invokes identifies the material as “dark
sayings of old: which we have heard and known, and our fathers have told us”
(Psalm 78:2-3). The source of the parables' vocabulary is therefore prior
Scripture: the Law, the Prophets, and the Psalms already heard by Jesus's
disciples. His question after the Sower — “Know ye not this parable? and how
then will ye know all parables?” (Mark 4:13) — holds them accountable for keys
already available to them.

This means there is no definition-free “direct reading” of a parable. The
parable supplies seed, ground, fowls, thorns, lamps, and oil, but a reader must
still decide what those things signify. If their meanings are not derived from
the prior Scriptures, they are being supplied invisibly by intuition,
tradition, or a modern theological vocabulary. The controlled question is not
whether definitions will be imported, but whether Scripture's own established
definitions or the reader's assumptions will supply them.

Source: `books/symbolic-language/02-the-parables-of-the-kingdom.adoc`,
“How the Exam Is Taken”

Before the first parable, fix the procedure, because it is this book's whole
method in miniature. Take the parable's own words — sower, seed, ground, thorns,
fowls — and search the Scriptures for the same words and phrases, everywhere
they fall. Where Scripture has already defined a term out loud — Isaiah's own
key, above: the vineyard *is* the house of Israel — that definition is not local
color for one passage; it holds, and it holds uniformly, or parables could not
be decoded at all.

Next, weigh what you gathered before you lean on it. One verse makes a
candidate, not a key: a symbol is established when the pattern spans Moses and
the prophets and the psalms — independent witnesses carrying the same figure,
with no counter-text standing against them.

Source: `books/symbolic-language/01-introduction.adoc`, “What Is a Symbolic
Word?” and “Supernatural Design of Scripture”

The symbol is the idea; the word is merely its label. Because the meaning lives
in the relationships between passages rather than in the label, it survives
translation. Meaning is not carried by words; it is carried by the relationships
among words. The meaning of a symbol is established by every place the word is
used and every word it stands beside, line upon line, across the whole text.

## A known-answer validation inside the book

Source: `books/symbolic-language/02-the-parables-of-the-kingdom.adoc`, “The
Sower, Derived and Graded”

The chapter first closes the Gospels and derives the Sower's figures from the
Law and the Prophets. It then compares those derivations with the explanation
Jesus supplied:

| Element | Derived from the Law and the Prophets | The Teacher's key |
|---|---|---|
| Seed | The word — rain-borne seed, rain-borne doctrine (Isaiah 55:10-11; Deuteronomy 32:2) | “The seed is the word of God” (Luke 8:11) |
| Ground | The heart — plowed, circumcised, tilled, and sown (Jeremiah 4:3-4; Hosea 10:12; Ezekiel 36:9, 26; Genesis 2:7) | The word “sown in his heart” (Matthew 13:19) |
| Fowls | The devourers on the covenant and on the bread (Genesis 15:11; 40:17-19) | “Then cometh the wicked one, and catcheth away that which was sown” (Matthew 13:19) |
| Thorns | The curse-crop; riches dividing the heart (Genesis 3:17-19; Hosea 10:1-8; Isaiah 5:6-12) | “The care of this world, and the deceitfulness of riches, choke the word” (Matthew 13:22) |

The chapter's conclusion is: “Four for four.” It later applies the same test to
the tares and the net, then states why the score matters: “not one answer hung
on a single verse.” Each stood on a pattern laid across Moses and the prophets,
witness upon witness.

## How this evidence should affect a review

The validation does not make later derivations automatically true. It shows
that the book's method can recover meanings later confirmed by an explicit
answer key. A later conclusion should therefore be tested on the method's own
falsifiable criteria:

1. Does the proposed relationship recur across independent passages?
2. Does it remain consistent across the relevant uses?
3. Does a counter-text break the pattern?
4. Does the gathered pattern distinguish the proposed meaning from its strongest alternative?
5. Is the competing interpretation itself derived from prior Scripture, or is it being privileged as an undefined default?

An explicit sentence of the form “X means Y” is decisive when Scripture gives
one, but its absence is not by itself a refutation. The question is whether the
cross-text pattern actually carries the proposed identification.
