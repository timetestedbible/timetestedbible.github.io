A woman who had bled for twelve years pressed through a crowd to touch the edge of Jesus’ robe, and __“immediately her flow of blood stopped”__ (Luke 8:44, NKJV).
The Gospels are precise about what she touched — not the cloak, but its __“border”__ (Luke 8:44), its __“hem”__ (Matthew 9:20).
She was reaching for a particular part of the garment, and to see why, you must follow one Hebrew word that English has scattered into more than a dozen.

The word is _kanaph_, and the scatter is worth counting.
It falls one hundred and eight times in the Hebrew Scriptures, and the King James renders it _wing_, _skirt_, _border_, _corner_, _quarter_, _end_, _uttermost part_ — once even __“overspreading”__ (Daniel 9:27).
It is the eagle’s _wing_ on which God __“bare you… unto myself”__ (Exodus 19:4), the _skirt_ Boaz spread over Ruth, the _border_ of a garment, the four _corners_ of the earth.
One word: the outermost edge of a thing, where it meets the world.

Because the English keeps changing, the reader who works only in English can miss the thread.
Learn this word, and you gain a signal: wherever a verse says _skirt_, _border_, _corner_, or _the ends of the earth_, lift the English and look underneath.
If _kanaph_ is there, the commandments may be near — for reasons the next section makes plain.

[discrete]
=== The Fringe on the Wing

God commanded something bound to that edge:

[quote.scripture, Numbers 15:38-39]
____
…bid them that they make them *fringes in the borders of their* sym:sym-garment[garments]… that ye may look upon it, and *remember all the commandments of the LORD, and do them*; and that ye seek not after your own heart and your own eyes…
____

Those fringes — the _tzitzit_ — hang at the _kanaph_: the reminder of the law is tied to every corner of the covenant garment.
Their purpose is stated flatly — to _remember the commandments and do them_, and not to follow __“your own eyes”__ (there is the man who is right in his own eyes — we will meet him properly in the sym:sym-fool[Fool and the Wise] chapter ahead).

Notice how well the command itself hides in English: here the kanaph is __“borders,”__ and when the command is repeated — __“thou shalt make thee fringes upon the four *quarters* of thy vesture”__ (Deuteronomy 22:12) — it is __“quarters.”__
Neither verse says _wing_; so the English reader never connects the commandment-fringe to the wings of refuge in the Psalms, though they are one word and one thing.
That is the signal to carry through Scripture: the kanaph is where the *reminder* of the commandments is bound — the fringe is a memory-device, not the law itself — so the kanaph in a verse is a flag that the verse may be speaking of remembering — or forgetting — the commands.

Be precise about what __“remember”__ asks, for in Scripture remembering is not recalling; it is *doing*.
The Torah defines forgetting as disobedience outright — __“Beware that thou *forget* not the LORD thy God, *in not keeping* his commandments”__ (Deuteronomy 8:11): to forget *is* to not keep — and the psalm gives the other side in a single phrase: __“those that remember his commandments *to do them*”__ (Psalm 103:18).
Even God's remembering moves His hand: __“God *remembered* Noah… and God *made a* sym:sym-wind[wind] *to pass over* the earth”__ (Genesis 8:1); __“God heard their groaning, and God *remembered his covenant*”__ (Exodus 2:24) — and the Exodus follows.
So the fringe on the wing is not an aid to reflection; it is a trigger for obedience — __“not a forgetful hearer, but a *doer* of the work”__ (James 1:25).

Strip the vowel points, and the word gives up one more secret.
The points — and Strong’s numbering after them — split the consonants into two entries: a noun for the wing, and a verb that acts only once in all of Scripture.
Unpointed they are one word, and the lone verse where it moves as a verb shows what a kanaph _does_:

[quote.scripture, Isaiah 30:20-21]
____
And though the Lord give you the sym:sym-bread[bread] of adversity, and the water of affliction, yet shall not thy *teachers* be *removed into a corner* any more, but thine eyes shall *see thy teachers*:
And thine ears shall hear a word behind thee, saying, This is the sym:sym-way[way], walk ye in it…
____

To be _kanaph_-ed is to be tucked into the corner — and what Israel had tucked away there was its teachers of the law.
Read it beside the fringe-command and the two verses answer each other: the tzitzit were tied at the corner __“that ye may *look upon it*, and remember,”__ and restoration is promised in the same grammar — eyes that __“*see* thy teachers”__ again, and ears that hear __“this is the way.”__
The corner is where the teaching waits: hidden in judgment, seen in mercy — which is exactly where God tied it.

The counterfeit obliges by aiming at exactly this spot: the Pharisees __“make broad their phylacteries, and *enlarge the borders of their* sym:sym-garment[garments]”__ (Matthew 23:5) — bigger fringes, __“to be seen of men,”__ and the weightier matters of the law undone.
A tassel grown large to advertise an obedience that is not there.

=== Healing in His Wings

Now the woman’s reach makes sense.
She did not grab at random cloth; she took hold of the _tzitzit_ — the corner where the law hangs.
The word the Gospels use for what she touched, the __“hem”__ or __“border”__ of His garment, is _kraspedon_ — the very word the Greek Old Testament uses for the _tzitzit_ of Numbers 15.
She laid hold of His fringes, and the prophet had foretold exactly where healing would be:

[quote.scripture, Malachi 4:2]
____
But unto you that sym:sym-fear[fear] my sym:sym-name[name] shall the sym:sym-sun[Sun] of sym:sym-righteousness[righteousness] arise with *healing in his wings*…
____

Healing _in his wings_ — in the _kanaph_, the corner where the tzitzit hang.
The healing is not loose in the air; it is at the fringe, where the commandments are.
She reached for the law made flesh and laid hold of its corner — __“If I may but touch his sym:sym-garment[garment], I shall be whole”__ (Matthew 9:21) — and she was; and she was not alone: __“as many as touched were made *perfectly whole*”__ (Matthew 14:36).

[discrete]
=== The Covenant Under the Wings

If the wing carries the tassels that remind the wearer of the commandments, the sanctuary should show that reminder — and it does, in furniture.
When Solomon brought the ark home, Scripture places it with this phrase:

[quote.scripture, 1 Kings 8:6]
____
And the priests brought in the *ark of the covenant* of the LORD unto his place, into the oracle of the house, to the most holy place, even *under the wings of the cherubims*.
____

The covenant dwells _under the wings_ — Scripture’s own words.
And the pattern was set at Sinai: the cherubim stretch their wings __“covering the mercy seat”__ (Exodus 25:20), the testimony — the tablets of the law — goes _inside_ the ark beneath (Exodus 25:21), and God tells Moses where He will speak from:

[quote.scripture, Exodus 25:22]
____
And there I will meet with thee, and I will commune with thee from above the mercy seat, *from between the two cherubims* which are upon the ark of the testimony, of all things which I will give thee *in commandment* unto the children of Israel.
____

The commanding voice speaks from between the wings, above the law: the whole architecture of the most holy place is Numbers 15 built in gold — commandments kept under the kanaph.

Even the burning ones wear the covering: the seraphim, six-winged, use four of their wings not to fly but to cover themselves before the fire (Isaiah 6:2).
And the psalm that promises refuge names what the covering is made of: __“under his wings shalt thou trust: his sym:sym-truth[truth] shall be thy shield and buckler”__ (Psalm 91:4) — and the truth, this book has shown, is the law.
To hide __“under the sym:sym-shadow[shadow] of thy wings”__ (Psalm 17:8) is not to hide from the law; it is to hide _inside_ it.

This is why the eagle’s wings of the Exodus land where they land:

[quote.scripture, Exodus 19:4-5]
____
Ye have seen what I did unto the Egyptians, and how I *bare you on eagles’ wings*, and brought you *unto myself*.
Now therefore, *if ye will obey my voice* indeed, and *keep my covenant*, then ye shall be a *peculiar treasure* unto me above all people…
____

The wings carry Israel to Sinai, to the commandments — __“so the LORD alone did lead him”__ (Deuteronomy 32:12).
At the end of the story the flight is replayed: the woman is given __“two *wings* of a great eagle, that she might fly into the wilderness”__ (Revelation 12:14) — the Exodus again, wings again, out of the serpent’s reach and into the place where God keeps His own.

[discrete]
=== Spread Your Wing Over Me

The edge that carries the law is the edge that makes covenant.
To spread your _kanaph_ over someone is to take them into covenant — we saw it last chapter, where the whole wedding was built on it: God __“spread [his] skirt”__ over Israel and __“entered into a covenant”__ (Ezekiel 16:8).
Ruth asks it of Boaz in the same word:

[quote.scripture, Ruth 3:9]
____
…spread therefore thy *skirt* over thine handmaid; for thou art a near kinsman.
____

To dwell under the wing, then, is to dwell under the covenant — and to uncover the wrong one is to violate it: the law forbids a man his father’s wife with the words __“nor *discover his father’s skirt*”__ (Deuteronomy 22:30), the kanaph as the covenant boundary itself, in the very chapter that commands the fringes.

Here is the grief of it: again and again He longed to gather His people under His wing, as a mother bird her young, and again and again they would not.
Jesus laments it over Jerusalem in that very image:

[quote.scripture, Matthew 23:37]
____
O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, thou that killest the prophets, and stonest them which are sent unto thee, how often would I have *gathered thy children together*, even as a hen gathereth her chickens *under her wings*, and *ye would not!*
____

Mark where that lament sits: the same chapter opened with the enlarged fringes of the Pharisees (Matthew 23:5) — Matthew 23 begins with the kanaph counterfeited and ends with the kanaph refused.
The wing offered is the covenant offered; to refuse it is rebellion, and it leaves the house bare — __“Behold, your house is left unto you desolate”__ (Matthew 23:38).

Refuge does not disappear when God’s wing is refused; it is replaced.
Assyria comes on wings of its own — __“the stretching out of his *wings* shall fill the breadth of thy land, O Immanuel”__ (Isaiah 8:8) — and Ezekiel’s empires are __“great eagles with great wings”__ carrying off the top of the sym:sym-cedar[cedar] (Ezekiel 17:3, 7).
Everyone ends up under some wing; the question is whose.

[discrete]
=== The Torn Skirt

Because the kanaph carries covenant, what happens to the skirt happens to the kingdom — and Scripture stages it twice in one book.
When Samuel turned to leave, Saul __“laid hold upon the *skirt* of his mantle, and it *rent*”__ — and Samuel reads the torn kanaph on the spot:

[quote.scripture, 1 Samuel 15:27-28]
____
And Samuel said unto him, The LORD hath *rent the kingdom* of Israel from thee this day, and hath given it to a neighbour of thine, that is *better than thou*.
____

The torn skirt _is_ the torn kingdom — the deposed-covenant verdict of the sym:sym-marriage[marriage] chapter, enacted in cloth.

David, hiding in the cave, __“cut off the *skirt* of Saul’s robe privily.
And it came to pass afterward, that *David’s heart smote him*, because he had cut off Saul’s skirt”__ (1 Samuel 24:4-5).
His men saw a missed kill; David felt what he had actually done — put a knife into the covenant edge of the LORD’s anointed.
Yet the same cut skirt becomes his evidence: __“see the *skirt of thy robe in my hand*: for in that I cut off the skirt… and killed thee not… *I have not sinned* against thee”__ (1 Samuel 24:11) — the kanaph held up in court, the covenant kept where it could have been cut.

For the skirt is where a people’s covenant record is kept.
__“In thy *skirts* is found the *blood of the souls of the poor innocents*”__ (Jeremiah 2:34); __“her filthiness is in her *skirts*; she remembereth not her last end”__ (Lamentations 1:9) — what is carried in the kanaph is what the covenant sees.
Haggai’s priests rule that holiness does _not_ rub off through the skirt, but uncleanness does (Haggai 2:12-13) — the fringe transmits no merit; it testifies.
And when almost everything is judged, the kanaph is where the sym:sym-remnant[remnant] rides out the fire: __“Thou shalt also take thereof a *few in number*, and *bind them in thy skirts*”__ (Ezekiel 5:3).

[discrete]
=== The Corners of the Earth

Scale the garment up, as this book keeps doing, and the earth itself wears one — with corners.
The dispersed of Israel wait at them: God will __“gather together the dispersed of Judah from the *four corners of the earth*”__ (Isaiah 11:12) — the four _kanaph_.
Songs rise __“from the *uttermost part* of the earth”__ (Isaiah 24:16) — the kanaph again.
Judgment arrives at them: __“An end, the end is come upon the *four corners* of the land”__ (Ezekiel 7:2).
And in Job, God handles the whole earth exactly like the garment it is:

[quote.scripture, Job 38:13]
____
That it might take hold of the *ends of the earth*, that the *wicked* might be *shaken out of it*?
____

The dawn grips the earth by its kanaph and shakes the wicked out of it, as a man shakes out a garment.
The world has edges the way the covenant garment has edges — and the scattered people of God are kept at them, like the few hairs bound in the prophet’s skirt, until the gathering.

[discrete]
=== The Corner of the Field

The same shape appears at the edge of a sym:sym-field[field] — though the word now changes, for a field’s corner is _pe’ah_, not _kanaph_.
That is the symbol working as it always does: by structure, not by spelling.
The reserved edge may be a wing, a border, or a corner; what repeats is the shape — the boundary kept back, not reaped for oneself.
And that edge God reserved:

[quote.scripture, Leviticus 19:9-10]
____
…thou shalt not wholly reap the *corners of thy* sym:sym-field[field]… thou shalt leave them for the sym:sym-poor[poor] and sym:sym-stranger[stranger]…
____

The forgotten sheaf and the field’s corner are not the owner’s; they belong __“for the sym:sym-stranger[stranger], for the sym:sym-fatherless[fatherless], and for the sym:sym-widow[widow]”__ (Deuteronomy 24:19).
A field is people — humanity is sown and reaped like grain — and the corner is only a thin border of the whole.
Yet that thin edge is enough to live on: it is where Ruth gleaned (Ruth 2:3), and where she was redeemed.
The remnant is fed at the edge.

The wing gathers this book to a single edge, and hands the reader a working tool.
The law is sewn into the kanaph, and there is healing in it; the ark of the covenant sits beneath it; the covenant is made by spreading it and read in what it carries; the kingdom tears when it tears; the dispersed wait at the earth’s four corners, and the poor glean at the field’s.
From here on, when your English Bible says _skirt_, _border_, _corner_, _quarter_, or _the ends of the earth_, check the word underneath: if the wing is there, the commandments are near — remembered, refused, or waiting to be done.
And the stranger, the fatherless, the widow who glean the corner are the next symbols to define — and after them, the remnant they add up to.
