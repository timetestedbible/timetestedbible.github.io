Four words carry most of what people mean by being right with God: to _love_ Him, to _know_ Him, to have _faith_, to _believe_.
The dictionary has quietly emptied all four.
Knowledge becomes information; love becomes a feeling; faith and belief become assent — things held in the head and the heart, invisible, asking nothing of the hands.
By those definitions a man may know, love, believe, and have faith, and keep not one commandment.

Scripture will not allow it.
All four meet in one covenant response — the commandments kept — but they are not interchangeable synonyms.
Each holds a distinct office:

.One covenant response, four offices — and the word for holding them
[.prevalence-table%unbreakable, frame=topbot, grid=none, cols="2,7"]
|===
| *Love* | self-giving covenant allegiance embodied in the keeping
| *Knowing* | the covenant relationship proved by the keeping
| *Faith* | trust held steady through obedience in hope of the promise
| *Belief* | receiving God's word as governing sym:sym-truth[truth] and submitting to it
| *Faithful* | allegiance kept *consistent* — over time, under weight
|===

This chapter proves each office in turn.

[discrete]
=== Love

Begin where the confusion is thickest.
Love, we are told, is chiefly what you feel.
Scripture insists that love take a body:

[quote.scripture, 1 John 5:3]
____
For this is the *love of God, that we keep his commandments*: and his commandments are not grievous.
____

Jesus says it plainly: __“If ye love me, keep my commandments”__ (John 14:15).
And John's second epistle runs the whole chain in three verses, and swears none of it is new:

[quote.scripture, "2 John 4-6"]
____
I rejoiced greatly that I found of thy children *walking in* sym:sym-truth[truth], as we have received a *commandment* from the Father… not as though I wrote a *new commandment* unto thee, but that which we had *from the beginning*, that we love one another. And *this is love, that we walk after his commandments*…
____

Walking in sym:sym-truth[truth] and walking after His commandments are one walk — for __“thy law is the truth”__ (Psalm 119:142) — and the commandment that defines love is expressly *not new*: it is __“from the beginning”__ — _archē_, the very word that opens Genesis in the Greek Bible, and John's gospel after it.
The commandment to love is as old as creation.
Nor was it new when Moses wrote it: loving Him is __“to keep the commandments of the LORD”__ (Deuteronomy 6:5; 10:12-13).

These verses do not reduce love to a naked outward motion.
Paul says a man may give all his goods and even his body, yet lack love (1 Corinthians 13:3); John says __“God is love,”__ manifested when He sent His Son (1 John 4:8-10).
God is not obeying a superior.
The common center is self-giving covenant allegiance; for the creature under God's authority, that allegiance has a commanded body — obedience.
Warmth may accompany love, but it cannot substitute for it.
A deed severed from allegiance can be empty sacrifice; an allegiance that persistently refuses His commands is a false profession.

[discrete]
=== The Love Chapter, Read with the Symbols

Jesus was asked which commandment is greatest, and His answer hangs the whole canon on love:

[quote.scripture, Matthew 22:37-40, NKJV]
____
“You shall *love* the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your mind.” This is the first and great *commandment*.
And the second is like it: “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.”
On these two commandments *hang all the Law and the Prophets*.
____

Two loves, two tables of the same stone: the first four commandments are how one loves God; the last six are how one loves a neighbor.
The Law does not compete with love; it *hangs from it* — and love without the commandments is a hook with nothing on it.

Paul's hymn to love is quoted at weddings as if it were a mood; read it with the symbols, and it is a legal argument.

[quote.scripture, 1 Corinthians 13:1-3, NKJV]
____
Though I speak with the tongues of men and of angels, but have not *love*, I have become *sounding brass* or a *clanging cymbal*.
And though I have the gift of prophecy, and sym:sym-understanding[understand] all mysteries and all sym:sym-knowing[knowledge], and though I have all sym:sym-faith[faith], so that I could remove sym:sym-mountain[mountains], but have not love, I am *nothing*.
And though I bestow all my goods to feed the sym:sym-poor[poor], and though I give my body to be burned, but have not love, it profits me *nothing*.
____

Tongues, prophecy, mysteries, knowledge, sym:sym-mountain[mountain]-moving faith — every prized gift in the assembly, weighed and found to be *noise* without love.
This chapter has already located love's commanded body: __“this is the love of God, that we keep his commandments”__ (1 John 5:3).
Read it so, and the audit is exact: spectacular gifts and even self-chosen sacrifice, detached from obedient allegiance, are a cymbal — sound with nothing inside.

Jesus ran the same audit on the same inventory, and passed the same sentence: __“have we not *prophesied* in Your name?… and done *many wonders* in Your name?”__ — __“depart from Me, you who *practice lawlessness*”__ (Matthew 7:22-23, NKJV).
They plead *powers*; the sentence answers *works*.
Paul names the missing allegiance; Jesus names its lawless fruit.
The terms are not synonyms, but the audit reaches the same defect: a life full of religious gifts while refusing the command.

Samuel passed the sentence first: Saul spared the best of the flock *to sacrifice it* — an offering in place of the command — and heard, __“to *obey* is better than *sacrifice*, and to hearken than the fat of rams”__ (1 Samuel 15:22).
Sacrifice, powers, gifts — the offering never outranks the command.

The catalog that follows is the law in motion.
__“Love *does not envy*”__ — the tenth commandment kept; __“does not seek its own… thinks no *evil*”__ — no ill worked to a neighbor; __“does not rejoice in *lawlessness*, but rejoices in the sym:sym-truth[truth]”__ — and __“thy *law* is the truth”__ (Psalm 119:142).
Paul is not decorating; he is itemizing: __“Love worketh no ill to his neighbour: therefore love is the *fulfilling of the law*”__ (Romans 13:10) — and __“the *purpose of the commandment* is love from a pure heart”__ (1 Timothy 1:5, NKJV).
The commandment's own goal is love; love's own shape is the commandments.

The hymn then turns prophetic:

[quote.scripture, 1 Corinthians 13:8, 10, 12, NKJV]
____
Love *never ends*. But whether there are prophecies, they will end; whether there are tongues, they will cease; whether there is knowledge, it will vanish away…
But when *that which is perfect* has come, then that which is in part will be done away…
For now we see *in a mirror, dimly*, but then *face to face*. Now I sym:sym-knowing[know] in part, but then I shall know just as I also am sym:sym-knowing[*known*].
____

James supplies the mirror: the hearer who is not a doer is __“a man observing his natural face *in a mirror*”__ — and the doer looks __“into the *perfect law* of sym:sym-liberty[liberty]”__ (James 1:23-25).
The mirror we see dimly in is the law; what it shows a man is his own face.
Now the reflection is dim and partial; the day the *perfect* comes, the mediated image gives way to __“face to face”__ — the phrase of Moses, with whom God spoke __“face to face, even plainly, and not in *riddles*”__ (Numbers 12:8).
The hymn lands on __“I shall *know* just as I also am *known*”__ — Paul's love chapter ends in the covenant word this chapter defines next.
The gifts are scaffolding and come down; love — covenant allegiance embodied in the commandments — __“never ends,”__ because the law it fulfills does not pass away.

[discrete]
=== Hate

Love has an opposite, and Scripture defines hate with the same precision — not a hotter feeling, but the other pole of the same axis.
Jacob served for two sisters, and the record grades the marriage in consecutive verses: __“he *loved* also Rachel *more than* Leah”__ (Genesis 29:30) — then, one verse later, __“when the LORD saw that Leah was *hated*, he opened her womb”__ (Genesis 29:31).
Leah is a wife in the house, bearing son after son; the hated one is the loved-less, the not-chosen-first — Scripture's own equation, stated where the word first touches a family.
The Gospel translates its own idiom the same way: Luke's __“If any man come to me, and *hate not* his father, and mother… he cannot be my disciple”__ (Luke 14:26) stands in Matthew as __“He that *loveth* father or mother *more than me* is not worthy of me”__ (Matthew 10:37).
To hate is to un-choose — a prior claim renounced.
Wisdom states it as a plain parallel: they __“*hated* knowledge, and *did not choose* the sym:sym-fear[fear of the LORD]”__ (Proverbs 1:29).

When God Himself hates, the grammar is divorce:

[quote.scripture, Hosea 9:15]
____
All their wickedness is in Gilgal: for there I *hated* them: for the wickedness of their doings I will *drive them out of mine house*, I will *love them no more*: all their princes are revolters.
____

Hated, driven out of the house, loved no more — the covenant repudiated step by step, in its own legal order.
The KJV itself knows the word is a standing and not a temperature: six times it renders the Hebrew's _one who hates_ as __“enemies,”__ __“enemy,”__ or __“foes”__ — __“the kisses of an *enemy* are deceitful”__ (Proverbs 27:6) is, in the Hebrew, the kisses of the hater.
Even where the KJV keeps __“hate,”__ the English has drifted underneath it: the translators' word could mean no more than _strongly dislike, reject_, and four centuries have hardened it into hot hostility and little else.
The heat a modern reader feels in these verses is an import the translators never wrote — the word must be read with the Bible's own sense, not our century's.

Where did the feelings go? Scripture keeps them in other words.
Esau's murderous grudge is plain: __“Esau *hated* [*bore a grudge against*] Jacob… then will I *slay* my brother”__ (Genesis 27:41, KJV).footnote:satam[_Satam_ (H7852): to bear a grudge or cherish hostility.]
God's covenant judgment uses the same English word differently: __“Esau have I *hated* [*un-chosen*]”__ (Malachi 1:3, KJV).footnote:sane[_Sane_ (H8130): to hate, reject, or treat as an enemy.]
The KJV's single __“hated”__ hides the two words.
Leviticus splits them in consecutive verses: __“thou shalt not *hate* thy brother in thine heart: thou shalt in any wise *rebuke* thy neighbour”__ (Leviticus 19:17) — the alternative the verse offers to heart-hate is not a warmer feeling but the spoken rebuke, for the brother left unrebuked in his sin is the brother quietly un-chosen — and the grudge follows in the next verse under its own word: __“thou shalt not avenge, *nor bear any grudge*”__ (Leviticus 19:18, _natar_).

So the axis stands whole: love keeps the covenant; hate is the covenant refused — __“them that *hate* me”__ set against __“them that *love* me, and *keep my commandments*”__ (Exodus 20:5-6) — one axis, both enacted, neither an emotional temperature.

[discrete]
=== Knowing

To _know_ God, we assume, is to know about Him — facts, doctrine, a sound creed.
But Jesus turns away people whose facts are exactly right:

[quote.scripture, Matthew 7:23]
____
And then will I profess unto them, *I never knew you*: depart from me, ye that *work lawlessness*.
____

He does not say _you never knew me_; He says _I never knew you_ — and the disqualifier is *lawlessness*.
The Greek is _anomia_ — without _nomos_, without *law* — the word the KJV renders __“iniquity,”__ and the word John uses when he defines sin: __“sin is the *transgression of the law*”__ (1 John 3:4).
Read __“iniquity”__ in the New Testament and this is usually the word underneath — law-breaking, wearing a vaguer name.
To be unknown by God is to be what Scripture calls the one not known — a sym:sym-stranger[stranger]; so the whole sentence reads __“I never knew you: depart from me”__ — put out of the household like a man who was never of it.

John gives the test plainly:

[quote.scripture, 1 John 2:3-4]
____
And hereby we do know that we *know him, if we keep his commandments*.
He that saith, I know him, and keepeth not his commandments, is a *liar*, and the sym:sym-truth[truth] is not in him.
____

To know God is a covenant bond proven by obedience — the same sense in which God says of Israel, __“You only have I known of all the families of the earth”__ (Amos 3:2).
Knowing is not information held; it is a relationship kept.

Paul says it with a knife: __“They profess that they *know God*; but in *works* they *deny him*”__ (Titus 1:16).
Hosea mourns the same equation from the ruins: __“My people are destroyed for *lack of knowledge*… seeing thou hast *forgotten the law* of thy God”__ (Hosea 4:6) — the knowledge lost was not information; it was the law.
God defines the knowing by a king’s life: he __“sym:sym-judgment[judged] the cause of the sym:sym-poor[poor] and needy… *was not this to know me?* saith the LORD”__ (Jeremiah 22:16).

The relationship it names runs deeper than acquaintance.
The first time Scripture uses the word of two persons it means the union of marriage — __“Adam *knew* Eve his wife; and she *conceived*”__ (Genesis 4:1) — the union that bears sym:sym-fruit[fruit].
To _know_, at root, is the intimate joining of husband and wife; which is why God calls His covenant a sym:sym-marriage[marriage] and His people a bride.

To know God is to be joined to Him as a wife to her husband — and that union keeps an order: __“the husband is the head of the wife, even as Christ is the head of the church”__ (Ephesians 5:23), the bride subject to her Head __“in every thing”__ (Ephesians 5:24).
So the intimacy and the obedience are not two things: to know Him is to be His, and to be His is to obey the Head.
The ones He never knew were never joined to Him — they said __“Lord”__ but were no bride of His.

Put the symbols together and the Shepherd says it whole. __“My sym:sym-sheep[sheep] hear my sym:sym-voice[voice]… and they follow me”__ (John 10:27): the sheep are His people, the voice is His words, and to know that voice is to follow it.
And they follow no other — __“a sym:sym-stranger[stranger] will they not follow… for they know not the sym:sym-voice[voice] of strangers”__ (John 10:5): the stranger is the one outside the covenant, so His sheep will not heed the voice, the teaching, of those outside it. __“He calleth his own sym:sym-sheep[sheep] by sym:sym-name[name]”__ (John 10:3) — and the sym:sym-name[name] is a symbol with its own chapter just ahead.
To the one who overcomes He gives __“a sym:sym-new[new] sym:sym-name[name] written, which no man knoweth saving he that receiveth it”__ (Revelation 2:17) — _known_ only by the one who receives and keeps it.
Sheep, voice, name, stranger, knowing: each word in its place, and not one of them a thing held only in the head.

[discrete]
=== Faith

Belief and faith are not two disconnected substances.
The Greek keeps them in one word-family: _pisteuō_ is to believe; _pistis_ is faith.
Ephesians gives the order: __“after that ye heard the word of truth… after that ye believed”__ (Ephesians 1:13), and Paul concludes, __“faith cometh by hearing, and hearing by the word of God”__ (Romans 10:17).
The word is heard; belief receives it as governing truth; hope lays hold of its unseen promise; faith holds that belief steady through delay and testing; works enact it in the life.
Belief can stop at factual assent, as the demons and fearful rulers show.
Living faith cannot exist without belief, and faith without works is dead.

Faith, we are taught, is the one thing that needs no works.
James answers head-on:

[quote.scripture, James 2:17]
____
Even so faith, if it hath not works, is *dead*, being alone.
____

He presses it: __“the devils also believe, and tremble”__ (James 2:19) — perfect assent, and it saves none of them.
The faith Scripture honors always moves: __“By faith Abraham… obeyed”__ (Hebrews 11:8).

The Hebrew behind _faith_ is _emunah_, faithfulness — steadiness, reliability — so __“the just shall live by his faith”__ (Habakkuk 2:4) is to live by one’s faithfulness.
The word’s first appearance in Scripture is a picture: Moses’ hands, held up over the battle, __“were *steady*”__ — _emunah_ — __“until the going down of the sun”__ (Exodus 17:12): faithfulness is hands that do not drop.
Faith is not a conviction lodged in the mind; it is trusting faithfulness — reliance on God's promise held steady through obedience.
The trust and the works are distinguishable, but living faith will not leave them separated.
James says Abraham's faith __“wrought with his works”__ and __“by works was faith made perfect”__ (James 2:22): the works do not rename faith; they enact it and bring it to its goal.

Hebrews states the office in one verse:

[quote.scripture, Hebrews 11:1]
____
Now faith is the *substance* of things *hoped for*, the *evidence* of things *not seen*.
____

Evidence is a thing produced and examined — the unseen promise is proved in the life by the faithfulness it produces.
Scripture keeps the pattern consistently: hope's object is unseen by definition — __“hope that is *seen* is not hope… but if we hope for that we see not, then do we with *patience* wait for it”__ (Romans 8:24-25) — and the patience is faithfulness holding while the thing stays out of sight.
The unseen things are the eternal ones (2 Corinthians 4:18), and __“we *walk* by faith, not by sight”__ (2 Corinthians 5:7): conduct stands in where sight is denied.

Hebrews 11 itself is a catalog of visible acts — Noah, __“warned of God of things *not seen as yet*… prepared an ark”__ (Hebrews 11:7): unseen warning, visible obedience.
What is hoped for, Scripture also names: the resurrection (Acts 24:15), eternal life __“*promised* before the world began”__ (Titus 1:2), the city and better country Abraham looked for (Hebrews 11:10, 16), His appearing (Titus 2:13).
Faith is the trusting fidelity that holds obedience while the promise is still out of sight — a life that can be seen (Matthew 5:16), held steady for things that cannot.

This is not works offered as the purchase price of the promise.
Paul defines that error before he excludes it: __“to him that *worketh* is the reward not reckoned of grace, but of *debt*”__ — then sets over against the wage-claim the man who __“believeth on him that justifieth the ungodly”__ (Romans 4:4-5).
Romans is not setting faith against obedience; it is setting faith against labor offered for a wage.
Jesus makes the distinction exact: __“when ye shall have done all those things which are commanded you, say, We are unprofitable servants: we have done that which was our duty to do”__ (Luke 17:10).
Even complete obedience leaves the Master owing no wages.
Ephesians keeps the same order: salvation is __“by grace… through faith… not of works,”__ and those so saved are immediately __“created in Christ Jesus *unto good works*”__ (Ephesians 2:8-10).
The works neither buy the gift nor put God in debt; they are the life the received gift creates.
Faith receives the promise, then remains faithful to the One who promised.

What, then, are the works, without which faith is dead?
Scripture defines the word where the law is first taught: Jethro counsels Moses to __“teach them *ordinances and laws*, and shalt shew them the way wherein they must walk, and *the work that they must do*”__ (Exodus 18:20).
Ordinances taught, a way walked, *the* work done — a work is a deed, not a synonym for obedience.
A work becomes obedience when it executes a legitimate command; it becomes disobedience when the command or deed conflicts with God's command.
Romans 4 speaks of a laborer's work presented for wages; James 2 speaks of Abraham's work as the visible enactment of faith.
The word names the deed; the context names its master and whether any wage is claimed.
Paul's distinct uses of _law_, _works_, _obedience_, _wages_, and _duty_ are traced occurrence by occurrence in link:/books/symbolic-language/the-end-of-the-law/[The End of the Law].
The psalmist holds God's works and God's commandments as the two halves of one line: __“the *works* of his hands are verity and judgment; all his *commandments* are sure”__ (Psalm 111:7).
Jesus holds His works with the commandment-keeping verb: __“he that overcometh, and *keepeth my works* unto the end”__ (Revelation 2:26) — works can be *kept* because works are commandment-shaped.
So the sentence James wrote completes itself: faith without works is dead because works are the commandment-shaped enactment of trust — the keeping this chapter has been proving — and a faith that never acts does not become faithfulness.

[discrete]
=== Belief

Which leaves _believe_, the word on which whole systems are built — and here the ground is already broken: the link:/books/symbolic-language/gospel/[Gospel] chapter caught Paul trading __“obeyed”__ for __“believed”__ in a single breath (Romans 10:16), and pointed at the Greek of John 3:36.
Now read that famous verse whole:

[quote.scripture, John 3:36, ASV]
____
He that *believeth* on the Son hath eternal sym:sym-life[life]; but he that *obeyeth not* the Son shall not see life…
____

In the covenant question John is asking, the opposite of believing is disobeying.
Receiving the Son's word as governing truth means submitting to the Son who spoke it; persistent refusal to obey exposes the claim of belief as empty.

That does not mean Scripture never uses _believe_ for accepting a fact.
James says __“the devils also *believe*, and tremble”__ (James 2:19), and the Gospels say those same spirits know who Christ is and obey when His authority commands them: __“he commandeth even the unclean spirits, and *they do obey him*”__ (Mark 1:27).
Their belief is real enough to produce trembling and compelled compliance; it is not love, covenant allegiance, or saving trust.
John records the human counterpart: many rulers __“believed on him,”__ but would not confess Him because __“they loved the praise of men more than the praise of God”__ (John 12:42-43).
A man may believe a fact and still act against it because another love, fear, or appetite governs him.

Saving belief is therefore not bare assent and not obedience renamed.
It is receiving God's testimony as governing truth in a response that submits to it.
Where that submission is persistently refused, Scripture judges the claimed belief by its fruit and calls the condition unbelief.

Scripture keeps the pair together: Israel could not enter the land for __“unbelief”__ — they __“believed not”__ — and the refusal that exposed their distrust was disobedience (Hebrews 3:18-19).
Abraham, who __“believed in the LORD”__ (Genesis 15:6), is later shown to have been __“justified by works, when he had offered Isaac”__ (James 2:21): the obedience did not replace his belief; it brought the belief to its declared end, __“and by works was faith made perfect”__ (James 2:22).

The four words converge, then, in one covenant response — and keep four distinct offices.
*Love* is self-giving covenant allegiance embodied, for us, in the keeping: __“this is the love of God, that we keep his commandments”__ (1 John 5:3).
*Faith* keeps them in hope of the promise — __“faith is the substance of things *hoped for*”__ (Heb 11:1); Abraham __“obeyed; and he went out, not knowing whither he went… for he looked for a city”__ (Heb 11:8-10).
*Belief* receives the word as governing sym:sym-truth[truth] and submits to it: Thomas believed __“because thou hast seen”__ — and the Lord blessed __“they that have not seen, and yet have believed”__ (John 20:29), grading the two grounds in a single sentence.

*Knowing* keeps them as a bride keeps covenant — __“I will betroth thee unto me in *faithfulness*: and thou shalt *know* the LORD”__ (Hos 2:20); __“He judged the cause of the poor and needy… was not this to know me?”__ (Jer 22:16).
One covenant response, four distinguishable offices — and *faithful* is Scripture's word for that allegiance kept *consistent*: Moses' hands __“steady until the going down of the sun”__ (Ex 17:12), the servant found __“so doing”__ when his lord comes (Matt 24:46), __“faithful unto death”__ (Rev 2:10).
Flatten them into synonyms and each goes invisible — which is what the dictionary did, so a man could claim all four and live as he pleased.
Scripture binds them back together and sets them where they can be seen — in a life that does what God said.

See, then, what the symbols do to the simplest promise of all.

[quote.scripture, Romans 10:13]
____
For whosoever shall *call upon* the sym:sym-name[name] of the Lord shall be *saved*.
____

It is heard as a sentence you say — profess the name, and salvation is done.
Then remember the many this chapter has already met — who prophesied and did wonders *in that name*, and were not known.
They said the name; whatever it is to _call upon_ it, they did not do it.
So what *is* the sym:sym-name[name], that calling upon it saves and merely saying it does not?
The question has a chapter of its own just ahead — link:/books/symbolic-language/the-name/[The Name] — and everything this chapter proved about knowing, loving, and obeying rides on its answer.

This is what knowing the symbolic language does, and why it changes everything: the verse you thought you had mastered turns out to ask for your whole life.
