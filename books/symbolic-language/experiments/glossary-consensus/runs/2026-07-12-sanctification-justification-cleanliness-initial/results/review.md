## Why the chapter persuaded them

### Cleanliness

All three models judged the nonculpable cases decisive. Childbirth, marital
relations, and corpse contact show that uncleanness cannot intrinsically mean
sin or guilt. The book's functional definition — defilement that bars permitted
contact, approach, or use — includes those cases while also explaining moral
cleansing. The explicit holy/common and clean/unclean axes, and the distinction
between cleanse and sanctify, gave the book greater coverage with fewer
exceptions than the inherited moral-purity shorthand.

### Justification

All three models accepted the broader courtroom definition: judging, declaring,
or demonstrating a person, word, deed, or cause right against a question or
charge, with condemnation as the opposite. Justifying God, wisdom, words, deeds,
and oneself prevented the Romans-only formulation from serving as the lexical
definition. Romans 4:5-8, Romans 8:33, Deuteronomy 25:1, and Proverbs 17:15 then
carried the salvation application without changing the word's core. The imported
provider-specific findings on Faith, Works, and Righteousness supported rather
than prejudged the secondary clauses.

### Sanctification

All three models judged the non-moral uses decisive. Christ, God, God's Name, a
day, food, vessels, firstborn animals, and an unbelieving spouse cannot undergo
progressive moral improvement. The holy/common contrast, the firstborn statement
“it is mine,” the appointed-use texts, and the once-for-all/progressive pair
supported one ownership-claim-appointment definition. The chapter preserved
moral conduct as the necessary fitting of a claimed person without making moral
improvement the root meaning.

## Editorial implication

The chapter now fills the intended gap. It does not need more apologetic material
to satisfy the three-model test. Additional expansion would risk repeating the
Faith, Works, and Righteousness chapters without improving the adjudicated result.
