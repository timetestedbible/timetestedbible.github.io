Few commands are easier to applaud than the care of widows and orphans. __“Pure religion and undefiled,”__ says James, is __“to visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction”__ (James 1:27); even the godless agree, funding the work through the state.
It is a good thing, and we are commanded to it.
But it is so plainly good that we read past it — and we read past it assuming one thing: that we stand among the givers, never the widow, never the orphan.
The symbolic language overturns the assumption.

[discrete]
=== We Are the Widow

A widow is a woman whose husband is dead or has put her away; the fatherless are children left without their father.
Now hear how God speaks of His own people when they break covenant.
He hands them a bill of divorce:

[quote.scripture, Isaiah 50:1]
____
…Where is the bill of your mother’s *divorcement*, whom I have *put away*?… for your iniquities have ye sold yourselves…
____

And the city, cut off from her Husband, sits alone: __“how is she become as a widow!”__
(Lamentations 1:1).
Her children take up the cry: __“We are orphans and fatherless, our mothers are as widows”__ (Lamentations 5:3).
This is the turn.
The widow and the fatherless are not only the poor down the street; they are the covenant people themselves, severed from the God who was their Husband and their Father.
We are the widow; we are the orphans.
And in the Bible’s symbolic language this confession has an exact opposite, and she is a queen.
__“I sit a *queen*, and am *no widow*, and shall see no sorrow”__ (Revelation 18:7) — Babylon’s boast in her heart, quoted word for word from Isaiah’s oracle against her: __“thou that sayest in thine heart, I am, and none else beside me; I shall *not sit as a widow*, neither shall I know the *loss of children*”__ (Isaiah 47:8).
She assumes herself married to the King — Vashti’s posture, the deposed queen of the sym:sym-marriage[marriage] chapter — and the sentence answers the boast in kind: __“these two things shall come to thee *in a* sym:sym-moment[moment] *in one day*, the loss of children, and *widowhood*”__ (Isaiah 47:9); __“therefore shall her plagues come *in one day*, death, and mourning, and famine”__ (Revelation 18:8) — the sym:sym-moment[moment] itself has a study of its own in Part Five.
Two women, one word: the covenant people who *confess* their widowhood are the ones the Redeemer remarries — and the queen who *denies* hers is made a widow in a moment.
The whole difference is the confession.

[discrete]
=== Thy Maker Is Thine Husband

Which is why God’s fierce defense of the widow and the fatherless is no generic charity — it is a Husband and a Father moving toward His own:

[quote.scripture, Isaiah 54:5]
____
For thy *Maker* is thine *husband*; the LORD of hosts is his name; and thy *Redeemer* the Holy One of Israel…
____

He __“doth execute the judgment of the fatherless and widow, and loveth the stranger, in giving him food and raiment”__ (Deuteronomy 10:18).
And the corner of the field left unreaped from the last chapter was reserved for exactly these: __“for the stranger, for the fatherless, and for the widow”__ (Deuteronomy 24:19).
The widowed sym:sym-remnant[remnant] gleans the sym:sym-wings[edge], as Ruth did, and is brought home by the Redeemer.

[discrete]
=== The Stranger

The third name in that gleaning law is a defined symbol, and Scripture fixes its meaning by setting it against its exact opposite:

[quote.scripture, Ephesians 2:19]
____
Now therefore ye are *no more strangers and foreigners*, but *fellowcitizens* with the saints, and of the household of God.
____

The opposite of a stranger is a _fellowcitizen_; so a stranger is a non-citizen — one who belongs to another commonwealth than the one he stands in.
And Paul names the commonwealth in the verse just before: the Gentiles had been __“aliens from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers from the covenants of promise, having no hope, and without God”__ (Ephesians 2:12).
The covenant is the commonwealth, and to be a stranger is to hold no citizenship in it.

That key turns both ways.
Outside the covenant, a man is a stranger to God’s commonwealth — and the law leaves the field’s corner to draw him in.
The gleaning stranger is the _ger_, the sojourner who has left his own people to dwell among God’s; Israel had been one in Egypt — __“ye were strangers in the land of Egypt”__ (Leviticus 19:34) — so they must love him, and bring him toward citizenship: let him keep the Passover, and __“he shall be as one that is born in the land”__ (Exodus 12:48).
This is the whole of Ruth, a Moabite stranger who gleans the corner and is grafted into the line of the Redeemer, until the far-off are __“no more strangers… but fellowcitizens.”__

And once a man is made a citizen of God’s commonwealth, the symbol inverts: he becomes a stranger to the _world_.
The patriarchs __“confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth,”__ and Hebrews gives the reason — __“they seek a country… an heavenly”__ (Hebrews 11:13-16); __“our conversation”__ — the word is _citizenship_ — __“is in heaven”__ (Philippians 3:20); even in the land God says, __“ye are strangers and sojourners with me”__ (Leviticus 25:23).
The stranger to the world keeps the one law that is now his: __“I am a stranger in the earth: hide not thy commandments from me”__ (Psalm 119:19).
The covenant that ends a man’s estrangement from God begins his estrangement from the world.

[discrete]
=== The Poor and the Rich

There is a fourth name at that unreaped corner.
The gleaning law itself sets it beside the stranger — __“thou shalt leave them for the *poor* and stranger”__ (Leviticus 19:10) — and Zechariah gathers the whole defended household in one breath:

[quote.scripture, Zechariah 7:10]
____
And oppress not the sym:sym-widow[widow], nor the sym:sym-fatherless[fatherless], the sym:sym-stranger[stranger], nor the sym:sym-poor[poor]; and let none of you imagine evil against his brother in your heart.
____

Widow, fatherless, stranger, poor: one company, gleaning one corner.
And the fourth name turns exactly as the first three did.
We read __“the poor”__ as the man with less money than we have, count ourselves among the givers — and then open the promises and find the poor holding all of them:

[quote.scripture, Luke 6:20]
____
Blessed be ye *poor*: for yours is the *kingdom of God*.
____

Why should an empty wallet inherit a kingdom?
It should not, and it does not — because the wallet was never the measure.
The Hebrew word rendered __“poor”__ in these promises is the twin of the word rendered __“meek”__ — Strong’s concordance calls the one __“practically the same”__ as the other — and both are built on a root that means _bowed down_.
The poor of the promises are the bowed.
Watch the two renderings trade places across the same texts.
The psalm says __“the *meek* shall inherit the earth”__ (Psalm 37:11), and Jesus repeats it word for word: __“Blessed are the *meek*: for they shall inherit the earth”__ (Matthew 5:5).
Isaiah says the Anointed comes __“to preach good tidings unto the *meek*”__ (Isaiah 61:1) — and when Jesus stands up in Nazareth and reads that very verse, it comes out __“to preach the sym:sym-gospel[gospel] to the *poor*”__ (Luke 4:18): the same people under either rendering.
Matthew’s beatitude names the state outright: __“Blessed are the *poor in spirit*: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven”__ (Matthew 5:3) — bowed before God, nothing in the hand held up to Him.

Hear God say whom He means:

[quote.scripture, Isaiah 66:2]
____
…but to this man will I look, even to him that is *poor* and of a *contrite spirit*, and *trembleth at my word*.
____

The poor man God looks to is defined by his posture toward the word, not his balance.
He is the sym:sym-remnant[remnant]: __“I will also leave in the midst of thee an afflicted and *poor* people, and they shall trust in the sym:sym-name[name] of the LORD”__ (Zephaniah 3:12).
Hannah sang his future — __“He raiseth up the *poor* out of the dust… to make them inherit the throne of glory”__ (1 Samuel 2:8) — and James states it flat: __“Hath not God chosen the *poor* of this world *rich in faith*, and heirs of the kingdom?”__
(James 2:5).
When John the Baptist sent from prison to ask whether Jesus was the One, the closing credential of the answer was this: __“the *poor* have the sym:sym-gospel[gospel] preached to them”__ (Matthew 11:5).

Now the other column.
If the poor are the bowed, the sym:sym-rich[rich] of the symbol are the unbowed — the self-sufficient who have need of nothing.
Solomon wrote the proverb: __“There is that maketh himself *rich*, yet hath nothing: there is that maketh himself *poor*, yet hath great riches”__ (Proverbs 13:7) — and two churches in Revelation act out its two halves.
To suffering Smyrna: __“I know thy… *poverty*, (but thou art *rich*)”__ (Revelation 2:9).
To comfortable Laodicea: __“thou sayest, I am *rich*, and increased with goods, and have need of nothing; and knowest not that thou art wretched, and miserable, and *poor*, and blind, and sym:sym-naked[naked]”__ (Revelation 3:17) — and the counsel that follows prices heaven’s currency: __“buy of me gold tried in the fire, that thou mayest be rich; and white raiment, that thou mayest be clothed”__ (Revelation 3:18) — faith tried in the fire, and the sym:sym-garment[garment] of righteousness.
Jesus names the true wealth in a phrase — the fool who filled his barns was __“not *rich toward God*”__ (Luke 12:21) — and pronounces the woe: __“woe unto you that are *rich*! for ye have received your consolation”__ (Luke 6:24).
And the exchange at the center of the sym:sym-gospel[gospel] is stated in exactly these terms: __“though he was *rich*, yet for your sakes he became *poor*, that ye through his *poverty* might be *rich*”__ (2 Corinthians 8:9).

And here a long-standing paradox dissolves.
If the poor of the promises are the bowed, then the rich of the woes are the proud — the unbowed — and the hardest saying about them was never about a balance sheet: __“How hardly shall they that have riches enter into the kingdom of God!”__
(Mark 10:23).
The disciples were astonished, and Jesus answered again with the symbol opened: __“Children, how hard is it for them that *trust in riches* to enter into the kingdom of God!”__
(Mark 10:24) — the trust, not the treasure.
Read the camel and the needle as a statement about money, and Scripture contradicts itself on its face: Abraham was __“very rich in cattle, in silver, and in gold”__ (Genesis 13:2), Job’s latter end was blessed with double, Joseph administered an empire’s wealth and David a kingdom’s — and all of them heirs.
Read it as the symbol reads — the unbowed cannot pass the low door — and the paradox was never there: __“with men this is impossible; but with God all things are possible”__ (Matthew 19:26), and what God does with the proud is bow them.

As always, the symbol does not dissolve the literal command; it deepens it.
__“The poor shall never cease out of the land: therefore I command thee… thou shalt open thine hand wide unto thy brother, to thy poor”__ (Deuteronomy 15:11).
The corner stays unreaped and the hand stays open — but the giver now knows what he is handling, and where he himself stands.
Before God, every man is the beggar lifted from the dunghill (Psalm 113:7); the only question is whether he knows it, as Smyrna did, or says __“I have need of nothing.”__

[discrete]
=== Food and Raiment

He gives the widow __“food and raiment”__ (Deuteronomy 10:18) — and by now neither word is bare.
Scripture makes bread the word of God — __“man doth not live by sym:sym-bread[bread] only, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of the LORD”__ (Deuteronomy 8:3) — and the sym:sym-garment[garment], as the sym:sym-marriage[marriage] chapter showed, is the righteous deeds of the saints — deeds He ordains and grants her to do (Ephesians 2:10; Revelation 19:8).
So to feed and clothe His widowed people is to give them the word, and the works the word produces.
And the worst famine is not of bread:

[quote.scripture, Amos 8:11]
____
…I will send a famine in the land, not a famine of bread… but of *hearing the words of the LORD*.
____

This is why the charge to the shepherd is __“Feed my sheep”__ (John 21:17); why the faithful servant is the one who gives the household __“meat in due season”__ (Matthew 24:45); why the commission is to teach the nations __“to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded”__ (Matthew 28:20).
To feed is to teach the commandments; to clothe is to grant the righteous deeds the word teaches.
And the covenant itself is taken the same way — by eating His flesh and drinking His blood: __“the bread that I will give is my flesh”__ (John 6:51), and __“the words that I speak unto you, they are spirit, and they are life”__ (John 6:63).
His body is the bread, the word to be eaten and kept; His blood is what washes the garment clean.
The crowd could not bear it — __“from that time many of his disciples went back, and walked no more with him”__ (John 6:66) — because they took the symbol for the flesh.

So the command to care for the widow and the fatherless reaches all the way down.
They are God’s own people, cut off and gleaning at the edge; to feed them is to bring them the word, to clothe them is to robe them in righteousness, and to gather them is the work of the Husband who will not leave them widowed.
The next time Scripture says _feed the hungry_, hear what is handed across: the bread of His word, to a people hungrier than they know.
