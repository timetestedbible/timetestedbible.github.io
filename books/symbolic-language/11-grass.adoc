The last chapter set a mountain over a tree — a kingdom over a nation. Beneath them both is the smallest of the three figures, and the shortest-lived:

[quote.scripture, Isaiah 40:6-8]
____
…All *flesh is grass*, and all the goodliness thereof is as the *flower of the field*… The *grass withereth*, the flower fadeth… but the *word of our God shall stand for ever*.
____

Grass is a man — a single mortal, up in the morning and gone by evening. __“As for man, his days are as grass… the wind passeth over it, and it is gone”__ (Psalm 103:15-16); __“in the morning it flourisheth… in the evening it is cut down, and withereth”__ (Psalm 90:5-6). So the three figures of a people are complete: a _mountain_ is the kingdom, a _tree_ the nation, and the _grass_ the individual who springs up and dies within it. And the New Testament simply hands the definition back: __“all flesh is as grass… The grass withereth”__ (1 Peter 1:24).

[discrete]
=== Grain Is Grass

But grass is not only the picture of frailty; it is also the field God farms. The grains that feed the world — barley and wheat — are grasses, and with them the symbol turns from the withering blade to the standing crop. The field is sown, and Jesus names both the seed and the soil: __“The seed is the word of God”__ (Luke 8:11), scattered on the hearts of men — some hard, some shallow, some good ground that bears. And the seed must die to live: __“Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit”__ (John 12:24). The word buried in a man is a seed that dies and rises into a harvest.

[discrete]
=== The Harvest

Grain is grown to be reaped, and the reaping is judgment. The prophets fixed that meaning long before the parables — __“Put ye in the sickle, for the harvest is ripe… for their wickedness is great”__ (Joel 3:13); the nations gathered __“as the sheaves into the floor”__ to be threshed by Zion (Micah 4:12-13). So when Jesus says the same, He is not inventing an image but citing one:

[quote.scripture, Matthew 13:39]
____
…the *harvest is the end of the world*; and the *reapers are the angels*.
____

The field is the world, the crop is mankind, and the harvest is the day it is cut down.

[discrete]
=== Wheat and Chaff

At that reaping two kinds are parted, and the whole vocabulary is Old Testament. The good grain is the righteous; the chaff is the wicked — __“The ungodly… are like the chaff which the wind driveth away”__ (Psalm 1:4), __“chased as the chaff of the mountains before the wind”__ (Isaiah 17:13). John the Baptist says it in one breath and expects to be understood: __“he will throughly purge his floor, and gather his wheat into the garner; but he will burn up the chaff with unquenchable fire”__ (Matthew 3:12). And the parable of the wheat and the tares only names what the figures already meant:

[quote.scripture, Matthew 13:38]
____
The *field is the world*; the good *seed are the children of the kingdom*; but the *tares are the children of the wicked one*…
____

Wheat gathered into the barn, chaff and tares burned in the fire: the righteous kept, the wicked consumed. The threshingfloor and the winnowing fan are the day of judgment, sorting the harvest of men.

[discrete]
=== The Firstfruits

One grain is reaped before all the rest. Barley ripens first, in the spring, and the law commanded that its first sheaf be brought and waved before the LORD — __“a sheaf of the firstfruits of your harvest”__ (Leviticus 23:10) — the pledge of the whole harvest to come. That sheaf is Christ: __“now is Christ risen from the dead, and become the firstfruits of them that slept”__ (1 Corinthians 15:20), raised in the very season of the wave sheaf, the first of a harvest that rises after Him. Barley is the humble, early grain — the loaf a soldier dreams, which is Israel, tumbling into the enemy camp and overturning it (Judges 7:13); the five barley loaves a boy carries, from which a multitude is fed (John 6:9). The firstfruits are few and lowly, but they carry the whole field in promise.

[discrete]
=== Ears to Hear

Set it together and the parables come clear. When Jesus told of a sower, of wheat among tares, of a harvest and a burning, He was not speaking a new tongue; He was speaking the oldest one. A hearer who knew that flesh is grass, that the harvest is the day of the LORD, that the chaff is the wicked and the firstfruits the LORD's own, needed no interpreter — the meaning was written already in the Law and the Prophets. That is why He could say __“Who hath ears to hear, let him hear”__ (Matthew 13:9), and then wonder that His own disciples asked what the parable meant — __“Know ye not this parable? and how then will ye know all parables?”__ (Mark 4:13). The words were sown centuries before; the parable only brought in the sheaves. To read the New Testament by the Old is to reap what was long since planted.
