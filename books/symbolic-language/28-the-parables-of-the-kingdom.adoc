At the end of the seven parables of the kingdom, Jesus gave His hearers an exam of one question: __“Have ye understood all these things?”__
(Matthew 13:51).
This book opened with His warning that most would not — seeing, they would not see — and with His astonishment that His own disciples stumbled at the first one: __“Know ye not this parable? and how then will ye know all parables?”__
(Mark 4:13).
The sower, He said, is the key to all of them; and the Introduction staked this book’s whole claim on that sentence: the parables are written in a vocabulary Scripture had already defined, and the man who holds the vocabulary can read them.
The vocabulary is now in hand.
So take the exam: all seven parables of Matthew 13, read straight through by substitution — and where a word resists, mark it honestly, for that is how every symbol in this book was found.

[discrete]
=== The Sower, and the Wheat and the Tares

__“Behold, a sower went forth to sow”__ (Matthew 13:3).
The sym:sym-seed[seed] is the word of God (Luke 8:11); the soils are hearts; and the __“fowls”__ that devour the wayside seed (Matthew 13:4) are decoded by the Lord Himself — __“then cometh the *wicked one*, and catcheth away that which was sown”__ (Matthew 13:19).
Hold that decoding: birds that snatch and lodge are not always songbirds; the fowls can be the agents of the evil one — a reading Scripture set up when the fowls came down on Abram’s covenant sacrifice and __“Abram drove them away”__ (Genesis 15:11).
The second parable Jesus decoded for us entire, and the grass chapter walked it: the field is the world, the good seed the children of the kingdom, the sym:sym-tares[tares] the children of the wicked one, the sym:sym-harvest[harvest] the end of the age (Matthew 13:38-39).
Two parables, and the substitutions are not ours — they are His.
That is the pattern the rest must follow.

[discrete]
=== The Mustard Seed and the Leaven

__“The kingdom of heaven is like to a grain of mustard seed… which indeed is the least of all seeds: but when it is grown… it becometh a *tree*, so that the birds of the air come and *lodge in the branches* thereof”__ (Matthew 13:31-32).
Substitute: a sym:sym-tree[tree] is a nation, and fowls lodging in branches is Scripture’s fixed picture of peoples sheltering under a kingdom’s reach — Nebuchadnezzar’s tree, where __“the fowls of the heaven dwelt in the boughs”__ (Daniel 4:12), and God’s planted cedar, where __“all fowl of every wing”__ nest (Ezekiel 17:23).
The kingdom starts as the least of seeds — one word, one man, twelve fishermen — and grows into a nation-tree sheltering every people: the sym:sym-mountain[mountain] of Daniel 2 told in a gardener’s vocabulary.
But remember the fowls of the first parable before reading this one as pure triumph; the branches that shelter the nations shelter what the nations bring.

Then the strangest of the seven, if the symbols mean what Scripture made them mean:

[quote.scripture, Matthew 13:33]
____
The kingdom of heaven is like unto *leaven*, which a *woman* took, and *hid* in three measures of *meal*, till the *whole was leavened*.
____

Tradition reads it as the gospel quietly permeating the world — but test that against the vocabulary.
sym:sym-leaven[Leaven], everywhere Scripture defines it, is the word soured — purged before Passover, banned from the altar, and named by Jesus Himself, in this same gospel, as __“the *doctrine* of the Pharisees”__ (Matthew 16:12); Paul cries __“purge out the old leaven”__ (1 Corinthians 5:7).
If the symbol holds here, the parable is not a promise but a warning laid alongside the tares: as the enemy sows counterfeit wheat in the field, so corrupt teaching, hidden in the meal by stealth, works __“till the *whole* was leavened”__ — the kingdom’s bread-supply fermented from inside.
And the parable flags two words this book has not yet mapped: the _woman_ who hides it, and the _three measures of meal_ — the very quantity Sarah kneaded for the LORD at Mamre (Genesis 18:6).
Mark them as this book has marked every candidate: gather the uses, and let Scripture define them.
The exam does not end the work; it teaches you to see where the work is.

[discrete]
=== The Treasure and the Pearl

__“The kingdom of heaven is like unto *treasure hid in a field*; the which when a man hath found, he hideth, and for joy thereof goeth and selleth all that he hath, and *buyeth that field*”__ (Matthew 13:44).
The sym:sym-field[field], Jesus already ruled, is the world.
And the treasure has a definition older than the parable: __“if ye will obey my voice indeed, and keep my covenant, then ye shall be a *peculiar treasure* unto me above all people”__ (Exodus 19:5).
The treasure hid in the field is the covenant people hidden in the world — the sym:sym-remnant[remnant] scattered and unseen — and the man who sells all he has to buy the whole field for the treasure’s sake is the Redeemer, who __“gave himself”__ for it.
He did not buy the field because He wanted the field; He bought the world to take His treasure out of it.

Now grade the common teaching, for this parable is almost always preached inverted: God — or salvation — is the treasure, and we the fortunate finder who gladly gives up everything.
Test it against the text: the buyer __“selleth *all* that he hath”__ — and in this same gospel, the one man told to do exactly that __“went away sorrowful”__ (Matthew 19:21-22).
No son of Adam ever sold all he had for the kingdom; One did — __“though he was rich… he became poor”__ (2 Corinthians 8:9) — and the treasure was defined at Sinai before the parable ever borrowed it: the *people* are the treasure, and the seeker is God.
The Hebrew Matthew tradition sharpens the picture, reading *gold* hidden in the field — and no man buys a field for gold *coins*; coins you pocket and walk away.
Gold in a field is *ore*: scattered through the ground, dug, crushed, and refined — exactly what the prophets say the Owner does with His treasure: __“I will refine them as silver is refined, and will *try them as gold* is tried”__ (Zechariah 13:9); __“when he hath tried me, I shall come forth *as gold*”__ (Job 23:10).
He buys the whole field, mines His people out of it, and refines what He mines.

__“Again, the kingdom of heaven is like unto a merchant man, seeking goodly *pearls*: who, when he had found *one pearl of great price*, went and *sold all that he had*, and bought it”__ (Matthew 13:45-46).
The same purchase told again — but the object is new to us, and worth the dig it invites: a pearl is the one gem drawn up out of the _sea_ — the sym:sym-sea[sea] this book has read from the beginning as the mass of the nations — a thing of great price formed slowly, in hiddenness, around a wound.
That is as far as our mapped vocabulary carries it, and honesty is part of the method: _pearl_ goes on the candidate list beside the woman and the meal.
What the two parables already prove between them is the price: in both, the buyer __“selleth *all* that he hath.”__
Whatever the kingdom costs, it costs everything — and He paid it first.

[discrete]
=== The Net

__“Again, the kingdom of heaven is like unto a *net*, that was cast into the *sea*, and gathered of *every kind*: which, when it was full, they drew to shore… and gathered the good into vessels, but cast the bad away”__ (Matthew 13:47-48).
By now this one reads at sight.
The sym:sym-sea[sea] is the peoples; the sym:sym-fish[fish] are men in it; the sym:sym-net[net] is the sym:sym-gospel[gospel] gathering __“of every kind”__ — every nation and tongue — and the sorting on the shore is the same judgment the sym:sym-harvest[harvest] told in field-language: wheat to the barn and tares to the fire, good fish to vessels and bad away (Matthew 13:49-50).
Two landscapes, one message — the field for the covenant land, the sea for the nations — because the kingdom is drawing from both.

[discrete]
=== A Similitude Lived Out Loud

And then Jesus did what He had been saying.
With the symbols in hand, the feeding of the multitude reads as the lived parable it is — John even calls it a _sign_ (John 6:14).
A boy has __“five *barley* loaves, and two small *fishes*”__ (John 6:9); from them a multitude is fed, and __“they took up of the fragments that remained *twelve baskets* full”__ (Matthew 14:20).
Five loaves: the five books of the Law, the sym:sym-bread[bread] of the word — and sym:sym-barley[barley], the humble firstfruits grain that is Israel.
Two sym:sym-fish[fishes]: witnesses, for the Lord sent His disciples __“by *two and two*”__ (Mark 6:7) to be fishers of men.
The bread is blessed, broken, and handed to the disciples, and the disciples to the people — and as the word is shared it multiplies, until the hungry are filled and twelve baskets remain, one for each tribe.
The Law, carried by witnesses, feeds the famished, and there is more left at the end than was held at the start.
The crowd that watched it graded the sign correctly: __“This is of a truth *that prophet* that should come into the world”__ (John 6:14) — they read a similitude and recognized the Prophet like unto Moses, who had also fed a multitude on bread from heaven.

[discrete]
=== Have Ye Understood?

The disciples answered Jesus’ one-question exam __“Yea, Lord”__ (Matthew 13:51) — and He immediately described what such a reader becomes:

[quote.scripture, Matthew 13:52]
____
Therefore every scribe which is instructed unto the kingdom of heaven is like unto a man that is an householder, which bringeth forth out of his *treasure* things *new and old*.
____

Things new and old — the old vocabulary and the new speech written in it; the Law and the Prophets, and the parables that assumed you knew them.
That is what this book set out to make of its reader: not a keeper of our conclusions but a scribe instructed — one who can take a text he has never been taught, substitute what Scripture itself defined, mark honestly what remains unmapped, and go gather the uses.
Five of the seven parables opened at a touch; the leaven overturned a tradition; the pearl and the woman and the meal are waiting for you.
The exam is not over, because the language does not stop.
__“Who hath ears to hear, let him hear.”__
