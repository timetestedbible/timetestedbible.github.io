# Book Descriptions

## Best Audience

The self-directed, method-hungry Bible student who already cross-references Scripture and wants a reproducible way to test inherited interpretations. This reader values evidence over endorsements, expects conclusions to survive comparison with the whole canon, and is willing to follow that method into challenging, law-positive territory.

## Short Book Description

This is not a novelty codebook or a list of possible associations. Every definition must answer to Scripture.

Across 64 biblical books and nearly 700 chapters, the method develops over 160 symbols and internally defined words. Mountains, seas, trees, garments, lamps, winds, stars, and beasts begin speaking across the canon. Familiar words—gospel, faith, love, grace, worship, righteousness, and works—recover a force tradition and translation can soften.

The payoffs are concrete. Jesus’ parables become an exam whose answers were already written in the Law and the Prophets. Jonah’s entire journey unfolds as a lived similitude of betrayal, judgment, resurrection, and the turn to the nations. The gospel Jesus preached before the cross emerges as the royal proclamation of God’s reign, calling for allegiance rather than passive agreement. The name, seal, coin, garment, lamp, oil, and wedding feast converge into one covenant grammar.

Some conclusions confirm familiar teaching; others sharpen it or challenge it. The book marks the difference, names ancient witnesses where they exist, and leaves every citation open for checking. Its posture is confident and law-positive: grace, faith, love, and the gospel produce obedience rather than abolish God’s commands.

If you are ready to move beyond secondhand meanings, bring a Bible. Learn the language. Test every claim. Then use the method to keep discovering long after the final chapter.

## Long Book Description

You already know the Bible speaks in symbols. The Lamb is Messiah. Seed can be the word. A kingdom may rise like a mountain. What most readers never receive is the rest of that language—or a reliable way to verify it without borrowing someone else’s dictionary.

*MEAT — The Bible’s Symbolic Language* makes a bold but testable claim: Scripture supplies its own vocabulary, and its clearest passages can teach readers how to recover it. Daniel Larimer’s method is concrete and repeatable. Gather every occurrence of a word. Let plain uses define difficult ones. Compare parallel accounts. Require two or three witnesses. Test apparent exceptions. Discard any definition that breaks under the weight of the whole canon. The goal is not a private code, but an interpretation that remains answerable to the biblical text.

The parable of the sower demonstrates the method before the book asks for trust. Isaiah identifies the seed with the word that goes out from God. Jeremiah places fallow ground and thorns in the heart. Genesis, Hosea, and Isaiah establish the curse-crop and connect it with divided allegiance, riches, and oppression. Earlier covenant scenes establish the devouring birds. Only after that Old Testament case is assembled does Jesus’ explanation supply the grading key. His parables prove to be an exam whose vocabulary had already been taught in the Law and the Prophets.

From that foundation, the book traces over 160 symbols and internally defined words through 64 of the Bible’s 66 books and nearly 700 chapters. Mountains become kingdoms, seas become peoples, trees become rooted lives and nations, garments become righteous acts, and names and seals mark covenant identity. Yet the inquiry reaches beyond visible images. Gospel, faith, belief, love, grace, mercy, liberty, worship, righteousness, justification, sanctification, and works of the law are gathered and tested by the same standard.

Several extended studies reveal what this connected vocabulary can do. Jonah’s full journey becomes a lived similitude of betrayal, judgment, resurrection, and the gospel’s turn to the nations. The gospel Jesus preached before the cross recovers its own content: the royal proclamation of God’s reign, announced with covenant terms and answered through allegiance. The name, seal, coin, garment, lamp, oil, and wedding feast resolve into a single covenant grammar.

The same language opens prophecy without beginning from headlines or sensational identities. Daniel and Revelation are read through definitions established elsewhere: beasts rise from the sea of nations, mountains become kingdoms, Babylon’s fall replays the Exodus, and mark and seal become rival covenant enrollments. Hebrew and Greek word families, translation shifts, parallel poetry, and Hebrew consonants add witnesses where English can hide the relationship.

This is not a neutral symbol dictionary, a prophecy chart, or a light devotional. It is a confident, law-positive argument that familiar doctrine must survive comparison with the whole text. Grace, faith, love, discipleship, and the gospel produce obedience rather than abolish God’s commands. Some conclusions agree with common teaching; some refine it; others openly challenge it. The book grades those differences, names ancient witnesses where they exist, reports misses beside hits, and gives readers the citations needed to disagree on purpose rather than by habit.

A detailed glossary gathers the recovered vocabulary into a lasting study tool, while the Scripture index makes further testing practical. But the book’s deeper promise is a habit of reading: encounter a word in one passage and begin hearing its witnesses from across the canon.

If you have sensed that familiar verses carry more than you were taught—and you want evidence rather than hype—bring a Bible. *MEAT* will teach you how to gather the witnesses, test the meaning, and let Scripture finish its own sentences.
