When the religious leaders demanded a sign, Jesus refused them every sign but one — and it was a strange one to hand a skeptic: a short story about a runaway prophet. Read on the surface, it has fueled centuries of argument about how to count three days and three nights. Read through the symbols we have been building, it opens into something far larger — a detailed map of His betrayal, arrest, interrogation, death, and resurrection, and of the gospel’s turn to the nations.

[quote.scripture, Matthew 12:39-40]
____
…there shall no sign be given to it, but the sign of the prophet Jonas: For as Jonas was three days and three nights in the *whale’s belly*; so shall the Son of man be three days and three nights in the *heart of the earth*.
____

Two phrases hold the key — __“the heart of the earth,”__ and, in Jonah’s own account, __“the heart of the seas.”__

Almost everyone reads __“the heart of the earth”__ as the grave. But in the world Jesus spoke into, __“the heart of the earth”__ — or __“the navel of the earth”__ — was a known name for one particular place: _Jerusalem_. Ezekiel speaks of the people __“that dwell in the navel of the land”__ (Ezekiel 38:12 — Hebrew _tabbur ha’aretz_; the KJV renders it __“the midst of the land”__). Philo of Alexandria calls Jerusalem the __“heart of the whole earth.”__ The Sibylline Oracles call her __“the navel and heart of the earth.”__ Later Greek copies of Ezekiel even change __“navel”__ to __“heart”__ where Jerusalem is in view. So __“the Son of man… three days and three nights in the heart of the earth”__ is, first of all, three days and three nights in _Jerusalem_ — taken into the city under arrest, and out of it again on the third day.

Now set Jonah’s side of the sign beside it, and the symbols from the last chapter light up. Jonah is cast into the sea — and we have seen that the sea is the peoples, the nations:

[quote.scripture, Jonah 1:12]
____
…cast me forth into the *sea*; so shall the *sea* be calm unto you: for I know that for my sake this great tempest is upon you.
____

[quote.scripture, Jonah 1:15]
____
So they took up Jonah, and cast him forth into the *sea*: and the *sea ceased from her raging*.
____

A man hurled into the raging sea, and the raging sea grows calm — the very picture from the last chapter, where the One with authority over the nations says to the sea, __“Peace, be still”__ (Mark 4:39). And from inside, Jonah names where he has gone:

[quote.scripture, Jonah 2:3]
____
For thou hadst cast me into the deep, in the *midst of the seas* [Hebrew: the *heart of the seas*]; and the *floods* compassed me about: all thy *billows and thy waves* passed over me.
____

__“The heart of the seas”__ — so the Hebrew reads — mirrors __“the heart of the earth.”__ Jonah goes down into the heart of the _seas_, the gentile nations — the **floods**footnote:flood[_Flood_ — an invading army; see the link:/books/symbolic-language/sea-and-waters/[Sea & Waters] chapter.] that compass him about — on his way to Nineveh; the Son of Man goes into the heart of the _earth_, Jerusalem. Two centers, one sign.

And the great fish that swallows him out of the sea is no aquarium specimen. The King James calls it a __“whale,”__ but that is an interpreter’s guess. Jonah’s Hebrew says only a great fish (_dag gadol_); the Greek of Matthew calls it a _kētos_ — a great sea-creature, a sea-monster. And Scripture says plainly what such a monster is — not death, but a _devouring kingdom_. Babylon __“hath swallowed me up like a dragon, he hath filled his belly with my delicates, he hath cast me out”__ (Jeremiah 51:34); Pharaoh is __“the great dragon”__ (Ezekiel 29:3), and Egypt __“a whale in the seas”__ (Ezekiel 32:2). The swallowing sea-monster is a kingdom that devours — and, as we shall see, is forced to give back what it has swallowed.

Which kingdom swallowed the Messiah? Two of them, working as one. The city is named outright: __“the great city, which spiritually is called Sodom and Egypt, where also our Lord was crucified”__ (Revelation 11:8) — Jerusalem. Jerusalem is spiritually Egypt, and Egypt is the dragon; she is the harlot __“that killest the prophets”__ (Matthew 23:37), in whom __“was found the blood of prophets”__ (Revelation 18:24). But she did not act alone. She rode the beast — Rome, the _iron_ legs of Nebuchadnezzar’s image (Daniel 2:40, from the Mountain chapter), the latest face of the world-empire that image portrays. Offered His release, she chose her mount: __“We have no king but Caesar”__ (John 19:15). And Scripture sets the two side by side at the swallowing: __“both Herod, and Pontius Pilate, with the Gentiles, and the people of Israel, were gathered together”__ (Acts 4:27) — the harlot and the beast, Jerusalem and Rome. (Who the harlot is, and the beast she rides, are each their own study; here it is enough to see the dragon that swallowed Him.)

Her belly is the heart of the earth — Jerusalem — and there they held Him three days and three nights and put Him to death. From inside it Jonah cries:

[quote.scripture, Jonah 2:2]
____
…out of the *belly of hell* [Hebrew: _Sheol_] cried I, and thou heardest my voice.
____

[quote.scripture, Jonah 2:6]
____
I went down to the *bottoms of the mountains*; the earth with her bars was about me for ever: yet hast thou *brought up my life from corruption*, O LORD my God.
____

Notice what Jonah does and does not say. He is not a corpse for three days; he is alive in the belly, crying out — as good as dead, yet praying (Jonah 2:1, 7). His __“belly of Sheol”__ is the cry of a man in the jaws of death, brought down even to __“the bottoms of the mountains,”__ beneath the foundations of the kingdoms — and then __“brought up… from corruption,”__ the very word Peter presses at Pentecost: __“thou wilt not… suffer thine Holy One to see corruption”__ (Psalm 16:10; Acts 2:27). The death the city dealt Him was real. But a dragon does not keep what it devours: as God says of Babylon the Great — for Jeremiah’s Babylon is the same that Revelation 18 still calls to fall — __“I will bring forth out of his mouth that which he hath swallowed up”__ (Jeremiah 51:44) — and Jonah was cast up alive on dry land. The monster was made to give Him back. The sign is a resurrection.

Then comes the part a skeptic would never expect. Delivered up out of death, Jonah goes to the great gentile city — and it turns to God:

[quote.scripture, Jonah 3:5]
____
So the people of *Nineveh* believed God, and proclaimed a fast, and put on sackcloth, from the greatest of them even to the least of them.
____

[quote.scripture, Matthew 12:41]
____
The men of *Nineveh* shall *rise in judgment* with this generation, and shall *condemn it*: because they repented at the preaching of Jonas; and, behold, a greater than Jonas is here.
____

Nineveh is a city of the sea — a great mass of gentile peoples — and at the preaching of a man brought back from the dead, the sea turns. The order is fixed and cannot be reversed: the prophet must first be swallowed and disgorged, dead and raised, and only then do the nations repent. So the sign of Jonah is not only the Passion; it is the gospel going out to the nations after the resurrection.

And see what Jesus says these gentiles will then do. __“The men of Nineveh shall rise in judgment with this generation, and shall condemn it.”__ __“Shall rise”__ is no figure of speech: the Greek is _anastēsontai_, the resurrection word — the very form Paul uses of __“the dead in Christ”__ who __“shall rise”__ (1 Thessalonians 4:16). The Ninevites are themselves raised, and in rising they condemn __“this generation”__ — the generation that demanded a sign and refused the One who gave it. The reversal could not be sharper: pagan Nineveh repented at a reluctant prophet who carried no resurrection; this generation had the risen Christ in person, and would not. So the saved out of the nations rise to judge the very people who handed Him to the dragon — for __“the saints shall judge the world”__ (1 Corinthians 6:2). __“A greater than Jonas is here.”__

Hold the whole story up against the Passion and the correspondence is far too detailed to be accidental. The Sign of Jonah is a symbolic prophecy, scene for scene:

.The Sign of Jonah — the Passion, scene by scene
[.parallels-table, frame=topbot, grid=rows, cols="1,1", options="header"]
|===
| Jonah | Jesus

| Jonah flees God | Judas leaves Jesus
| Jonah pays the fare | Judas is paid thirty pieces of silver
| Jonah goes “down” to Joppa and into the ship | Jesus goes down to Jerusalem (from the Mount of Olives)
| Jonah falls into a deep sleep | Disciples fall asleep three times in Gethsemane
| Captain wakes Jonah: “What meanest thou, sleeper? Arise!” | Jesus wakes the disciples: “Could ye not watch one hour?”
| Sailors question Jonah about his identity and the cause of the storm | High priest and Pilate question Jesus about his identity and the cause of the trouble
| Sailors cry, “we are innocent of this man’s blood” | Pilate washes his hands: “I am innocent of the blood of this just person”
| Sailors cast lots to find the guilty one | Soldiers cast lots for Jesus’ garments
| Weeds wrapped about Jonah’s head | Crown of thorns wrapped about Jesus’ head
| Great storm, the sea roaring in commotion | The crowd roaring in commotion through the trial and crucifixion
| Jonah says, “Take me up, and cast me forth” | Jesus voluntarily lays down his life
| Jonah hurled into the sea — the storm stops | Jesus dies — earthquake, darkness, the veil torn
| Three days and three nights in the fish | Three days and three nights in the heart of the earth
| Jonah cast up alive onto dry land | Jesus rises alive from the tomb
| Jonah goes on to Nineveh | Jesus walks the road to Emmaus, then sends the gospel to the nations
|===

Now notice what the symbols have quietly done to the old argument. Because __“the heart of the earth”__ is _Jerusalem_ — not the tomb — the __“three days and three nights”__ are the days the Son of Man spent in the city: taken in under arrest on the night of His betrayal, and out of it again on the third day, when two disciples walk __“that same day”__ to Emmaus, saying __“to day is the third day since these things were done”__ (Luke 24:13, 21). The sign measures His time in the heart of the earth, and the count comes out exactly — without the calendar gymnastics the surface reading forces. (The full calendar case — Passover, First Fruits, and the counting of the days — is laid out in _Time Tested Tradition_; here the point is only that the symbol, rightly read, dissolves the supposed contradiction.)

Recall the claim from the Introduction: the very life stories of real people serve a double duty, standing also as parables and types (1 Corinthians 10:11). Here it is in full. A reluctant prophet’s biography — his flight, his fare, his sleep, the lots, the storm, the sea, the fish, the three days, the city that repents — turns out to be a frame-by-frame prophecy of the Messiah’s betrayal, arrest, interrogation, death, resurrection, and mission to the nations. Jesus called it __“the sign of the prophet Jonah,”__ and offered no other, because this one already held the whole gospel — at least as that word is commonly understood. You only had to know how to read it. And __“gospel”__ itself, it turns out, is one more symbol: what it truly means — and how much larger it is than the common sense allows — is where we turn next, reading it, like all the rest, through the Bible’s symbolic language.
