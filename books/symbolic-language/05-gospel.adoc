Of all the words this book will examine, none arrives more freighted than __“gospel.”__ Say it and a meaning leaps up already assembled: the message about Jesus — that He died for our sins so that we might be forgiven and reconciled to God. That message is true and precious. But notice what has just happened — we supplied the definition before consulting the text, the very habit this book exists to break. So we will do with __“gospel”__ what we have done with mountain and sea: set our assumptions down, gather every place the word appears, and let Scripture say what it means.

And Scripture is not vague about it. When Jesus preached the gospel, it was never the gospel in the abstract; it carried a name every time:

[quote.scripture, Matthew 4:23]
____
And Jesus went about all Galilee, teaching in their synagogues, and preaching the *gospel of the kingdom*, and healing all manner of sickness and all manner of disease among the people.
____

[quote.scripture, Matthew 9:35]
____
And Jesus went about all the cities and villages, teaching in their synagogues, and preaching the *gospel of the kingdom*, and healing every sickness and every disease among the people.
____

The good news has a content, and the content is a **kingdom**footnote:kingdom[_Kingdom_ — a king’s realm, pictured as the _mountain_; see the link:/books/symbolic-language/mountain/[Mountain] chapter.]. The gospel, then, is the announcement that a kingdom — God’s — has come. Mark records the herald-cry in full:

[quote.scripture, Mark 1:14-15]
____
Now after that John was put in prison, Jesus came into Galilee, preaching the *gospel of the kingdom of God*, And saying, The time is fulfilled, and the *kingdom of God is at hand*: *repent ye, and believe the gospel*.
____

Hear what the gospel asks for. Not __“accept this fact,”__ but __“repent ye, and believe”__ — turn, and trust. It is a king’s proclamation, and the only fitting response to a king’s proclamation is allegiance. This is why the word for preaching it is the herald’s word: a _kerux_ did not debate in the market; he stood in the square and announced the decree of the one who sent him. The gospel is that decree — the King has come; submit and live.

Isaiah had cried it seven centuries before, and named its single sentence:

[quote.scripture, Isaiah 52:7]
____
How beautiful upon the *mountains* are the feet of him that *bringeth good tidings*, that publisheth peace; that bringeth good tidings of good, that publisheth salvation; that saith unto Zion, *Thy God reigneth*!
____

There is the whole of it compressed to three words: __“Thy God reigneth.”__ The __“good tidings”__ is the very word — in Greek, _euangelizo_, the gospel-verb — and Paul reaches back and quotes this verse as the gospel itself: __“how beautiful are the feet of them that preach the gospel of peace”__ (Romans 10:15). And mark where the herald stands — __“upon the mountains.”__ The announcement that God reigns is published over the kingdoms of the earth.

Now the hinge the common definition hides. Jesus preached this gospel — the whole of it — _before_ He died:

[quote.scripture, Matthew 4:17]
____
From that time Jesus began to preach, and to say, Repent: for the *kingdom of heaven is at hand*.
____

From the opening of His ministry, years before the cross, He was already preaching the gospel of the kingdom (Matthew 4:23). If the gospel were the message _about_ His death and resurrection, He could not have preached it for three years while both were still ahead of Him. What He preached was the arrival of God’s reign and the way of life under it — the kingdom and its terms. The gospel is the message Jesus _taught_, not merely the message _about_ Jesus.

And it was not new even to Him. The book of Hebrews drops a line most readers pass over: the gospel was preached long before, to the generation in the wilderness —

[quote.scripture, Hebrews 4:2]
____
For unto us was the *gospel preached*, as well as unto them: but the word preached did not profit them, not being mixed with *faith* in them that heard it.
____

__“Unto them”__ is Israel at Sinai. The same gospel-word describes what was proclaimed there. And what was proclaimed there? A kingdom:

[quote.scripture, Exodus 19:6]
____
And ye shall be unto me a *kingdom of priests*, and an *holy nation*.
____

God, having brought a people up out of Egypt, declares His reign over them and calls them to enter it as His kingdom. That is the gospel of the kingdom — preached at Sinai. And earlier still, to Abraham:

[quote.scripture, Galatians 3:8]
____
And the scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the heathen through faith, *preached before the gospel* unto Abraham, saying, In thee shall *all nations be blessed*.
____

__“Preached before the gospel”__ is a single Greek word, _proeuangelizo_ — _pre-gospeled_. Abraham received the gospel, and its reach — _all nations_ — is the same reach it has from first to last. One gospel runs the length of Scripture: Abraham, Sinai, Jesus, the apostles. Revelation even gives it its proper adjective:

[quote.scripture, Revelation 14:6]
____
And I saw another angel fly in the midst of heaven, having the *everlasting gospel* to preach unto them that dwell on the earth, and to every nation, and kindred, and tongue, and people.
____

_Everlasting._ A thing cannot be everlasting and also have begun at the cross. The gospel is as old as God’s purpose to reign over a people who are His.

If the gospel announces a King’s reign, it necessarily carries terms, for a king reigns by covenant. Here the threads draw together: the gospel of the kingdom is the _covenant_ of the kingdom. And the covenant, Scripture is careful to say, was never scrapped and replaced — it was _renewed_. The promise everyone calls the __“new covenant”__ rests on a Hebrew word, _chadash_, whose root means renew, repair, rebuild:

[quote.scripture, Psalm 51:10]
____
Create in me a clean heart, O God; and *renew* a right spirit within me.
____

[quote.scripture, 2 Chronicles 24:4]
____
And it came to pass after this, that Joash was minded to *repair* the house of the LORD.
____

David does not ask for a spirit he never had; Joash does not build a different temple. _Chadash_ restores what already stands. So the __“new”__ covenant is the _same_ covenant, renewed — and Jeremiah, who gave the promise, says precisely what changes and what does not:

[quote.scripture, Jeremiah 31:31-33]
____
Behold, the days come, saith the LORD, that I will make a *new* covenant with the house of Israel… Not according to the covenant that I made with their fathers… *which my covenant they brake*… I will put my *law* in their inward parts, and *write it in their hearts*; and will be their God, and they shall be my people.
____

The fault lay not with the covenant but with the people — __“my covenant they brake.”__ The _law_ is the same law; the terms are the same terms. What changes is the _location_ — from tablets of stone to the inward parts, from a code outside us to a writing within. Same covenant, new surface. This is why Jesus could insist, in the very sermon where He pressed the law to its depths:

[quote.scripture, Matthew 5:17]
____
Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets: I am not come to destroy, but to *fulfil*.
____

Not destroy — _fulfil_. And __“fulfil”__ is the word _plēroō_: to _fill full_, to fill to the brim — the exact opposite of __“destroy”__ (_kataluō_, to tear down). Jesus did not come to abolish the law, nor to complete it and set it aside, but to _fill it full_ — to preach it in its fullness, packing back the weight that the surface reading had drained away. That is precisely what He does next: __“Ye have heard that it was said… but I say unto you,”__ pressing past the act to the heart behind it (Matthew 5:21-22) — filling each commandment full, writing it where Jeremiah said it would be written, on the inward parts. The Sermon on the Mount is the renewed covenant in motion — the same Torah, filled full and taught from the inside. _This_ is the message Jesus taught: not a new religion about Himself, but the ancient covenant of the kingdom, renewed and written on the heart.

So the gospel is no abolition of the law of Moses but its establishment — __“we establish the law,”__ as Paul says (Romans 3:31). The gospel of the kingdom _is_ the law of that kingdom: the Torah preached in the wilderness, the constitution of God’s reign. It is the very law the prophets foresaw going forth from Zion when the King at last reigns from His mountain:

[quote.scripture, Isaiah 2:3]
____
…Come ye, and let us go up to the *mountain of the LORD*, to the house of the God of Jacob; and he will teach us of his ways, and we will walk in his paths: for *out of Zion shall go forth the law*, and the word of the LORD from Jerusalem.
____

One law, from first to last: preached in the wilderness, written now upon the heart, and one day issuing from Zion over all the earth (Micah 4:2 says the same). To preach the gospel of the kingdom, then, is to publish the law of the King — the terms of life under His reign, the same at Sinai, in the Sermon on the Mount, and from Zion when He comes.

Where, then, is the cross in all this? Exactly where Scripture sets it — as the _mechanism_ that secures and seals the kingdom, not as the _content_ of the gospel:

[quote.scripture, John 19:30]
____
When Jesus therefore had received the vinegar, he said, *It is finished*: and he bowed his head, and gave up the ghost.
____

__“It is finished”__ completes the work that ratifies the covenant in blood. The cross is indispensable — it is _how_ the kingdom is purchased and the renewed covenant sealed. But it is the engine beneath the announcement, not the announcement itself. The proof is plain: Jesus preached the whole gospel of the kingdom for years before Calvary. The modern gospel — __“Christ died for your sins; believe and be saved”__ — is gloriously true; but it is true only if __“believe”__ is read as Scripture means it — not bare mental assent, but the trusting obedience the wilderness generation lacked, the faith without which the gospel __“did not profit them”__ (Hebrews 4:2). Taken so, it remains a _part_ standing in for the _whole_: it names the engine and forgets the kingdom it drives. (__“Believe”__ is its own symbol, with its own study to come.)

But here a fair objection rises: does not Paul himself _define_ the gospel as the death and resurrection? He does say it plainly:

[quote.scripture, 1 Corinthians 15:3-4]
____
For I delivered unto you first of all that which I also received, how that *Christ died for our sins according to the scriptures*; And that he was buried, and that he *rose again the third day according to the scriptures*.
____

Read closely, this confirms rather than overturns what we have seen. Twice Paul anchors it __“according to the scriptures”__ — the death and resurrection _fulfill_ what the prophets had already announced; they are how the long-promised kingdom is secured, not a new message that cancels it. And here he is expressly defending the resurrection (the burden of all of 1 Corinthians 15), summarizing the hinge of the gospel, not redrawing its whole. Where Paul opens a letter by stating his gospel at length, the kingdom is unmistakable:

[quote.scripture, Romans 1:1-4]
____
Paul… separated unto the *gospel of God*, (Which he had *promised afore by his prophets in the holy scriptures*,) Concerning his Son Jesus Christ our Lord, which was made of the *seed of David* according to the flesh; And *declared to be the Son of God with power*… by the *resurrection from the dead*.
____

Every clause is kingdom language. The gospel was __“promised afore by his prophets”__ — the same ancient announcement, not a novelty. Its subject is the __“seed of David”__ — the rightful heir to the throne. And the resurrection __“declared”__ Him __“the Son of God with power”__ — it is the _enthronement_ of the King. Paul’s gospel is that the Son of David has been raised and installed as Lord; the cross and empty tomb are how He took the throne. That is the gospel of the kingdom, told from the side of the King’s accession. It is also why Paul can stake everything on there being only _one_ gospel — __“though we, or an angel from heaven, preach any other gospel unto you… let him be accursed”__ (Galatians 1:8) — the very gospel, he reminds us, that was __“preached before… unto Abraham”__ (Galatians 3:8). He is not trading Jesus’ message in Galilee for a different one; he is defending the same one, now that its mechanism is accomplished. The cross is indispensable — it secures and inaugurates the kingdom — but it is the turning point _within_ the gospel, not the whole of its content.

So the word we thought we owned opens up like all the rest. The mountain is the kingdom; the gospel is the herald’s cry that the kingdom has come — __“Thy God reigneth”__ — published upon the mountains and carried to every nation: the same good news Abraham heard, that thundered at Sinai, that Jesus taught upon the mount, and that an angel still proclaims as everlasting. Read it as a private transaction and it shrinks to a fraction of itself. Read it as Scripture defines it — the arrival of God’s reign and the renewal of His covenant in the heart — and the turning of the nations that closed the last chapter stands revealed for what it is: not the spread of information about a man, but the kingdom of God going forth to fill the earth.
