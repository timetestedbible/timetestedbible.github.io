A single word can put a man __“in danger of hell fire”__:

[quote.scripture, Matthew 5:22]
____
…whosoever shall say, Thou *fool*, shall be in danger of *hell fire*.
____

If calling someone a fool is that grave, the word is worth knowing — and it has drifted far from its meaning in Scripture.
To us a fool is someone slow or silly.
In the Bible a fool is something else entirely, and it has nothing to do with intelligence.

[discrete]
=== Hearing and Doing

The fool is marked first by what he says in his heart:

[quote.scripture, Psalms 14:1]
____
The *fool* hath said in his heart, *There is no God*.
They are corrupt, they have done abominable works…
____

Not “there is no God” as a theory, but as a way of living — as though no one were watching and nothing were owed.
From there the rest follows: __“fools despise sym:sym-wisdom[wisdom] and sym:sym-instruction[instruction]”__ (Proverbs 1:7); __“the sym:sym-way[way] of a fool is right in his own eyes”__ (Proverbs 12:15) — his own eyes again, his own measure of right.
And the Lord draws the line where you would least expect it: not between those who hear and those who don’t, but between two men who hear the very same words:

[quote.scripture, Matthew 7:24-26]
____
Whosoever heareth these sayings of mine, and *doeth them*, I will liken him unto a *wise* man, which built his house upon a sym:sym-rock[rock]… And every one that heareth these sayings of mine, and *doeth them not*, shall be likened unto a *foolish* man, which built his house upon the sym:sym-sand[sand].
____

Both hear.
One does, one does not.
That is the whole difference between the wise and the fool.
So wisdom is not cleverness but obedience: __“a good sym:sym-understanding[understanding] have all they that do his commandments”__ (Psalms 111:10); __“keep therefore and do them; for this is your wisdom”__ (Deuteronomy 4:6).
The fool is not the dull man; he is the disobedient one, however clever.

[discrete]
=== The Ten Virgins

Hold that, and the parable of the ten virgins opens, for it is built of symbols already defined.
Ten virgins go to meet the bridegroom; and all ten are virgins indeed — the Greek is _parthenos_, chaste maidens, not merely young women: the very picture of the faithful bride, __“espoused… to one husband”__ (2 Corinthians 11:2), and the opposite of the sym:sym-harlot[harlot] who runs after many.
None of them is a harlot; all are pure, all carry lamps, all wait for the Bridegroom — and still five are shut out.
For here it is not the harlot turned away, but the virgin; and one thing divides them:

[quote.scripture, Matthew 25:3-4]
____
They that were *foolish* took their sym:sym-lamp[lamps], and *took no oil* with them: But the *wise* took *oil* in their vessels with their lamps.
____

They are not careless about a detail; they are foolish _women_ — the nature comes first, the empty lamp only reveals it.
When the cry comes at midnight their lamps are going out; they have no light.
They beg the wise for oil, are refused, and are sent off to buy it — and while they are gone, the door is shut.

One verse decodes the scene: __“the commandment is a sym:sym-lamp[lamp]; and the law is sym:sym-light[light]”__ (Proverbs 6:23).
The lamp is the commandment — and all ten carry it, just as both builders heard the very same sayings.
The light is the law _lived_, link:/books/symbolic-language/light-and-darkness/[as the next chapter will show].footnote:fn16lawlived[Derived in the link:/books/symbolic-language/light-and-darkness/[Light and Darkness] chapter.]
Between the lamp and the light stands the oil: burn it, and the commandment in hand becomes a life that shines; lack it, and the lamp holds the commandment still, but gives no light.

[discrete]
=== What Is the Oil?

Everything turns on the oil, so everything turns on knowing what it is.
Asked how the oil is commonly explained, the answers rank like this:

.How the “oil” is usually read (ten virgins)
[.prevalence-table%unbreakable, frame=topbot, grid=none, cols="^2,6,^2", options="header"]
|===
| Rank | Common interpretation | Est. %

| 1 | The Holy Spirit | 65%
| 2 | Saving faith | 12%
| 3 | Grace | 10%
| 4 | Good works, or readiness | 8%
| 5 | The word, or a relationship with Christ | 5%
|===

Nearly all of it gathers to one answer — the Holy Spirit — and the parable refuses it.
The wise send the foolish to __“buy”__ oil (Matthew 25:9); but the Spirit is a gift that cannot be bought — __“Thy money perish with thee, because thou hast thought that the gift of God may be purchased with money”__ (Acts 8:20).
Nor can it be grace or faith, gifts freely given, for this oil must be gathered beforehand, kept in one's own vessel, and cannot be lent to another.

The parable's word is exact, for Scripture commands just such a purchase: __“Buy the sym:sym-truth[truth], and sell it not; also sym:sym-wisdom[wisdom], and sym:sym-instruction[instruction], and sym:sym-understanding[understanding]”__ (Proverbs 23:23).
And these are not four loose virtues; each is a symbol Scripture fixes to one thing — the law kept.
The sym:sym-truth[truth] is the law itself — __“thy law is the truth”__ (Psalm 119:142); _wisdom_ and _understanding_ are the doing of it (Psalm 111:10) — __“the sym:sym-fear[fear] of the Lord, that is wisdom; and to depart from evil is understanding”__ (Job 28:28); and _instruction_ is its discipline, which only fools despise (Proverbs 1:7).
To buy the truth, then, is to buy and keep the law.
And the price is never silver — __“buy… without money and without price”__ (Isaiah 55:1) — but a listening, obeying ear: __“hearken diligently unto me, and eat ye that which is good… Incline your ear, and come unto me: hear, and your soul shall live; and I will make an everlasting covenant with you”__ (Isaiah 55:2-3).
What it costs is a whole life of hearing and doing; what it buys is the covenant itself, and life; and what is so bought is never _sold_, never passed to another hand.
That is the oil: the law bought and kept, the tried doing of the truth, stored in one's own vessel — which is why the foolish, sent to buy it at the last hour, find the door already shut.

Scripture says what oil is.
It is the pressed fruit of the olive — __“pure oil olive beaten for the light, to cause the sym:sym-lamp[lamp] to burn always”__ (Exodus 27:20) — and the olive tree is Israel, the covenant people: __“The LORD called thy name, *A green* sym:sym-olive[olive tree]”__ (Jeremiah 11:16).
Oil is fruit crushed and tested until it yields; it is obedience proven under pressure and stored up over a life.
Burned, it is what turns the lamp to light — the commandment held becomes the law lived, obedience made visible, the symbol of the next chapter.
The foolish had the lamp — the profession, the commandment in hand — but no oil, no store of works; so when the test came, there was no light.
And no one can lend it: your neighbor’s obedience will not burn for you.

The place where the Lord Himself was pressed even bears the name: Gethsemane, the _oil press_.
And it sits on the Mount of Olives (Luke 22:39) — by these symbols the mount, the nation, of olives: Israel itself.
On the olive-nation's own hill stood the press, and there the Lord was crushed under the weight of death — His sweat __“as it were great drops of blood”__ (Luke 22:44) — and gave up the oil of a perfect obedience: __“not my will, but thine, be done”__ (Luke 22:42).
The foolish are turned away with the worst words in Scripture: __“I sym:sym-knowing[know] you not”__ (Matthew 25:12) — the same verdict as __“I never knew you”__ spoken over those who worked lawlessness.

So the foolish virgins were not undone by a forgotten errand; they were foolish from the first — hearers who never became doers, lamps that never held oil.
And the wise were wise the way the wise man built on rock: they heard, and they did, and when the Bridegroom came their light was burning.
Between the fool and the wise there is finally one question — the question this whole book keeps asking: not what you heard, but whether you did it.
