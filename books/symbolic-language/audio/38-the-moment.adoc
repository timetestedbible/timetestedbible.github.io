MEAT The Bible's Symbolic Language. [beat] Chapter Thirty-Eight: The Moment.

[long pause]
Babylon fell in the middle of a boast.
__“I sit a queen, and am no widow, and shall see no sorrow”__ — and the verse that supplies her exact words, seven centuries earlier, supplies the timing of her answer too:

[quote.scripture, Isaiah 47:9]
____
But these two things shall come to thee *in a moment* in one day, the loss of children, and widowhood: they shall come upon thee in their perfection…
____

[pause]

__“In a moment.”__
Not a slow decline, not a siege that could be watched approaching for years — a moment, and both children and husband are gone.
This book has followed one Hebrew word into a dozen chapters now; this one is smaller and sharper than any of them, and once you have it, an entire vocabulary of sudden things in Scripture turns out to be one thing.

[long pause]

The argument now turns to The Wink of an Eye.

[long pause]

The Hebrew behind __“moment”__ is _rega_, and its root meaning is exact: to wink, to blink — the very motion of an eyelid.
It is the shortest interval Scripture has a word for, and it never describes a process.

God tells Moses what would happen if He walked with a stiff-necked people even one stage of the journey: __“I will come up into the midst of thee in a moment, and consume thee”__.
When Korah’s company rose up against Moses, God did not say _I will judge them in time_; He said, __“Separate yourselves from among this congregation, that I may consume them in a moment”__ — and when the plague broke out among the people themselves, the same word is used again: __“that I may consume them *as in a moment*”__.
Job draws the general rule from such scenes: __“In a moment shall they die, and the people shall be troubled at midnight, and pass away: and the mighty shall be taken away *without hand*”__ — no visible weapon, no army, no hand at all; the moment does the work alone.

Scripture makes sure you cannot mistake this for a merely human turn of phrase.
The Greek New Testament reaches for the identical image, unprompted, to describe the resurrection:

[quote.scripture, 1 Corinthians 15:52]
____
In a *moment*, in the *twinkling of an eye*, at the last trump: for the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised incorruptible…
____

[pause]

__“Moment”__ here is _atomos_ — literally an _uncut_ thing, an atom of time, indivisible — paired with __“the twinkling of an eye,”__ the eye’s own glance.
Two languages, centuries apart, reach for the same picture: the blink of an eye is Scripture’s unit for _instantaneous_, whether the news is a resurrection or a ruin.
The moment is not vague; it is the shortest thing a human body can do.

[long pause]

That brings us to Suddenly.

[long pause]

Beside _rega_ stands its constant companion, _pithom_ — __“suddenly”__ — and Strong’s traces it back to the same family of roots: a wink, an unguarded instant, __“used only adverbially, quickly or unexpectedly.”__
The two words travel together so often that they read as one idea wearing two names.
Watch them arrive in the same verse over the ruin of a land: __“suddenly are my tents spoiled, and my curtains *in a moment*”__.
And watch the word do its full work on Babylon herself:

[quote.scripture, Jeremiah 51:8]
____
*Babylon is suddenly fallen* and destroyed: howl for her; take balm for her pain, if so be she may be healed.
____

[pause]

No warning shot, no visible siege in the sentence — she is simply, suddenly, fallen.
Revelation counts the same fall by the clock rather than the eye, and repeats the number until it cannot be missed: __“for in *one hour* is thy judgment come”__; __“for in *one hour* so great riches is come to nought”__ (18:17); __“for in *one hour* is she made desolate”__ (18:19).
Three times in one chapter, the mourners themselves keep saying the word — because the thing that stuns them most is not that Babylon fell, but how fast.

The same suddenness that ends the wicked announces the LORD Himself: __“the Lord, whom ye seek, shall *suddenly* come to his temple”__ — the true King’s arrival keeps the same timing as the false queen’s fall.
Neither is a process you can watch coming.
Both are a wink.

[long pause]

The next step is No Remedy in the Wink.

[long pause]

Scripture is careful to note what the moment does not allow: appeal, delay, or repair.

Let's read:

[quote.scripture, Proverbs 29:1]
____
He, that being often reproved hardeneth his neck, shall *suddenly* be destroyed, and that *without remedy*.
____

[pause]

The warnings can run for years; the destruction, when it lands, allows none.
Paul names the same collapse waiting behind the world’s most confident sentence:

[quote.scripture, 1 Thessalonians 5:3]
____
For when they shall say, Peace *and safety*; then *sudden destruction* cometh upon them, as *travail upon a woman with child*; and they shall not escape.
____

[pause]

The image is deliberately double-edged, and worth holding next to Babylon’s own boast of childlessness: travail is sudden, unstoppable once it starts, and ends in a birth the mother cannot delay — but here it ends the one who is unprepared, __“as a thief in the night”__.
Jesus warns His own not to be caught the same way — __“lest… that day come upon you *unawares*”__, the Greek _aiphnidios_, the exact cousin of the __“suddenly”__ that fells Babylon.
The moment does not ask whether you are ready.

[long pause]

With that in place, consider The Moment That Blesses.

[long pause]

The shortness of the word is not only a threat, and Scripture will not let the reader forget it.
The same _rega_ that consumes Korah’s company also measures God’s own anger toward His covenant people — and measures it as mercy:

[quote.scripture, Isaiah 54:7-8]
____
For a *small moment* have I forsaken thee; but with great mercies will I *gather thee*.
In a little wrath I hid my face from thee *for a moment*; but with *everlasting kindness* will I have mercy on thee…
____

[pause]

The wrath is a moment; the kindness is everlasting — the same asymmetry David sings: __“his anger endureth but a *moment*; in his favour is life: weeping may endure for a night, but joy cometh in the morning”__.
And Paul, under the weight of real suffering, reaches for the identical word to shrink it: __“our light affliction, which is but *for a moment*, worketh for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory”__.
The moment cuts both ways because it is not itself good or evil; it is simply the unit in which God acts — decisively, without process, whether the act is a judgment or a rescue.

Even the devil's temptation borrows the word, and gives away more than he intends by doing so.
He takes Jesus __“up into an *exceeding high mountain*”__ and shows Him __“all the kingdoms of the world *in a moment of time*”__ — and by now every term of the offer reads.
The mountain is an empire, and Scripture knows one mountain that holds all the kingdoms at once: the destroying mountain that destroys all the earth — Babylon, the composite kingdom.
And the offer arrives priced in Babylon's own doom-unit: he shows the kingdoms in a *moment* — the very measure by which they fall.
What the devil could display in a _rega_, God can end in one; the Lord declined the mountain that falls in a moment.

What changes hands in that moment is not what the devil was holding.
The trumpet that raises the dead __“*in a moment*, in the twinkling of an eye, at the *last trump*”__ is the seventh, at which __“The kingdoms of this world are become the kingdoms of our Lord, and of his Christ”__ — and the next breath tells how: __“the *nations were angry*, and *thy wrath is come*”__.

The world's kingdoms are not transferred; they fall — Daniel's stone grinds them to chaff and the wind carries them off, no place found for them — and what is given to the Son is another kingdom entirely, the kingdom the God of heaven sets up to break and consume all these: __“there was *given him* dominion, and glory, and a kingdom”__.
The devil offered the mountains as they stood — __“that is *delivered unto me*,”__ he claimed; the Father's grant arrives only over their dust.
In the moment Babylon falls, the nations of the world fall with her — and the kingdom is given to the Son of man.

Take the phrase as a marker wherever you meet it: *in a moment*, *suddenly* — when Scripture hangs those words on a kingdom, it is speaking the judgment of Babylon, or a similitude of it.

[long pause]

The argument now turns to The Sign That Was Not Sudden.

[long pause]

Set against all this, one long-fused word makes its point by contrast.
The vision that seems slow is not therefore false: __“the vision is yet for an *appointed time*, but at the end it shall speak, and not lie: though it tarry, *wait for it*; because it will *surely come*, it will not tarry”__.
God’s patience and God’s suddenness are not opposites; they are the same character seen at two distances.
He can hold back for centuries and then close the account in a single wink, because the tarrying was never uncertainty — it was the appointed time, running its full course before the moment falls.

[long pause]

That brings us to The Queen’s Clock Is Not Her Own.

[long pause]

Return, then, to the queen who claimed she would never be a widow.
The moment is not a separate judgment laid on top of Babylon’s fall; it is that judgment’s own timer.
Isaiah addresses her as the *daughter* of Babylon — not the empire itself, but the covenant wife who wears its name.
The wife of the covenant is warned, pleaded with, given generation after generation to return — the long, patient _tarrying_ of Habakkuk’s vision — but when the sentence at last falls, it falls the only way Scripture ever lets it fall on the unrepentant: __“in a moment, in one day”__, __“in one hour”__, __“suddenly”__.
The years of warning and the instant of ruin are not in tension; they are the same pattern this book keeps meeting — in the remnant of Part Two, in the shadow beside it — every symbol that turns out to turn on one small, decisive word.

God is not slow, and He is not sudden by accident.
He simply does not need more than a wink — and neither, Scripture keeps insisting, will the trumpet that raises the dead.
And if the account can close in a wink, the vocabulary had best be in hand before it does.
The case studies are next — the rest of the symbols, one by one, beginning where Daniel's image began: with the mountain.
