MEAT The Bible's Symbolic Language. [beat] Bonus Study: The Ship.

[long pause]
Scripture does not leave the ship of state to political speeches.
It builds one in front of us, plank by plank, and then tells us the city's name.

Let's read:

[quote.scripture, Ezekiel 27:2-4]
____
…take up a lamentation for *Tyrus*;
And say unto Tyrus, O thou that art situate at the entry of the sea, which art a merchant of the people for many isles…
Thy borders are in the *midst of the* seas, thy builders have perfected thy beauty.
____

[pause]

The subject is Tyre, an organized city trading among peoples and nations.
Ezekiel then constructs her as 1 vessel.

[long pause]

Now consider A City Built as a Ship.

[long pause]

The boards are fir, the mast cedar, the oars oak, the benches ivory, and the sail embroidered linen.
Then the materials become people:

Let's read:

[quote.scripture, Ezekiel 27:8-10]
____
The *inhabitants of Zidon and Arvad were thy mariners*: thy *wise men*, O Tyrus, that were in thee, were thy *pilots*.
The ancients of Gebal and the wise men thereof were in thee thy calkers…
They of Persia and of Lud and of Phut were in thine *army*, thy men of war…
____

[pause]

The transfer is exact.
The city's inhabitants are its sailors, its wise men steer, allied peoples defend it, and merchants from the surrounding nations fill its hold.
Silver, iron, tin, lead, horses, precious stones, grain, honey, oil, spices, garments, lambs, rams, and goats pass through its markets.
The city's commerce is the ship's cargo.

Ezekiel gathers the whole polity into the vessel before it breaks:

Let's read:

[quote.scripture, Ezekiel 27:26-27]
____
Thy rowers have brought thee into great waters: the east wind hath *broken thee in the midst of the* seas.
Thy riches, and thy fairs, thy merchandise, thy mariners, and thy pilots… and all thy men of war, that are in thee, and in all thy company which is in the midst of thee, shall fall into the midst of the seas in the day of thy ruin.
____

[pause]

The east wind does not merely break Tyre's fleet.
It breaks __“thee”__ — Tyre herself — and the lament closes by asking, __“What *city* is like Tyrus, like the destroyed in the midst of the sea?”__.
The city is the ship.
The fall is written in Jonah's own deep — the __“midst of the seas”__ is, in the letters, the *heart* of the seas — and it is Babylon talk: Revelation sings this very lament over Babylon the Great, from the shipmasters' mouths — __“What city is like unto this great city!”__ — and stages the same plunge, a millstone __“cast… into the sea”__, the sinking Jeremiah rehearsed with a stone bound to his book: __“Thus shall Babylon sink”__.

A **ship** is an earthly kingdom, city, state, or governed political body riding upon the sea of peoples.
Its pilots are its rulers and counselors; its crew and company are its inhabitants and servants; its defenders and commerce travel within it; its cargo is its wealth.
The surrounding symbols tell us its scale: the sea is the population in mass, while the ship is the bounded political structure raised over and carried by that population.

Its position matters.
A fish lives within the sea as a person within the peoples; a ship rides upon the sea as government over the people it directs.
The waters bear the structure, the pilots guide from above, and those aboard act together under its order.

[long pause]

Now consider The King Above the Sea.

[long pause]

An earthly ruler needs a constructed vessel to stand above the peoples.
Jesus does not.
Scripture reserves direct dominion over the waters for God:

Let's read:

[quote.scripture, Job 9:8]
____
Which alone spreadeth out the heavens, and *treadeth upon the* waves of the sea.
____

[pause]

Jesus comes to the disciples __“walking on the sea”__, standing where an earthly government must build a ship to stand.
The contrast is exact: the state is carried by the people beneath it; Christ's authority over the people is inherent.
He needs neither hull nor human office to keep Him above their tumult.

At Christ's command, Peter also steps out of the ship and walks upon the water.
But __“when he saw the wind boisterous, he was afraid; and beginning to sink, he cried, saying, Lord, save me”__.
Peter stands above the sea only while trusting and obeying the Word of the King who called him.
When fear of the people and their tumult displaces that trust, he begins to sink back into the sea; Christ alone reaches down and raises him above it.

He also sails.
When Jesus enters the ship with His disciples, the vessel becomes a governed company under its true Head.
The storm fills it, its experienced sailors despair, and the One asleep in its stern rises and commands both wind and sea: __“Peace, be still”__.
The ship may carry Him, but it does not give Him authority.
His word preserves the company and stills the people beneath it.

Mark stages His teaching the same way.
The multitude gathers by the sea, Jesus enters a ship and sits __“in the sea,”__ and the whole multitude stands upon the land.
The Teacher takes His seat above the peoples before He speaks the parables of the kingdom.

The 2 images therefore belong together without collapsing into 1.
The ship is constructed earthly rule riding upon the people; Jesus walking upon the sea is the true King ruling them directly.
When He sails with His disciples, the earthly vessel and its company are placed under their rightful Pilot.

[long pause]

Now consider When the Ship Breaks.

[long pause]

The ship and the people within it are joined, but they are not identical.
Paul's last voyage separates them with unusual precision.

A tempest takes the ship until __“all hope that we should be saved was then taken away”__.
The word then promises, __“there shall be *no loss of any man's life among you, but of the ship*”__.
The persons still have to remain inside the organized body — __“Except these abide in the ship, ye cannot be saved”__ — and Scripture counts 276 souls aboard.

The vessel finally strikes where 2 seas meet and breaks apart, yet all 276 souls escape alive.
The ship is lost; the people are not.
A state, city, administration, or covenant body may be broken while its people survive to be gathered elsewhere.

The voyage also reveals what makes a true pilot.
The centurion, shipmaster, sailors, soldiers, and prisoners retain their offices, but the word given through Paul becomes the only direction that brings everyone alive to land.
Office may hold the helm while wisdom comes from the prisoner no one believed.

[long pause]

Now consider Tyre's Wake Reaches Babylon.

[long pause]

Isaiah had already placed Tyre in the same sea.
She is a __“mart of nations,”__ a crowning city whose merchants are princes.
When the LORD stretches His hand over the sea, He __“shook the kingdoms”__, and the ships howl because their strength is laid waste.
The maritime city becomes a harlot trading with __“all the kingdoms of the world”__.

John takes up Ezekiel's lament when Babylon falls.
Again come the kings and merchants, the inventory of costly cargo, shipmasters and sailors standing afar off, dust cast upon their heads, and the cry, __“What city is like unto this great city!”__.
Babylon is Tyre's world-spanning successor: the mercantile city riding the political powers and enriching every vessel joined to her trade.

The image preserves scale.
1 ship is 1 governed body; many ships are many bodies riding upon the same sea, trading, fighting, or sailing together.
Their alliances do not dissolve their borders.

[long pause]

Now consider The Ark Carries a House.

[long pause]

Noah's ark belongs beside the ship, but it should not be forced into the same word.
The Hebrew calls it _tevah_, not the ordinary word for a ship.
It is a covenant vessel: __“with thee will I establish my covenant; and thou shalt come into the ark”__.
God then calls, __“Come thou and all thy *house* into the ark”__.

The house enters with every kind of beast and bird, clean and unclean, and the bounded vessel carries that ordered world through the flood.
The beasts later stand openly for peoples and kingdoms; here every creature-kind is preserved under 1 covenant head until the waters fall.

The ark therefore confirms the structure without replacing Ezekiel's definition.
A vessel can bear a house, a covenant, and many kinds of people through judgment.
Ezekiel shows what a political ship is; Noah shows what a covenant vessel can preserve.

[long pause]

Now consider A Metaphor the Nations Already Spoke.

[long pause]

Scripture is sufficient to define the ship, yet the world around it already spoke the same political image.
The evidence matters because it shows that a reader in the Mediterranean world did not need a modern commentator to invent the ship of state.

The 7th-century-BC poet Alcaeus pictured a vessel driven between opposing waves, its sail torn and water entering the hold; ancient commentary identified the ship as the state suffering under the tyrant Myrsilus.
Aeschylus opens _Seven Against Thebes_ with the ruler guarding the city's affairs from the stern and guiding its helm; _Suppliant Women_ calls the city a ship outright.
Plato's _Republic_ expands the picture: sailors fight for control of the helm while rejecting the 1 man who understands navigation.
Horace later addresses a battered ship driven back toward dangerous waters in an ode long recognized as an appeal to the Roman state.

Those poets do not establish the biblical meaning.
They establish recognition.
Ezekiel establishes the meaning by calling the ship Tyre and naming every class of person aboard.

[long pause]

Now consider One Man for the Ship.

[long pause]

Return to Jonah with the symbol in hand.
The LORD sends a storm for 1 man, and the ship is __“like to be broken”__.
The crew tries to save Jonah and the vessel, but the storm continues until the man it seeks is surrendered.

Jerusalem's rulers state the corresponding danger before the Passion: __“the Romans shall come and take away both our place and nation”__.
Their ship of state is about to break.
Caiaphas answers with the bargain spoken aboard Jonah's vessel: __“it is expedient for us, that *1 man* should die for the people, and that the whole nation *perish not*”__.

The ship is Jerusalem and Judea as an organized covenant polity.
Its priests and rulers pilot it, its officers and people crew it, and Rome threatens to break it.
Arrest by that body's officers places Jesus under its custody even before they carry Him through a city gate; suffering outside the wall does not release Him from the state that condemned Him.

Rome is the larger imperial power ruling over the ship and the surrounding sea.
At the sentence Jerusalem names its chosen head: __“We have no king but Caesar”__.
The smaller ship surrenders 1 man to the Great Fish so that, as its rulers suppose, the ship may live.
