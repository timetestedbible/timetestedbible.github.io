Chapter Sixteen. [beat] Heaven and Hell.

[long pause]

Deuteronomy, chapter 30: __“I call heaven and earth to record this day against you, that I have set before you *life and death*, blessing and cursing: therefore *choose life*.”__

[long pause]

Ask the modern reader where the Bible's story ends, and the answer comes back as a direction — the saved go up to heaven, and the lost go down to hell, and both go forever.
Neither direction survives the text.
The prayer the Lord Himself taught runs the traffic the other way:

[quote.scripture, Matthew 6:10]
____
Thy kingdom come. Thy will be done *in earth, as it is in heaven*.
____

[pause]
Heaven is not asked to receive us; earth is asked to become like heaven.
This chapter gathers the words — heaven, and the three different words our Bibles print as *hell* — and lets Scripture define each one.

[long pause]

Two Jurisdictions.

[long pause]

Scripture draws the map in one verse:

[quote.scripture, Psalm 115:16]
____
The *heaven*, even the heavens, are the LORD's: but the earth hath he *given* to the children of men.
____

[pause]
Heaven is where God's rule runs uncontested; the earth is the delegated realm, given to men — which is why His will must be prayed *done* here, and simply *is* done there.
Isaiah states the same structure as government: __“The heaven is my *throne*, and the earth is my *footstool*”__ — throne and footstool are not scenery but offices — the seat of rule, and the territory under it.
The Hebrew word itself keeps both registers at once — _shamayim_ is always dual or plural, never singular — the visible sky overhead, and the seat of authority above. [beat] Two layers of one word.
When fire falls on Sodom __“from the LORD out of heaven,”__ it comes through the sky and from the throne in the same motion.
[beat]
This is the heaven the fourth day furnished.
The sun, moon, and stars were *set in* it, to govern — offices the chapter on the Sun, Moon, and Stars will seat, in Part Four.

[pause]

The direction of movement between the two domains is the Bible's quiet grammar.
God's motion is downward.
Rain, from heaven's treasure.
Fire, on Sodom.
The LORD, descending on Sinai.
The Spirit, descending as a dove.
The Son — __“he that *came down* from heaven.”__
And at the end, the city itself.
[beat]
Man's upward reach is almost always the counterfeit.
Babel builds __“a tower, whose top may reach unto heaven.”__
Lucifer's five-fold boast begins, __“I will *ascend* into heaven”__ — the self-made climb into the jurisdiction that was never given.
[beat]
Scripture leaves no third account: __“*no man hath ascended up* to heaven, but he that came down from heaven.”__
Peter, preaching the resurrection with David's tomb in sight, says it of the sweet psalmist himself: __“For David is *not ascended* into the heavens.”__
The one legitimate ascent belongs to the One who descended first — and to those He takes up.

[long pause]

The Hope Comes Down.

[long pause]

Where, then, are the dead — Abraham, David, the faithful of every age?
Scripture's answer is consistent, and calm. [beat] They sleep.
Daniel: __“Many of them that *sleep in the dust of the earth* shall awake.”__
Job asks to be hidden in the grave __“until thy wrath be past… thou shalt call, and I will answer thee.”__
David __“fell on sleep, and was laid unto his fathers.”__
The dead are not watching from above; they are waiting below — __“the dead in Christ shall *rise*.”__
The hope Scripture holds out was never a disembodied escape upward.
It is resurrection — bodies raised as His was raised, on this side of the sky.

[pause]

The destination comes to meet them:

[quote.scripture, Revelation 21:2-3]
____
And I John saw the holy city, new Jerusalem, *coming down from God out of heaven*, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband.
And I heard a great voice out of heaven saying, Behold, the tabernacle of God is *with men*, and he will dwell with them…
____

[pause]
The city descends, and the tabernacle — heaven's authority touching earth at one curtained point in the wilderness — becomes the whole order: God dwelling with men, on a renewed earth, under renewed heavens.
*New* is a word this book has met before.
The Hebrew beneath Isaiah's promise of new heavens and a new earth is _chadash_ — the root of *renew, repair, rebuild*; the word of the renewed covenant.
The Greek is _kainos_ — new in freshness, not new in existence.

[pause]

The heavens are not discarded; they are purged and restored — shaken until __“those things which *cannot be shaken*”__ remain, the contested powers swept out of the heavenly places, and the whole three-story world simplified: __“a new heaven, and a new earth… and there was *no more sea*”__ — no more restless mass of nations at all.
This is why *the meek shall inherit the earth* is a promise and not a consolation prize.
The earth, measured and allotted, is where the kingdom lands — the very ground the last chapter measured.
Heaven was never the finish line; it is the government that finally arrives.

[long pause]

Three Words Under One English Word.

[long pause]

Open a concordance under *hell*, and the single English word splits into three that Scripture never confuses.

[pause]

The first is _sheol_ — and the King James itself testifies that something is buried here, because it renders the one Hebrew word *grave* thirty-one times, and *hell* thirty-one times.
One word, split down the middle.
[beat]
Sheol is not where the wicked go; it is where *everyone* goes.
Jacob expects it: __“I will go down into the *grave* unto my son mourning.”__
Job prays for it: __“O that thou wouldest hide me in the *grave*.”__
David's psalm sends the Messiah Himself through it: __“thou wilt not leave my soul in *hell*; neither wilt thou suffer thine Holy One to see corruption”__ — the verse Peter preached at Pentecost as the resurrection.
If the patriarch, the blameless man, and the Holy One all enter sheol, sheol is not the place of the damned.
It is the grave — the *demanding place* — for the word grows from _sha'al_, *to ask, to demand*.
__“Hell and destruction are *never full*,”__ says the proverb.
Sheol __“hath enlarged herself, and *opened her mouth* without measure.”__
[beat]
Scripture personifies it as a power — a mouth that swallows, *gates*, *bars*, cords — mortality's claim on all flesh, holding what death delivers.

[pause]

What is it like inside?
Not fire, and not screaming — silence:

[quote.scripture, Ecclesiastes 9:5]
____
For the living know that they shall die: but the dead *know not any thing*, neither have they any more a reward; for the memory of them is forgotten.
____

[pause]
The psalms say it three ways.
__“In death there is *no remembrance* of thee: in the grave who shall give thee thanks?”__
__“The dead *praise not* the LORD, neither any that go down into *silence*.”__
__“His breath goeth forth… in that very day his *thoughts perish*.”__
The dead of sheol think nothing, feel nothing, praise nothing.
[beat]
That is why Scripture's own word for their state is *sleep* — and why sheol is temporary.
Hannah sings it: __“The LORD killeth, and maketh alive: he *bringeth down to the grave, and bringeth up*.”__
Hosea carries the promise: __“O grave, I will be thy *destruction*.”__
And in the end, __“death and hell *delivered up the dead* which were in them.”__
The grave gives back everything it demanded — and the risen Lord already holds its keys.

[pause]

The second word is _Gehinnom_ — in Greek dress, _Gehenna_ — the Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem, where apostate Israel burned its children to Molech, and which the prophets made a name for judgment.
When Jesus warns of *hell fire*, this is His word — and He keeps it strictly apart from hades.
The gates of *hades* shall not prevail; but it is in *Gehenna* that God is __“able to *destroy* both soul and body.”__
One is the grave that holds the dead; the other is the judgment that ends the wicked.

[pause]

The third is the fire itself — and here the popular picture fails most completely, because the fire is not a place at all.

[long pause]

The Fire Is God.

[long pause]

[quote.scripture, Deuteronomy 4:24]
____
For the LORD thy God is a *consuming fire*, even a jealous God.
____

[pause]
The verse does not say God *uses* fire; it says God *is* fire — and the writer of Hebrews repeats it without softening: __“for our God is a consuming fire.”__
The unquenchable fire cannot be quenched for the same reason God cannot be extinguished.
There is no chamber constructed for the wicked in some corner of creation; there is the unshielded presence of the Holy One — and the question Scripture presses is not *where you are sent* but *what you are wearing when you meet Him*.
Moses is told the rule at the cleft of the rock: __“thou canst not see my face: for there shall *no man see me, and live*.”__
Even His own land is warned — the song of Moses, rendered literally: __“a fire is kindled in mine anger, and shall burn unto the *lowest sheol*.”__
The fire reaches into the grave itself; there is no God-free zone, for __“if I make my bed in sheol, behold, thou art there.”__

[pause]

The same fire produces three different outcomes.
The bush __“burned with fire, and the bush was *not consumed*”__ — sustained, because God willed to dwell in it.
The furnace of Babylon, heated seven times, killed the soldiers at its mouth — the uncovered — burned the ropes off the three inside, and left the three themselves without the smell of smoke — __“and the form of the fourth is like the Son of God.”__
The covered walked free, and only their bonds were consumed.
And the refiner: __“he shall sit as a *refiner and purifier* of silver”__ — the dross goes, the silver stays, in one and the same crucible — the very fire the last chapter watched carry the third part through.
Same fire, every time.
The difference was never the fire — it is the covering.

[pause]

This is why Scripture never once describes salvation as escape *from* the fire, but as passage *through* it.
Paul: __“He himself shall be saved; yet so as *by fire*.”__
The psalmist: __“We went *through fire and through water*: but thou broughtest us out into a wealthy place.”__
Joshua the high priest stands accused, and the LORD calls him __“a *brand plucked out of the fire*”__ — charred, alive, and about to be given clean garments.
[beat]
The pattern is set at the gate of Eden.
God set the cherubim and the flaming sword __“to *keep* the way of the tree of life”__ — and the Hebrew is _shamar_ — to guard and preserve, the word of watchmen and keepers, not the word for shutting.
The way to the tree of life was never bricked up.
It was kept — behind fire — and the road home runs through the flame, for the covered.

[long pause]

What the Law Says Punishment Is.

[long pause]

If the fire is God, what does God's own law say His punishments are like?
Every principle the law lays down points one direction.
[beat]
Punishment is proportional — *eye for eye, tooth for tooth* — the penalty is measured by the offense.
Punishment is capped, and the cap comes with God's own reason: __“*Forty stripes* he may give him, and *not exceed*: lest… thy brother should *seem vile* unto thee.”__
Excess punishment degrades the punished in the eyes of all — and God forbids it.
Punishment ends at death — the law's severest sentence is stoning, and the sentence is finished when the man is dead; the gravest sins are punished by being *cut off* from among the people. [beat] A cutting, not a keeping.
And release is built into the calendar itself — the jubilee, which the chapter on Liberty will proclaim, frees every debtor and bond-servant on a set day.
Nothing in the system is endless.

[pause]

Now comes the silence that should end the argument.
Deuteronomy twenty-eight is God's own catalogue of covenant curses — fifty-four verses of escalating horror: blight, boils, madness, siege, famine driving parents to unspeakable things, exile, slavery.
In all fifty-four verses of the worst that God Himself threatens, endless torment after death is never mentioned.
Either the Lawgiver forgot the most terrible penalty in His own covenant document — or it is not in His system at all.
The law's whole grammar of punishment is proportional, bounded, and terminal.
A torment without proportion, bound, or end contradicts every clause of it — and would do precisely what the forty-stripes rule forbids: make the Judge Himself seem vile.

[long pause]

The Soul Can Die.

[long pause]

The doctrine of endless torment survives on a single hidden premise — that the soul cannot die; that God *must* keep the wicked in existence, because souls are indestructible.
Scripture denies the premise in plain words.
[beat]
Adam did not receive a soul; man *became* a living soul — the soul is what happened when breath met dust, and what began can end.
Ezekiel: __“The soul that sinneth, *it shall die*.”__
Jesus: __“Fear him which is able to *destroy* both soul and body”__ — in Gehenna.
And Paul closes the question twice over.
God __“*only* hath immortality”__ — if every soul were immortal, He would not be alone in it.
The righteous are those who __“*seek* for glory and honour and *immortality*”__ — and no one seeks what he already owns.
Immortality is the gift on the far side of the fire, not the factory setting of every man.

[long pause]

The Second Death.

[long pause]

Revelation's final scene therefore reads exactly as the law and the prophets prepared it to read:

[quote.scripture, Revelation 20:13-14]
____
And the *sea* gave up the dead which were in it; and death and *hell* delivered up the dead which were in them: and they were judged every man according to their works.
And *death and hell* were cast into the lake of fire. *This is the second death.*
____

[pause]
The grave surrenders its dead; then the grave itself — with death, its rider — goes into the fire, and is destroyed.
The text supplies its own label for the fate of the lost, and the label is not *the second torment*.
It is *the second death* — and the Hebrew Revelation of the Cochin manuscripts sharpens the word.
In three of its four occurrences the phrase is _acher maveth_ — *another* death, a death of a *different kind* — the word the law uses for taking *another* wife; the word for Caleb's *another spirit*.
[beat]
The Aramaic Targums had already coined the same phrase for the same verses of the prophets.
Jeremiah's Babylon sleeps __“a *perpetual sleep*, and *not wake*”__ — and the Targum glosses it: *the second death*.
The death from which there is no waking.
Permanent, final, past atonement — not endless dying, but a death that holds.
Obadiah says it of the condemned: __“they shall be as though they *had not been*.”__
Un-created — returned to the nothing they were called out of — while __“God shall wipe away all tears… and there shall be *no more death*, neither sorrow, nor crying,”__ because the whole apparatus of death and judgment has itself been burned.

[pause]

The hard verses fall into place around this, once the words are weighed.
[beat]
__“Everlasting punishment.”__
The Greek is _kolasis_, from a root meaning *to prune* — the gardener's word — and Jesus drew its picture Himself: the fruitless branch __“is cast forth… and burned.”__
Cut off and consumed — not kept alive in the coals.
And __“everlasting”__ — _aiōnios_ — marks the permanence of the *result* — exactly as __“eternal redemption”__ is a redeeming that happened once and holds forever; exactly as Sodom, set forth as an example of __“eternal fire,”__ is not burning this afternoon.
[beat]
The undying worm and the unquenched fire, on Isaiah's last page, work on __“the *carcases* of the men that have transgressed”__ — corpses, viewed by the living, being consumed.
The dead in that verse are looked *at*, not listening.
[beat]
Smoke that __“ascendeth up for ever”__ is Scripture's picture of a *finished* burning — Babylon's smoke rises while Babylon lies destroyed — for the smoke is the receipt of the fire, not its continuation: __“as smoke is *driven away*… so let the wicked *perish*.”__

[pause]

The rich man and Lazarus is the fourth parable in a chain, told in the furniture of the day.
Its spoken point is not the architecture of the afterlife, but the sufficiency of the Scriptures: __“They have *Moses and the prophets*; let them hear them… If they hear not Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded, though one rose from the dead.”__
A parable's furniture cannot overrule the plain testimony of the very Moses and prophets it commands us to hear.
The dead know not anything — and David is not ascended.

[long pause]

Moses or Plato.

[long pause]

If endless torment is not in the law, not in the prophets, and not in Revelation's own label — where did the doctrine come from?
It has an author, and a paper trail.
Plato taught, four centuries before the cross, exactly the two planks Scripture denies — that the soul is indestructible by nature, and that the incurably wicked are punished forever in Tartarus.
Greek philosophy flooded Judea after Alexander.
Philo of Alexandria wove Plato into Moses.
And the church fathers trained in that stream — Tertullian first, Augustine most powerfully, both steeped in the philosophers — read *death* and *destruction* as *conscious torment*, because they had already accepted the premise that a soul cannot die.
Dante painted the result, and the painting hangs in the imagination of the church to this day.

[pause]

Strip the Greek premise out — let the soul be what Scripture says it is: mortal, destructible, seeking immortality as a gift — and the whole edifice comes down in one motion, leaving what the text always said: __“the wages of sin is *death*; but the gift of God is *eternal life*.”__
Life or death — existence or its end.
The choice Moses set before Israel, with heaven and earth as his witnesses, is the choice of the whole book: __“I have set before you *life and death*… therefore *choose life*.”__

[pause]

One question remains.
If the covered pass through the fire, and the uncovered are consumed — what is the covering, and how does a man come under it?
Scripture has a word for shelter in the presence of the Almighty… and it is the next chapter.

[long pause]
