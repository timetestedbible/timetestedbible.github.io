MEAT The Bible's Symbolic Language. [beat] Chapter Fourteen: Orphans, Widows, and the Fatherless.

[long pause]

Lamentations ends with this cry: __“We are *orphans and fatherless*, our mothers are as *widows*.”__

[long pause]

Few commands are easier to applaud than the care of widows and orphans.
__“Pure religion and undefiled,”__ says James, is __“to visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction.”__
Even the godless agree — funding the work through the state.
It is a good thing, and we are commanded to it.
[beat]
It is so plainly good that we read past it — and we read past it assuming one thing: that we stand among the givers.
Never the widow. Never the orphan.
[pause]
The symbolic language overturns the assumption.

[long pause]

We Are the Widow.

[long pause]

A widow is a woman whose husband is dead or has put her away; the fatherless are children left without their father.
Now hear how God speaks of His own people when they break covenant.
He hands them a bill of divorce:

[quote.scripture, Isaiah 50:1]
____
…Where is the bill of your mother’s *divorcement*, whom I have *put away*?… for your iniquities have ye sold yourselves…
____

[pause]
The city, cut off from her Husband, sits alone — Lamentations opens with it: __“how is she become as a widow!”__
Her children take up the cry: *we are orphans and fatherless, our mothers are as widows*.
[beat]
Here the symbol turns.
The widow and the fatherless are not only the poor down the street; they are the covenant people themselves, severed from the God who was their Husband and their Father.
We are the widow. We are the orphans.

[pause]

In the Bible's symbolic language this confession has an exact opposite — and she is a queen.
__“I sit a *queen*, and am *no widow*, and shall see no sorrow”__ — the boast in her heart, in Revelation, quoted word for word from the oracle Isaiah spoke against the *daughter of Babylon* long before:

[quote.scripture, Isaiah 47:8-9]
____
Therefore hear now this, thou that art given to pleasures, that dwellest carelessly, that sayest in thine heart, I am, and none else beside me; I shall *not sit as a widow*, neither shall I know the *loss of children*:
But these two things shall come to thee *in a moment in one day*, the loss of children, and *widowhood*…
____

[pause]
She assumes herself married to the King — Vashti's posture, the deposed queen from the chapter on marriage.
The sentence answers the boast in kind, repeated in Revelation: __“therefore shall her plagues come *in one day*, death, and mourning, and famine.”__
The *moment* itself has a study of its own, in a later part of this book.

[pause]

Two women, one word.
The covenant people who *confess* their widowhood are the ones the Redeemer remarries — and the queen who *denies* hers is made a widow, in a moment.
[pause]
The whole difference is the confession.

[long pause]

Thy Maker Is Thine Husband.

[long pause]

This is why God's fierce defense of the widow and the fatherless is no generic charity.
It is a Husband and a Father moving toward His own:

[quote.scripture, Isaiah 54:5]
____
For thy *Maker* is thine *husband*; the LORD of hosts is his name; and thy *Redeemer* the Holy One of Israel…
____

[pause]
He doth execute the *judgment* of the fatherless and widow, says the law, and loveth the stranger, in giving him food and raiment.
And the corner of the field left unreaped, from the last chapter, was reserved for exactly these: for the *stranger*, for the *fatherless*, and for the *widow*.
[beat]
The widowed *remnant* gleans the *edge*, as Ruth did — and is brought home by the Redeemer.

[long pause]

The Stranger.

[long pause]

The third name in that gleaning law is a defined symbol, and Scripture fixes its meaning by setting it against its exact opposite:

[quote.scripture, Ephesians 2:19]
____
Now therefore ye are *no more strangers and foreigners*, but *fellowcitizens* with the saints, and of the household of God.
____

[pause]
The opposite of a stranger is a *fellowcitizen*; so a stranger is a non-citizen — one who belongs to another commonwealth than the one he stands in.
Paul names the commonwealth in the verse just before: the Gentiles had been __“aliens from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers from the covenants of promise, having no hope, and without God.”__
The covenant is the commonwealth, and to be a stranger is to hold no citizenship in it.

[pause]

The key turns both ways.
Outside the covenant, a man is a stranger to God's commonwealth — and the law leaves the field's corner to draw him in.
The gleaning stranger is the _ger_ — the sojourner who has left his own people to dwell among God's.
Israel had been one in Egypt — *ye were strangers in the land of Egypt* — so they must love him, and bring him toward citizenship: let him keep the Passover, and __“he shall be as one that is born in the land.”__
This is the whole of Ruth — a Moabite stranger who gleans the corner and is grafted into the line of the Redeemer, until the far-off are __“no more strangers… but fellowcitizens.”__

[pause]

Once a man is made a citizen of God's commonwealth, the symbol inverts: he becomes a stranger to the *world*.
The patriarchs __“confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth,”__ and Hebrews gives the reason: __“they seek a country… an heavenly.”__
Paul writes, __“our conversation”__ — the word is *citizenship* — __“is in heaven.”__
Even in the land, God says: __“ye are strangers and sojourners with me.”__
The stranger to the world keeps the one law that is now his — the psalmist prays: __“I am a stranger in the earth: hide not thy commandments from me.”__
[pause]
The covenant that ends a man's estrangement from God begins his estrangement from the world.

[long pause]

The Poor and the Rich.

[long pause]

There is a fourth name at that unreaped corner.
The gleaning law itself sets it beside the stranger — *thou shalt leave them for the poor and stranger* — and Zechariah gathers the whole defended household in one breath:

[quote.scripture, Zechariah 7:10]
____
And oppress not the *widow*, nor the *fatherless*, the *stranger*, nor the *poor*; and let none of you imagine evil against his brother in your heart.
____

[pause]
Widow, fatherless, stranger, poor — one company, gleaning one corner.
The final member of the defended household seems at first to differ from the rest: the first three names are losses of kin and country; the fourth sounds like money.
Yet Scripture unfolds the *poor* by the same pattern.
We read the word as the man with less money than we have; we count ourselves among the givers.
Then we open the promises, and find the poor holding all of them:

[quote.scripture, Luke 6:20]
____
Blessed be ye *poor*: for yours is the *kingdom of God*.
____

[pause]
Why should the poor inherit a kingdom?
It should not, and it does not — because the wallet was never the measure.
Among the Hebrew words rendered *poor* in these promises is a close family also rendered *meek* and *humble* — Strong's calls its two chief members practically the same — and the family grows from a root that means *bowed down*.
[beat]
The poor of the promises are the bowed.

[pause]

Watch the two renderings trade places across the same texts.
The psalm says, the *meek* shall inherit the earth — and Jesus repeats it word for word: __“Blessed are the *meek*: for they shall inherit the earth.”__
Isaiah says the Anointed comes __“to preach good tidings unto the *meek*”__ — and when Jesus stands up in Nazareth and reads that very verse, it comes out __“to preach the gospel to the *poor*.”__
The same people, under either rendering.
Matthew's beatitude names the state outright: __“Blessed are the *poor in spirit*: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven”__ — bowed before God, nothing in the hand held up to Him.

[pause]

Hear God say whom He means:

[quote.scripture, Isaiah 66:2]
____
…but to this man will I look, even to him that is *poor* and of a *contrite spirit*, and *trembleth at my word*.
____

[pause]
The poor man God looks to is defined by his posture toward the word — not his balance.
He is the *remnant* — Zephaniah promises: __“I will also leave in the midst of thee an afflicted and *poor* people, and they shall trust in the *name* of the LORD.”__
Hannah sang his future: __“He raiseth up the *poor* out of the dust… to make them inherit the throne of glory.”__
James states it flat: __“Hath not God chosen the *poor* of this world *rich in faith*, and heirs of the kingdom?”__
When John the Baptist sent from prison to ask whether Jesus was the One, the closing credential of the answer was this: __“the *poor* have the gospel preached to them.”__

[pause]

Now the other column.
If the poor are the bowed, the *rich* of the symbol are the unbowed — the self-sufficient, who have need of nothing.
Solomon wrote the proverb: __“There is that maketh himself *rich*, yet hath nothing: there is that maketh himself *poor*, yet hath great riches.”__
Two churches in Revelation act out its two halves.
To suffering Smyrna: __“I know thy… *poverty* — but thou art *rich*.”__
To comfortable Laodicea:

[quote.scripture, Revelation 3:17]
____
Because thou sayest, I am *rich*, and increased with goods, and *have need of nothing*; and knowest not that thou art wretched, and miserable, and *poor*, and blind, and *naked*…
____

[pause]
The counsel that follows prices heaven's currency: __“buy of me gold tried in the fire, that thou mayest be rich; and white raiment, that thou mayest be clothed”__ — faith tried in the fire, and the garment of righteousness.

[pause]

Jesus names the true wealth in a phrase — the fool who filled his barns was __“not *rich toward God*”__ — and pronounces the woe: __“woe unto you that are *rich*! for ye have received your consolation.”__
And the exchange at the center of the gospel is stated in exactly these terms: __“though he was *rich*, yet for your sakes he became *poor*, that ye through his *poverty* might be *rich*.”__

[pause]

Here a long-standing paradox dissolves.
If the poor of the promises are the bowed, then the rich of the woes are the proud — the unbowed — and the hardest saying about them was never about a balance sheet: __“How hardly shall they that have riches enter into the kingdom of God!”__
The disciples were astonished, and Jesus answered again with the symbol opened: __“Children, how hard is it for them that *trust in riches* to enter into the kingdom of God!”__
[beat]
The trust — not the treasure.

[pause]

Read the camel and the needle as a statement about money, and Scripture contradicts itself on its face.
Abraham was very rich in cattle, in silver, and in gold.
Job's latter end was blessed with double.
Joseph administered an empire's wealth, and David a kingdom's — and all of them heirs.
[beat]
Read it as the symbol reads — the unbowed cannot pass the low door — and the paradox was never there.
__“With men this is impossible; but with God all things are possible”__ — and what God does with the proud is bow them.

[pause]

As always, the symbol does not dissolve the literal command; it deepens it.
Deuteronomy states it as law: __“The poor shall never cease out of the land: therefore I command thee… thou shalt open thine hand wide unto thy brother, to thy poor.”__
The corner stays unreaped, and the hand stays open — but the giver now knows what he is handling, and where he himself stands.
[pause]
Before God, every man is the beggar lifted from the dunghill; the only question is whether he knows it, as Smyrna did — or says, *I have need of nothing*.

[long pause]

Food and Raiment.

[long pause]

He gives the widow food and raiment — and by now, neither word is bare.
Scripture makes bread the word of God: __“man doth not live by *bread* only, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of the LORD.”__
And the garment, as the chapter on marriage showed, is the righteous deeds of the saints — deeds He ordains, and grants her to do.
So to feed and clothe His widowed people is to give them the word, and the works the word produces.
[beat]
The worst famine is not of bread:

[quote.scripture, Amos 8:11]
____
…I will send a famine in the land, not a famine of bread… but of *hearing the words of the LORD*.
____

[pause]
This is why the charge to the shepherd is *feed my sheep*.
Why the faithful servant is the one who gives the household *meat in due season*.
Why the commission is to teach the nations *to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded*.
[beat]
To feed is to teach the commandments; to clothe is to grant the righteous deeds the word teaches.

[pause]

The covenant itself is taken the same way — by eating His flesh and drinking His blood.
__“The *bread* that I will give is my flesh”__ — and his words are spirit, and life.
His body is the bread — the word to be eaten and kept.
His blood is what washes the garment clean.
The crowd could not bear it — the scene this book opened with — and many walked no more with him, because they took the symbol for the flesh.

[pause]

The command to care for the widow and the fatherless therefore reaches all the way down.
They are God's own people, cut off and gleaning at the edge.
To feed them is to bring them the word; to clothe them is to robe them in righteousness; and to gather them is the work of the Husband who will not leave them widowed.
[pause]
The next time Scripture says *feed the hungry*, hear what is handed across — the bread of His word, to a people hungrier than they know.

[long pause]
