Chapter Twelve. [beat] Wings.

[long pause]

Malachi closes the prophets with this promise: __“Unto you that fear my name shall the Sun of righteousness arise with *healing in his wings*.”__

[long pause]

A woman who had bled for twelve years pressed through a crowd to touch the edge of Jesus' robe — and __“immediately her flow of blood stopped.”__
The Gospels are precise about what she touched.
Not the cloak — its *border*, Luke says; its *hem*, says Matthew.
She was reaching for a particular part of the garment, and to see why, you must follow one Hebrew word that English has scattered into more than a dozen.

[pause]

The word is _kanaph_, and the scatter is worth counting.
It falls one hundred and eight times in the Hebrew Scriptures, and the King James renders it *wing*, *skirt*, *border*, *corner*, *quarter*, *end*, *uttermost part* — once, in Daniel, even *overspreading*.
It is the eagle's *wing* on which God bare His people unto Himself.
The *skirt* Boaz spread over Ruth.
The *border* of a garment.
The four *corners* of the earth.
[beat]
One word — the outermost edge of a thing, where it meets the world.

[pause]

Because the English keeps changing, the reader who works only in English can miss the thread.
Learn this word, and you gain a signal: wherever a verse says *skirt*, *border*, *corner*, or *the ends of the earth*, lift the English and look underneath.
If _kanaph_ is there, the commandments may be near — for reasons the next section makes plain.

[long pause]

The Fringe on the Wing.

[long pause]

God commanded something bound to that edge:

[quote.scripture, Numbers 15:38-39]
____
…bid them that they make them *fringes in the borders of their garments*… that ye may look upon it, and *remember all the commandments of the LORD, and do them*; and that ye seek not after your own heart and your own eyes…
____

[pause]
Those fringes — the _tzitzit_ — hang at the _kanaph_.
The reminder of the law is tied to every corner of the covenant garment.
Their purpose is stated flatly: to *remember the commandments and do them*, and not to follow *your own eyes*.
There is the man who is right in his own eyes — we will meet him properly in the Fool and the Wise chapter, ahead.

[pause]

Notice how well the command itself hides in English.
Here, the kanaph is *borders*.
When the command is repeated in Deuteronomy — __“thou shalt make thee fringes upon the four *quarters* of thy vesture”__ — it is *quarters*.
Neither verse says *wing*; so the English reader never connects the commandment-fringe to the wings of refuge in the Psalms, though they are one word and one thing.
[beat]
That is the signal to carry through Scripture.
The kanaph is where the *reminder* of the commandments is bound — the fringe is a memory-device, not the law itself.
So the kanaph in a verse is a flag: the verse may be speaking of remembering — or forgetting — the commands.

[pause]

Be precise about what *remember* asks, for in Scripture remembering is not recalling; it is *doing*.
The Torah defines forgetting as disobedience outright: __“Beware that thou *forget* not the LORD thy God, *in not keeping* his commandments.”__
To forget *is* to not keep.
The psalm gives the other side in a single phrase: those that remember his commandments *to do them*.
[beat]
Even God's remembering moves His hand.
God *remembered* Noah — and God *made a wind to pass over* the earth.
God heard their groaning, and God *remembered his covenant* — and the Exodus follows.
So the fringe on the wing is not an aid to reflection; it is a trigger for obedience — as James says, __“not a forgetful hearer, but a *doer* of the work.”__

[pause]

Strip the vowel points, and the word gives up one more secret.
The points — and Strong's numbering after them — split the consonants into two entries: a noun for the wing, and a verb that acts only once in all of Scripture.
Unpointed they are one word, and the lone verse where it moves as a verb shows what a kanaph *does*:

[quote.scripture, Isaiah 30:20-21]
____
And though the Lord give you the *bread* of adversity, and the water of affliction, yet shall not thy *teachers* be *removed into a corner* any more, but thine eyes shall *see thy teachers*:
And thine ears shall hear a word behind thee, saying, This is the *way*, walk ye in it…
____

[pause]
To _kanaph_ something is to tuck it into the corner — and what Israel had tucked away there was its teachers of the law.
Read it beside the fringe-command, and the two verses answer each other.
The tzitzit were tied at the corner __“that ye may *look upon it*, and remember”__ — and restoration is promised in the same grammar: eyes that *see* thy teachers again, and ears that hear, *this is the way*.
The corner is where the teaching waits — hidden in judgment, seen in mercy — which is exactly where God tied it.

[pause]

The counterfeit obliges by aiming at exactly this spot.
The Pharisees, Jesus says, __“make broad their phylacteries, and *enlarge the borders of their garments*”__ — bigger fringes, *to be seen of men*, and the weightier matters of the law undone.
A tassel grown large, to advertise an obedience that is not there.

[long pause]

Healing in His Wings.

[long pause]

Now the woman's reach makes sense.
She did not grab at random cloth; she took hold of the _tzitzit_ — the corner where the law hangs.
The word the Gospels use for what she touched — the hem, the border of His garment — is _kraspedon_: the very word the Greek Old Testament uses for the tzitzit of the fringe-command.
She laid hold of His fringes, and the prophet had foretold exactly where healing would be:

[quote.scripture, Malachi 4:2]
____
But unto you that *fear* my *name* shall the *Sun* of *righteousness* arise with *healing in his wings*…
____

[pause]
Healing *in his wings* — in the _kanaph_, the corner where the tzitzit hang.
The healing is not loose in the air; it is at the fringe, where the commandments are.
She reached for the law made flesh and laid hold of its corner — *if I may but touch his garment*, she said within herself, *I shall be whole* — and she was.
She was not alone: __“as many as touched were made *perfectly whole*.”__

[long pause]

The Covenant Under the Wings.

[long pause]

If the wing carries the reminder of the commandments, the sanctuary should show it — and it does, in furniture.
When Solomon brought the ark home, Scripture places it with this phrase:

[quote.scripture, 1 Kings 8:6]
____
And the priests brought in the *ark of the covenant* of the LORD unto his place, into the oracle of the house, to the most holy place, even *under the wings of the cherubims*.
____

[pause]
The covenant dwells *under the wings* — Scripture's own words.
The pattern was set at Sinai.
The cherubim stretch their wings __“covering the mercy seat.”__
The testimony — the tablets of the law — goes *inside* the ark beneath.
And God tells Moses where He will speak from:

[quote.scripture, Exodus 25:22]
____
And there I will meet with thee, and I will commune with thee from above the mercy seat, *from between the two cherubims* which are upon the ark of the testimony, of all things which I will give thee *in commandment* unto the children of Israel.
____

[pause]
The commanding voice speaks from between the wings, above the law.
The whole architecture of the most holy place is Numbers fifteen, built in gold — commandments kept under the kanaph.

[pause]

Even the burning ones wear the covering: the seraphim, six-winged, use four of their wings not to fly, but to cover themselves before the fire.
The psalm that promises refuge names what the covering is made of: __“under his wings shalt thou trust — his *truth* shall be thy shield and buckler.”__
And the truth, this book has shown, is the law.
To hide __“under the *shadow* of thy wings”__ is not to hide from the law; it is to hide *inside* it.

[pause]

This is why the eagle's wings of the Exodus land where they land:

[quote.scripture, Exodus 19:4-5]
____
Ye have seen what I did unto the Egyptians, and how I *bare you on eagles’ wings*, and brought you *unto myself*.
Now therefore, *if ye will obey my voice* indeed, and *keep my covenant*, then ye shall be a *peculiar treasure* unto me above all people…
____

[pause]
The wings carry Israel to Sinai — to the commandments.
As Moses sings, __“so the LORD alone did lead him.”__
At the end of the story, the flight is replayed: the woman in Revelation is given __“two *wings* of a great eagle, that she might fly into the wilderness”__ — the Exodus again, wings again, out of the serpent's reach and into the place where God keeps His own.

[long pause]

Spread Your Wing Over Me.

[long pause]

The edge that carries the law is the edge that makes covenant.
To spread your _kanaph_ over someone is to take them into covenant.
We saw it last chapter, where the whole wedding was built on it: God *spread his skirt* over Israel, and *entered into a covenant*.
Ruth asks it of Boaz in the same word:

[quote.scripture, Ruth 3:9]
____
…spread therefore thy *skirt* over thine handmaid; for thou art a near kinsman.
____

[pause]
To dwell under the wing, then, is to dwell under the covenant — and to uncover the wrong one is to violate it.
The law forbids a man his father's wife with the words, __“nor *discover his father’s skirt*”__ — the kanaph as the covenant boundary itself, in the very chapter that commands the fringes.

[pause]

Here is the grief of it.
Again and again He longed to gather His people under His wing, as a mother bird her young — and again and again, they would not.
Jesus laments it over Jerusalem in that very image:

[quote.scripture, Matthew 23:37]
____
O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, thou that killest the prophets, and stonest them which are sent unto thee, how often would I have *gathered thy children together*, even as a hen gathereth her chickens *under her wings*, and *ye would not!*
____

[pause]
Mark where that lament sits.
The same chapter opened with the enlarged fringes of the Pharisees.
Matthew twenty-three begins with the kanaph counterfeited — and ends with the kanaph refused.
The wing offered is the covenant offered; to refuse it is rebellion, and it leaves the house bare: __“Behold, your house is left unto you desolate.”__

[pause]

Refuge does not disappear when God's wing is refused; it is replaced.
Assyria comes on wings of its own — Isaiah hears it: __“the stretching out of his *wings* shall fill the breadth of thy land, O Immanuel.”__
Ezekiel's empires are *great eagles with great wings*, carrying off the top of the cedar.
[beat]
Everyone ends up under some wing.
The question is whose.

[long pause]

The Torn Skirt.

[long pause]

Because the kanaph carries covenant, what happens to the skirt happens to the kingdom — and Scripture stages it twice in one book.
When Samuel turned to leave, Saul laid hold upon the *skirt* of his mantle — and it *rent*.
Samuel reads the torn kanaph on the spot:

[quote.scripture, 1 Samuel 15:27-28]
____
And Samuel said unto him, The LORD hath *rent the kingdom* of Israel from thee this day, and hath given it to a neighbour of thine, that is *better than thou*.
____

[pause]
The torn skirt *is* the torn kingdom — the deposed-covenant verdict of the chapter on marriage, enacted in cloth.

[pause]

David, hiding in the cave, cut off the *skirt* of Saul's robe privily — __“and it came to pass afterward, that *David’s heart smote him*, because he had cut off Saul's skirt.”__
His men saw a missed kill; David felt what he had actually done — put a knife into the covenant edge of the LORD's anointed.
Yet the same cut skirt becomes his evidence.
*See the skirt of thy robe in my hand* — for in that I cut off the skirt, and killed thee not, *I have not sinned against thee*.
The kanaph held up in court — the covenant kept where it could have been cut.

[pause]

For the skirt is where a people's covenant record is kept.
Jeremiah charges: __“In thy *skirts* is found the *blood of the souls of the poor innocents*.”__
Lamentations mourns: __“her filthiness is in her *skirts*; she remembereth not her last end.”__
What is carried in the kanaph is what the covenant sees.
[beat]
Haggai's priests rule that holiness does *not* rub off through the skirt — but uncleanness does.
The fringe transmits no merit; it testifies.
And when almost everything is judged, the kanaph is where the remnant rides out the fire — Ezekiel is told: __“Thou shalt also take thereof a *few in number*, and *bind them in thy skirts*.”__

[long pause]

The Corners of the Earth.

[long pause]

Scale the garment up, as this book keeps doing, and the earth itself wears one — with corners.
The dispersed of Israel wait at them — Isaiah promises: God will __“gather together the dispersed of Judah from the *four corners of the earth*”__ — the four _kanaph_.
Songs rise __“from the *uttermost part* of the earth”__ — the kanaph again.
Judgment arrives at them — hear Ezekiel: __“An end, the end is come upon the *four corners* of the land.”__
And God handles the whole earth exactly like the garment it is:

[quote.scripture, Job 38:13]
____
That it might take hold of the *ends of the earth*, that the *wicked* might be *shaken out of it*?
____

[pause]
The dawn grips the earth by its kanaph and shakes the wicked out of it, as a man shakes out a garment.
The world has edges the way the covenant garment has edges — and the scattered people of God are kept at them, like the few hairs bound in the prophet's skirt, until the gathering.

[long pause]

The Corner of the Field.

[long pause]

The same shape appears at the edge of a field — though the word now changes, for a field's corner is _pe'ah_, not _kanaph_.
That is the symbol working as it always does — by structure, not by spelling.
The reserved edge may be a wing, a border, or a corner; what repeats is the shape — the boundary kept back, not reaped for oneself.
And that edge God reserved:

[quote.scripture, Leviticus 19:9-10]
____
…thou shalt not wholly reap the *corners of thy field*… thou shalt leave them for the *poor* and *stranger*…
____

[pause]
The forgotten sheaf and the field's corner are not the owner's.
They belong, says the law, __“for the *stranger*, for the *fatherless*, and for the *widow*.”__
A field is people — humanity is sown and reaped like grain — and the corner is only a thin border of the whole.
Yet that thin edge is enough to live on.
It is where Ruth gleaned — and where she was redeemed.
[beat]
The remnant is fed at the edge.

[long pause]

The wing gathers this book to a single edge, and hands you a working tool.
The law is sewn into the kanaph, and there is healing in it.
The ark of the covenant sits beneath it.
The covenant is made by spreading it, and read in what it carries.
The kingdom tears when it tears.
The dispersed wait at the earth's four corners — and the poor glean at the field's.
[pause]
From here on, when your English Bible says *skirt*, *border*, *corner*, *quarter*, or *the ends of the earth*, lift the word and look.
If the wing is underneath, the commandments are near — remembered, refused, or waiting to be done.
[beat]
The stranger, the fatherless, the widow who glean the corner are the next symbols to define — and after them, the remnant they add up to.

[long pause]
