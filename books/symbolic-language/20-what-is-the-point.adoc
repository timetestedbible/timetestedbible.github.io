Walk into any synagogue on earth and ask to see the scroll.
The Torah read aloud this Sabbath, like the Torah read in Jesus' day, carries no vowels — bare consonants, letter after letter, exactly as the scribes have copied them for thousands of years.
The reader supplies the breath.
Every vowel you have ever seen printed in a Hebrew Bible was added later — the pointing system was fixed by the Masoretes of Tiberias, careful men working roughly from the seventh to the tenth century after Christ, a thousand years and more after the last prophet set down his last letter.
What the prophets wrote, what the scribes counted and guarded jot by jot, was letters.
What the Masoretes added was a reading — one way to breathe each word — written down and handed on as tradition.

That distinction is not a technicality; it is Jesus' own.

[quote.scripture, Matthew 5:18]
____
For verily I say unto you, Till heaven and earth pass, one *jot* or one *tittle* shall in no wise pass from the law, till all be fulfilled.
____

The jot is the yod, the smallest letter of the alphabet; the tittle is smaller still — the little horn of a stroke that separates one letter from its look-alike.
The pledge of preservation is sworn to the *letters*, down to their smallest parts.
It says nothing of vowel points, because when He said it there were none to swear by.

And the oldest witnesses we hold read exactly the way the promise runs.
The scrolls from the caves at Qumran, copied before the apostles preached, carry the bare consonants.
The Greek translation of the Old Testament — the Septuagint, made two to three centuries before Christ — is something even more useful: a written record of *how the letters were read* a thousand years before the Masoretes breathed on them.
Where the later pointing and that older Greek diverge, two readings of the same letters stand side by side, and the Greek is the elder witness.

The apostles have already told us which court they sat in.
The Introduction of this book stood on the fortieth Psalm — __“I delight to do thy will”__ — so read two verses up, in the psalm as the pointed Hebrew gives it:

[quote.scripture, Psalm 40:6]
____
Sacrifice and offering thou didst not desire; *mine ears hast thou opened*: burnt offering and sin offering hast thou not required.
____

Now hear the writer of Hebrews quote that same verse — and mark whose mouth he puts it in:

[quote.scripture, Hebrews 10:5]
____
Wherefore when he cometh into the world, he saith, Sacrifice and offering thou wouldest not, but *a body hast thou prepared me*.
____

__“A body hast thou prepared me”__ is the old Greek reading of the psalm, and it is not what the pointed Hebrew says.
The apostle sets it in the Messiah's own mouth __“when he cometh into the world,”__ and two verses later the psalm arrives at the line this book began with: __“Lo, I come… to do thy will, O God”__ (Hebrews 10:7).
The New Testament's canon of the Old includes readings the later pointing does not carry.
That is not a problem to be explained away; it is a permission — the apostolic practice treats the pre-Masoretic reading as the word of God.

The Introduction already showed what hangs on this.
The same three letters — חלב — are _chalav_, sym:sym-milk[milk], or _cheleb_, fat, divided only by the points; the pointed text of Ezekiel 34:3 indicts the shepherds for eating the fat, the older Greek for drinking the milk, and both readings convict.
One word, two breaths, and a doctrine in each.

So weigh the points for what they are.
The Masoretes were the most careful custodians a text has ever had — they counted the letters of every book, marked the middle letter of the Torah, and built a fence of numbers around the text precisely so that nothing could be added or lost.
Honor them for it.
But custodians are not judges, and these custodians were not neutral.
The breath they fixed is the synagogue's breath, handed down through the very generations that had answered the Messiah question with a ban — and Scripture documents the enforcement itself:

[quote.scripture, John 9:22]
____
The Jews had agreed already, that if any man did *confess that he was Christ*, he should be *put out of the synagogue*.
____

It worked, and on the men best placed to know better:

[quote.scripture, John 12:42-43]
____
Nevertheless among the chief rulers also many believed on him; but because of the Pharisees they did not confess him, lest they should be *put out of the synagogue*: for they *loved the praise of men* more than the praise of God.
____

A reading that confessed too much could cost a man his place, his trade, and his family's standing; the reading that survived a thousand years of that pressure is the reading of those who stayed.
And this bias is not a hypothesis — it can be watched working.
The psalm this chapter just opened is one example: the synagogue's pointing keeps the ears; the apostles' Greek gives the Messiah His body.
Psalm 22 is another — the psalm that opens with the cry from the cross.

[quote.scripture, Psalm 22:16]
____
For sym:sym-dog[dogs] have compassed me: the sym:sym-assembly[assembly of the wicked] have inclosed me: they *pierced* my hands and my feet.
____

__“Pierced”__ is how the old Greek read the word — a verb, _they dug through_ — and it is how a Hebrew scroll found in the Judean desert, copied before the Masoretes were born, spells it: _ka'aru_, ending in a vav.
The synagogue's text ends the same word with a yod instead: _ka'ari_ — __“like a lion, my hands and my feet”__ — a sentence with no verb left in it.
The entire difference between the piercing and the lion is the length of a single stroke: a yod drawn out is a vav, the very jot the promise names.
And the reading that takes the crucifixion out of the psalm is the one the tradition kept.

Now mark what kind of case that is: not a point at all, but a letter — a letter the promise itself guards.
If the tradition was bold enough to settle even a letter against the elder witnesses, how much more easily a point?
The points touch no letter; they pass the jot-and-tittle pledge untried and slip through the fence of numbers with every scribal count intact — the one place a man could make his own point instead of God's, and leave the text looking untouched.

And there is a command standing over exactly this.

[quote.scripture, Deuteronomy 4:2]
____
Ye shall not *add* unto the word which I command you, neither shall ye *diminish* ought from it, that ye may *keep the commandments* of the LORD your God which I command you.
____

Kept as commentary, the points add nothing — they stand beside the word, a reading of men, weighed like any reading.
But elevated to Scripture itself — bound into the text and taught as if God breathed them — they become an addition to the word in the plainest sense: a mark of man on every syllable of God's, __“Add thou not unto his words, lest he reprove thee, and thou be found a *liar*”__ (Proverbs 30:6).
And mark what the command is for: __“that ye may keep the commandments”__ — the fence against adding exists for the sake of obedience, the same stake this chapter set at its start.
And His calendar sat under the same fence: the same community that fixed the breath had long since traded the moon that is watched for a moon that is calculated — the appointed times this book reaches in the next part were guarded by the same social pressure.
This is no charge that the letters were lost; guarded by their numbers and answered by the scrolls from the desert, they held — held so well that the rare disputed letter stands exposed for weighing.
It is the breath that is on trial.
What the pointing added, by its own accounting, was a tradition of reading — and tradition is a category Scripture has already taught this book how to handle.

[quote.scripture, Jeremiah 16:19]
____
The Gentiles shall come unto thee from the ends of the earth, and shall say, Surely our fathers have inherited *lies*, *vanity*, and things wherein there is *no profit*.
____

Peter says the redeemed were bought out __“from your vain conversation received *by tradition from your fathers*”__ (1 Peter 1:18); Jesus charges the scribes with __“making the word of God *of none effect* through your tradition”__ (Mark 7:13).
None of that abolishes the scribes' text — it ranks it.
The letters are the text; the pointing is the fathers' reading of it — often right, worth hearing, and never the judge.

That ranking gives this book its rule, and it cuts both ways.
A reading the letters permit may stand even against the pointing — but it earns its place the way every reading in this book earns it: every occurrence gathered, witnesses required, counter-texts weighed.
The pointing gets a vote; it does not get a veto — and neither does the re-reading, until the letters and the witnesses agree.

And the stakes are the same stakes this book has carried from its first chapter: obedience.
__“Ye eat neither *fat* nor blood”__ is a standing command (Leviticus 3:17), and it is written in the same three letters as milk — so which food a verse names can decide what a servant may put in his mouth.
A man who intends to keep the commandments exactly will want them exactly as they were written.
Dig past the breath to the letters; that is where the best sym:sym-meat[meat] is cut.

There is one more thing the letters do — something no pointing can record and no translation can carry.
They rhyme in meaning.
The next chapter opens that second dimension: one verse, written once, that reads two ways and is true in both.
