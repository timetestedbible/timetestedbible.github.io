# Ship — occurrence sweep and definition

## Terms and range

- **H591, `oni / oniyyah`** — the ordinary Hebrew ship word. It appears in
  literal voyages and navies, but Ezekiel 27 extends the entire anatomy of a
  ship over the city-state of Tyre.
- **H590, `oni`** — fleet or navy in Kings and Chronicles.
- **H5600, `sephinah`** — the inner vessel in Jonah 1:5; used once.
- **H8392, `tevah`** — Noah's and Moses' ark. This is not the ordinary ship
  word and should remain a related covenant-vessel witness, not the lexical
  basis of the Ship definition.
- **G4143, `ploion`** — the ordinary New Testament ship word.

The tagged KJV sweep returns 123 verses containing the principal ship, navy,
sailor, shipmaster, and ark terms. Most are literal transport, fishing, naval,
or commercial uses. A symbol does not erase those uses; it becomes visible
where Scripture itself transfers the vessel's parts and fate to a city or
organized body.

## Identity-grade witness: Ezekiel 27

Ezekiel 27 begins by naming its subject: "take up a lamentation for Tyrus"
(v. 2). It then speaks to Tyre directly and constructs her as 1 ship:

| Vessel element | Stated political referent |
|---|---|
| borders in the seas | Tyre's reach (vv. 3-4) |
| boards, mast, oars, benches, sail | the city's constructed beauty (vv. 5-7) |
| mariners | inhabitants of Zidon and Arvad (v. 8) |
| pilots | Tyre's wise men (v. 8) |
| calkers | elders and wise men of Gebal (v. 9) |
| defenders | Persia, Lud, Phut, Arvad, and Tyre's army (vv. 10-11) |
| cargo | international merchandise and wealth (vv. 12-24) |
| company within | merchants, mariners, pilots, army, and population (v. 27) |
| ship broken in the seas | ruin of the city-state (vv. 26-36) |

The lament closes the equation by asking, "What city is like Tyrus, like the
destroyed in the midst of the sea?" (v. 32). Tyre is the city and the ship.

**Core definition:** A ship is an earthly kingdom, city, state, or governed
political body riding upon the sea of peoples. Its pilots are its rulers and counselors; its
crew and company are its inhabitants and servants; its defenders and commerce
travel within it; its cargo is its wealth. Its breaking is the loss of the
organized body, which need not mean the loss of every person in it.

The vertical relation is essential. A fish lives within the sea as a person
within the peoples; a ship rides upon the waters as an organized authority over
the people it directs. The population bears the political structure as the sea
bears the vessel.

## Christ above the sea

The spatial image has 2 forms that should not be collapsed. An earthly ruler
requires a constructed vessel to ride above the peoples. God "treadeth upon the
waves of the sea" (Job 9:8), and Jesus fulfills that image by walking on the
water (Matt 14:25). His authority is direct rather than supplied by a political
hull.

At Christ's command Peter also leaves the constructed vessel and walks above
the water (Matt 14:28-29). When he sees the wind and becomes afraid, he begins
to sink (v. 30). Within the established sea/peoples symbol, fear of the people
and their tumult draws the disciple back beneath what Christ's word enabled him
to stand above. Peter's derived authority depends upon trusting and obeying the
King; Christ's authority over the sea is inherent.

When Jesus sails with His disciples, He enters a governed company and acts as
its true Head. In Mark 4:36-41 the trained sailors cannot preserve the vessel,
but His word stills the wind and sea. In Mark 4:1 He sits in the ship "in the
sea" while the multitude stands on land: the Teacher takes His seat above the
peoples. The ship does not become Christ and does not confer His authority; it
is brought under its rightful Pilot.

## The Tyre–Babylon line

Isaiah 23 surrounds Tyre with the same political-maritime vocabulary. Tyre is
a "mart of nations" (v. 3), a crowning city whose merchants are princes (v.
8), and a harlot trading with all kingdoms (vv. 15-17). When God stretches His
hand over the sea, He "shook the kingdoms" (v. 11), and the ships howl because
their strength is laid waste (vv. 1, 14).

Revelation 18 reprises Ezekiel 27's lament over Babylon: kings and merchants,
an inventory of luxury cargo, shipmasters and sailors standing afar off,
dust cast on heads, the question "What city is like unto this great city?",
and sudden desolation (Rev 18:9-19). Revelation's Babylon is the final form of
the mercantile city riding upon the seas, and the ships are the organized powers and
commercial bodies enriched through her.

## The polity and the persons can separate

Acts 27 supplies an important boundary. In the storm Paul is promised, "there
shall be no loss of any man's life among you, but of the ship" (v. 22). The
persons must remain in the organized vessel for safety (v. 31), 276 souls are
counted within it (v. 37), the ship breaks (v. 41), and every person escapes
alive (v. 44). The ship is not identical to the people it contains. A governed
body can be destroyed while its members survive and are gathered elsewhere.

The chapter also distinguishes formal office from true guidance. The
centurion, shipmaster, sailors, soldiers, and prisoners retain their offices,
but the word given through Paul becomes the only reliable direction. The ship
is a compact ordered society at sea.

## Noah's ark: related, not identical

Noah's ark is `tevah`, not the normal ship word. It is called a house-bearing
covenant vessel: "Come thou and all thy house into the ark" (Gen 7:1), after
God establishes His covenant with Noah (Gen 6:18). Every kind of beast and bird
is gathered inside and carried through judgment (Gen 6:19-20; 7:14-15).

This supports the larger structure — a bounded vessel carries an ordered house
and the creature-kinds through the waters — but the ark's primary emphasis is
covenant preservation. It should corroborate rather than define Ship.

## Jonah and the Passion

Jonah's vessel is threatened with destruction by a storm sent for 1 man
(Jonah 1:4, 7, 12). The crew attempts to save both him and the ship, then
surrenders him so the whole company will not perish (1:13-16).

The Passion supplies the political statement of the same bargain. Jerusalem's
rulers fear that Rome will "take away both our place and nation" (John 11:48),
and Caiaphas answers that danger by declaring it expedient for 1 man to die
so "the whole nation perish not" (John 11:50). The ship is Jerusalem/Judea as
an organized covenant state; its threatened breaking is the feared loss of
place and nation. Arrest by its officers places Jesus within its custody even
outside the municipal wall.

Rome is the larger imperial power over that ship and sea. Jerusalem finally
names its head at the sentence: "We have no king but Caesar" (John 19:15).

## Ancient corroboration

The external sources do not define the biblical symbol; they show that the
political image was already conventional in the Mediterranean world.

- Alcaeus, fragments 6/208, portrays a storm-tossed vessel amid political
  conflict; Heraclides' ancient commentary identifies the state under Myrsilus
  as the allegory.
- Aeschylus, *Seven Against Thebes* 1-3, places the ruler at the city's stern
  guiding its helm; *Suppliant Women* 345 explicitly invokes the city's ship.
- Plato, *Republic* 6.488a-489d, develops the state as a ship whose sailors
  fight for the helm while rejecting the true pilot.
- Horace, *Odes* 1.14, adapts the storm-beaten ship in a poem conventionally
  read as an address to the Roman state.

These witnesses establish recognition, not authority. Ezekiel 27 carries the
definition inside Scripture.

## Counterexamples and limits

- Most biblical ships are literal. Context must activate the political
  transfer; the definition is not a license to allegorize every fishing boat.
- A fleet may represent several subordinate bodies or simply literal naval and
  commercial power. Number and relationship matter.
- The ship is not the sea. The sea is the mass of peoples; the ship is an
  organized body maintaining a boundary and direction among them.
- The ship is not necessarily the highest kingdom. It may be subordinate to a
  larger empire, as Judea's polity operated under Caesar.
- The ship is not identical to everyone aboard. Acts 27 explicitly separates
  loss of the ship from preservation of its souls.

## Verdict

**Ship = an earthly kingdom, city, state, or governed political body riding upon the sea of
peoples. Its rulers guide, its inhabitants and servants crew it, its armies
defend it, and its wealth is carried as cargo. When it breaks, the polity is
lost even if its people survive.**
