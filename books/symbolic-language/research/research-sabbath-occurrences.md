# Every occurrence of *shabbat* — the exhaustive catalog (day-sense vs. week-sense)

Method: every tagged occurrence of the noun *shabbat* (Strong's H7676, **108 occurrences in 89 verses**)
and *shabbaton* (H7677, **11 in 10**) in `bibles/kjv_strongs.txt`, with each Hebrew form read from the
consonantal text of `bibles/wlc.txt`; every occurrence of Greek *sabbaton* (G4521, **68 in 62**) in the New
Testament. Nothing is sampled and nothing is omitted — the class totals below sum to the corpus totals,
verified by script. The symbol derivation lives in [research-sabbath.md](research-sabbath.md); this file is
the occurrence-level appendix behind the scope claim.

## The claim, stated precisely

**Wherever the text itself marks the extent of *shabbat*, the marking is consistent — without exception.**
Day-sense is always carried by a day-marker: the construct *yom ha-shabbat*, a predicate (“this day is a
sabbath”), a date anchor, or the *shabbaton* rest-grade. Unit-sense (the seven) is always carried by a
unit-marker: a count, an ordinal, a distributive, or a duty rotation. No counted, distributed, or rotated
*shabbat* can be read as a single day; no *yom ha-shabbat* can be read as a week; and the unmarked bare
form never states an extent at all — context assigns it, and no context contradicts the system. *Shabbaton*
never appears in a count, and *shabbat shabbaton* marks the GRADE of rest, not the extent — it attaches to
the seventh day, to fixed feast dates, and to the seventh YEAR alike (Lev 25:4).

Every “first day of the week” in the resurrection accounts is literally *mia (ton) sabbaton* — “one of the
sabbaths.” Luke 18:12 is decisive on its own terms: *dis tou sabbatou*, “twice in the sabbath” — no man
fasts twice in a day. The Hebrew Gospels (Ele Ha-Sodot) render both: Matt 28:1 with *shavua* (H7620,
“week”) and Luke 18:12 *ba-shavua* — the Hebrew translator read these *sabbaton* as the week, plainly.

## The contested texts, one by one

- **Lev 23:11, 15, 16 — “the morrow of the sabbath.”** The statute's own parallel decides it: v. 16 sets the
  fiftieth day at “the morrow of the SEVENTH sabbath,” so the sabbaths in the count are the counted units,
  and v. 15's *temimot* (“complete”) requires a span — a day cannot be partially complete. Deut 16:9 restates
  the same command as “seven WEEKS” (*shavuot*) — sabbath = week by parallel statute, and the Feast of
  WEEKS is named for what was counted. The LXX reads the boundary the same way: τῇ ἐπαύριον τῆς πρώτης,
  “on the morrow of the FIRST [day]” (Lev 23:11) — the morrow of a unit's first day, not of a Saturday.
- **Neh 13:19 — gates shut “before the sabbath.”** Boundary language around the bare form. It tells you the
  observance has an edge; it does not tell you the extent. Nothing in the chapter strains either reading —
  the commerce verses around it (13:15-21) are day-extent by context, and the catalog claims nothing more.
- **Num 28:10 / Isa 66:23 — *shabbat b'shabbato*.** The distributive suffix idiom is a closed family — *yom
  b'yomo* (daily), *chodesh b'chodsho* (monthly), *shanah b'shanah* (yearly) — and it always distributes over
  the recurring unit named. Isa 66:23 sets *shabbat b'shabbato* in direct parallel with *chodesh b'chodsho*:
  unit beside unit, week beside month.
- **2 Kgs 11 / 2 Chr 23 — “them that enter in on the sabbath.”** Guard companies “enter” and “go out of”
  the sabbath, and 2 Chr 23:8 names why: “Jehoiada dismissed not the courses” — the week-long duty shifts
  (cf. 1 Chr 9:25). One enters a span; one does not enter a Saturday with two-thirds of an army still inside it.
- **Lev 23:24, 39 — feast-day *shabbaton*.** Trumpets and the 1st/8th of Tabernacles are *shabbaton* —
  rest-GRADE days on fixed dates. They are never called *ha-shabbat*, and they show the grade-word working
  exactly as classified: grade, not extent, not the weekly seventh.
- **Lev 25:4 — *shabbat shabbaton* on a YEAR.** The intensive attaches to the seventh year. If *shabbat
  shabbaton* meant “the seventh day,” this verse could not exist. Grade, not extent — stated by the law itself.
- **Matt 28:1 — both senses in one verse.** “At the end of the sabbaths [the closing unit], as it began to
  dawn toward one of the sabbaths [the new week]” — the grammar holds day-boundary and week in a single
  breath, which is only possible if the word carries both.
- **Acts 17:2 — “three sabbaths.”** A count — and counts are unit-territory throughout the corpus. Whether
  one renders “three sabbath days” or “three weeks,” Paul's stay is measured in sevens.
- **Luke 6:1 — *deuteroprotos*.** “The second-first sabbath” — an ordinal position inside a counted sequence
  of sabbaths (the omer count is the natural referent), witnessing that first-century usage numbered sabbaths
  in series.
