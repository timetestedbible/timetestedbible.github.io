## 02 — The Parables of the Kingdom (the sower; Matthew 13:3-8)

A black-and-white etching in the style of Rembrandt — fine cross-hatched line work, drypoint burr, dramatic chiaroscuro, the look of an aged copper-plate print; pure black ink on white paper, line work only, no gray washes. A sower strides across a hillside field at first light, seen in three-quarter profile, seed-bag slung at his hip, one arm sweeping a broadcast arc of seed rendered as a spray of fine dots against the dawn. The single field holds all four grounds in one view: the trodden path along the near edge where birds already drop and peck; a shelf of stony ground with thin quick shoots; a dark corner of thorns choking pale stalks; and the deep plowed good ground opening away toward the light, furrows converging to the horizon. A yoke of oxen rests at the field's far margin. The sower does not look at any of it; he sows. Stippled sky gradations, plate-tone vignette at the edges, high contrast, suitable for reproduction as pure black line art. Portrait orientation, roughly 2:3 (e.g., 1600 × 2400).

## 38 — Garments (the man without a wedding garment; Matthew 22:11-13)

The threshold of a wedding hall at night, blazing with lamplight from within. At the entry a steward stands beside an open chest of folded white garments, laying one across the arms of each guest who enters — the provision is free and visible, that is the point — and guests already robed in white move past him into the glowing hall where the long tables shine. In the near foreground, at the edge of the light, the crisis: a man in his own fine street clothes, having refused the offered robe, is taken by two porters with torches, his face caught between indignation and dawning fear, his hands empty. The doorway's light ends in a hard line at his feet; beyond that line, at the plate's bottom corner, the outer darkness is total. ## 39 — The Bow (the covenant bow, in hand and in heaven; Revelation 6:2, Genesis 9:13)

A rider on a white horse crests a rise at full gallop, crowned, holding a great strung bow lifted high — and no arrow anywhere: the quiver empty, the weapon carried as a standard rather than drawn. His face is lost in the shadow of the crown. Behind him the storm he rides out of is clearing: a curtain of rain still falls along the horizon, and through it stands the covenant bow — one vast arc of fine concentric engraved bands spanning the whole sky, light shining through the falling rain — and the rider's lifted bow and the sky's great bow are bent to the same curve, the small echoing the immense. Below the rise, the plain of the nations — camps, roads, distant walled towns — lies under the arc. Low light breaks across the field so the white horse burns bright against the dark rain. ## 40 — Jacob, Israel, and Ephraim (wrestling till the day breaks; Genesis 32:24-28)

A black-and-white etching in the style of Rembrandt — fine cross-hatched line work, drypoint burr, dramatic chiaroscuro, aged copper-plate character; pure black ink on white paper, line work only, no gray washes. The ford of the Jabbok in the last hour of night: shallow water braiding over stones, Jacob's staff and bundle abandoned on the bank. Two figures locked in a wrestle that has lasted till now — Jacob gripping with both arms, face pressed into the other's shoulder, every muscle in extremity, one hip visibly wrenched and his leg failing under him, yet his hold is the hold of a man who will not let go. His opponent is drawn entirely as a dark form against the dawn — back to the viewer, featureless, rimmed in the first light breaking over the ridge behind — no face, no wings, a man-shaped mystery. The day-break light comes exactly along the line of their struggle, and Jacob's upturned, exhausted, asking face is the one face in the plate. Stippled gradations, plate-tone vignette, high contrast, pure black line art. Portrait orientation, roughly 2:3 (e.g., 1600 × 2400).

## 41 — The Foreskin (the orchard keeper and the young tree; Leviticus 19:23)

A terraced hillside orchard in spring light. In the foreground an old keeper kneels at a young fruit tree, grafting knife in hand, cutting away the wild covering growth that has sheathed the graft-union at the trunk — the curl of severed bark falling from the blade at the caught instant, the clean living wood beneath catching the light. Around the young tree's base, its early fruit lies dropped and deliberately ungathered in the grass — the forbidden first years, left to the ground. A farmhouse doorway at morning, light slanting in across a stone floor. A woman works a tall wooden churn with both hands, sleeves rolled, caught mid-stroke at the exact moment of arrival: the lifted dasher shows solid butter clinging in a bright mass — the labor has set. On the table beside her, the diet it belongs to: a dish of butter, a comb of honey, and a round loaf, plainly drawn. At the table's end a child sits eating butter and honey from a piece of bread, watchful eyes over the crust — the one who is learning to refuse the evil and choose the good.
