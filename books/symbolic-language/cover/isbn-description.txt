MEAT The Bible's Symbolic Language — ISBN registration description (315 words)

"Know ye not this parable? and how then will ye know all parables?" Jesus
asked His disciples — and He is still asking. "I have fed you with milk,
and not with meat," Paul told a church that should have been ready for
more. By now, you ought to be eating meat.

The Bible defines its own symbolic language. A mountain is a kingdom —
Scripture says so. The sea is the peoples; a tree is a nation; bread is
teaching; a coin is a person, stamped with its owner's image. These
meanings are not imposed by any interpreter: each is defined openly in
one passage, used consistently in the rest, and contradicted by none.
MEAT teaches that method of discovery and then applies it, symbol by
symbol, until the reader can do it without help.

More than 140 symbolic words are collected in the glossary, each with
its meaning, its defining verse, and the place where Scripture
establishes it — supported by over 2,500 citations from 64 of the
Bible's 66 books, gathered in a complete Scripture Index. And every
definition was tested: a blind experiment measured the whole glossary
against the consensus of mainstream teaching. Three of every four
entries land on, or sharpen, what careful readers have always held — the
record that proves the method is no private invention. Thirty-five say
something you have almost certainly never heard taught, and each one is
badged in the glossary with the common reading printed beside it, so the
two can be weighed face to face.

From that vocabulary the book reads the hard questions plainly: what
heaven and hell actually are, what the seal of God is and what
counterfeits it, marriage and divorce, the remnant and how narrow the
way is, Babylon and its fall, and the path to salvation.

No argument rests on the author's say-so. The sources are cited, the
tools are handed over, and the book's standing instruction is its own:
take no man's word — verify.
