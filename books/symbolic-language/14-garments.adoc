A man is bound hand and foot and thrown out of a wedding feast into the dark — and his offense was his clothing.
The feast is no ordinary supper: it is a king’s, made __“for his son”__ (Matthew 22:2) — a parable of the kingdom, and the wedding is the sym:sym-marriage[marriage] of the Lamb.
The man __“had not on a wedding garment”__ (Matthew 22:11), and when the king asks how he entered so, he has no answer; __“he was speechless”__ (Matthew 22:12) — the Greek is stronger than the English: _phimoō_, to *muzzle*, the same word Paul uses of the ox that treads out the grain (1 Corinthians 9:9) and the very word Jesus speaks to the storm and to unclean spirits, __“Peace, be still”__ (Mark 4:39), __“Hold thy peace”__ (Mark 1:25).
He was not merely at a loss for words; he was muzzled — silenced the way a power greater than his own silences what it overrules, with no defense in his mouth because he had none on his back.
He was not stopped at the gate — he was invited, seated, and only then found out, for __“many are called”__ (Matthew 22:14).
What condemns him is what he has on.
And at that very marriage Scripture halts to name the garment.

[quote.scripture, Revelation 19:8, ASV]
____
And it was given unto her that she should array herself in fine linen, bright and pure: for the fine linen is the *righteous acts* of the saints.
____

The fine linen _is_ the righteous _acts_ of the saints — the things they did.
The Greek is _dikaiōmata_, righteous deeds, and the ASV, ESV, NASB, and NIV all render it so.
The King James shortens it to __“the righteousness of saints,”__ and on that one narrowed word a long confusion is built: _righteousness_ gets turned from something you _do_ into a state you merely _have_, sanctification folded into justification until the garment seems to require no weaving at all.
But the word is plural and active — righteous _acts_.
The garment is righteousness performed; to be clothed is to have done it, to be bare is to stand in your sins.
So the man who came to the wedding with no garment came with no righteous acts on him at all — invited, seated, and naked.

And Scripture says plainly what those acts are, for _righteousness_ is itself a word men stumble over — taken for a vague goodness, or a verdict merely received.
The law defines it: __“it shall be our righteousness, if we observe to do all these commandments before the LORD our God”__ (Deuteronomy 6:25); __“all thy commandments are righteousness”__ (Psalm 119:172).
And lest anyone make it a state without deeds, John says it in one line: __“he that doeth righteousness is righteous”__ (1 John 3:7).
Righteousness is the doing of what God commanded — so the garment of righteous acts is simply the law kept, and worn.
This is why the song over this chapter sings the way it does: __“he hath clothed me with the *garments of salvation*, he hath covered me with the *robe of righteousness*”__ (Isaiah 61:10) — garments of _yeshuah_: the covering He gives is salvation-wear, and its fabric is righteousness.
And it is the robe the father calls for when the prodigal comes home: __“Bring forth the *best robe*, and put it on him”__ (Luke 15:22) — the covenant covering restored on repentance, before the boy can finish his speech.

=== The Covering Is the Covenant

Behind the garment stands the covenant.
When God first took Israel for His own, He told it as the clothing of a naked foundling:

[quote.scripture, Ezekiel 16:8]
____
…I spread my *skirt* over thee, and *covered thy nakedness*… and entered into a *covenant* with thee… and thou becamest mine.
____

Three phrases for one act: to spread the skirt, to cover the nakedness, to enter the covenant.
That spread skirt is the covenant covering itself — the kanaph, the sym:sym-wings[wing], the sym:sym-name[name] taken, as the chapters behind us showed.
And mark what follows it, for the garment is a *second* gift, given inside the covenant: __“Then washed I thee… I *clothed thee* also with broidered work… and I girded thee about with *fine linen*”__ (Ezekiel 16:9-10).
First the covering of the covenant; then the clothing of the covered.
Revelation keeps the same grammar at the wedding — __“to her was *granted* that she should *array herself*”__ (Revelation 19:8): given, and put on by her; deeds God __“before ordained that we should *walk in them*”__ (Ephesians 2:10), worked in her by His word, and washed whiter than snow when she stains them.
And the word for __“skirt”__ here is _kanaph_ — also sym:sym-wings[wing], _border_, _corner_ — the very edge of the garment where God commanded the fringes to be bound, __“that ye may… remember all the commandments of the LORD, and do them”__ (Numbers 15:39).
The covenant garment has the law sewn into its hem; that thread has its own chapter to come.
So the righteous acts that make up the garment are not scattered good turns — they are the covenant lived out, the law kept and worn where every eye can see it.
As the sym:sym-name[name] is the covenant _owned_, the garment is the covenant _worn_.

And the first garment was cut at the cost of blood.
The man and his wife stood naked by their sin, and God makes them __“coats of skins”__ and __“clothed them”__ (Genesis 3:21) — a covering that cost a life, the first blood shed to clothe the guilty, and the first shadow of the Lamb to come.
So nakedness is a symbol in its own right, the exact opposite of the garment: the clothed are covered by covenant, the naked stand exposed in their breach — and every verse that defines the one defines the other.

[discrete]
=== Filthy Rags

The garment is woven of righteous acts, and the acts themselves are clean — but the cloth can be soiled.
One verse is pressed into service to deny this:

[quote.scripture, Isaiah 64:6]
____
But we are all as an *unclean thing*, and all our *righteousnesses* are as *filthy rags*…
____

It is read as if obedience itself were filth — as if the more law a man keeps, the dirtier he grows.
But read the whole line: __“we are all as an unclean thing,”__ and so even __“our righteousnesses”__ come out stained.
It is not the righteous act that is filthy; it is the sin it is worked in.
The rag is blood-stained — fouled by the very iniquities the verse goes on to name — not because doing right is wrong, but because the doer is unclean.
A clean deed from a stained hand comes away marked.

The answer to a dirty garment is not to take it off; it is to wash it. __“Wash you, make you clean,”__ God says in the same prophet, and then, __“though your sins be as sym:sym-scarlet[scarlet], they shall be as white as snow”__ (Isaiah 1:16-18).
The saints __“washed their robes, and made them white in the blood of the Lamb”__ (Revelation 7:14); Joshua the high priest stands __“clothed with filthy garments”__ until the word goes out, __“Take away the filthy garments from him… I will clothe thee with change of raiment”__ (Zechariah 3:4) — the iniquity removed, the robe made clean.
And the keeping is itself commanded: the last beatitude in Scripture is for __“they that do his commandments, that they may have right to the tree of life”__ (Revelation 22:14).
Those who take no responsibility for their robe — who neither weave nor wash — are the ones found bare.

The Lord enacted it the night before He died.
He girded a towel and washed the disciples’ feet, and when Peter refused, He warned, __“If I wash thee not, thou hast no part with me”__ (John 13:8).
Then the key: __“He that is washed needeth not save to wash his feet, but is clean every whit”__ (John 13:10).
The bath is baptism — washed once into the Name, and so into the covenant: __“as many of you as have been baptized into Christ have put on Christ”__ (Galatians 3:27).
A man is not bathed again and again; he is not baptized over and over, and the robe is put on once.
But his feet gather the dirt of the road and must be washed — the sym:sym-feet[feet] are the walk, the conduct that is a man’s sym:sym-way[way].
So Jesus stooped to wash them: not a second baptism, but the daily cleansing of the walk that keeps the garment clean.
And __“ye also ought to wash one another’s feet”__ (John 13:14).

There is an opposite error, and it is the older one.
In the garden the man and his wife __“sewed fig leaves together, and made themselves aprons”__ (Genesis 3:7) — a covering of their own cut and their own measure, what was right in their own eyes.
Scripture marks that road: __“There is a way which seemeth right unto a man, but the end thereof are the ways of death”__ (Proverbs 14:12); __“Every way of a man is right in his own eyes: but the LORD pondereth the hearts”__ (Proverbs 21:2).
God strips the fig leaves off and clothes them Himself, at the cost of blood.
The covering must be cut to His pattern, not to ours.

And the fig tree itself shows what such a covering is worth.
Jesus came to one __“having leaves”__ and __“found nothing thereon, but leaves only”__; He cursed it — __“Let no fruit grow on thee henceforward for ever”__ — and it withered away (Matthew 21:19; Mark 11:13-14).
The leaves are the show; the fruit is the substance.
A tree hung with leaves and bearing no fruit is the fig-leaf covering in one picture — the look of righteousness where the works God came seeking are not.
He is not fed by leaves; He looks for fruit.

=== Keep Your Garments

A garment given can still be defiled, so it must be kept.
The Lord commends the few in Sardis __“which have not defiled their garments,”__ and promises __“they shall walk with me in white”__ (Revelation 3:4); Jude speaks of __“hating even the garment spotted by the flesh”__ (Jude 23); and the watchword for the last hour is just this:

[quote.scripture, Revelation 16:15]
____
Behold, I come as a thief.
Blessed is he that watcheth, and *keepeth his garments*, lest he *walk naked*, and they see his shame.
____

To keep the garment is to go on doing the righteousness it is made of.
This is why the apostles speak of it as something you _put on_: __“put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ”__ (Romans 13:14); __“put on the new man,”__ and with him __“bowels of mercies, kindness… meekness, longsuffering”__ (Colossians 3:10-12); as Job said long before, __“I put on righteousness, and it clothed me”__ (Job 29:14).
The clothing is conduct.
And the wardrobe is God’s own: Isaiah watched Him dress — __“he put on *righteousness* as a breastplate, and an helmet of *salvation* upon his head”__ (Isaiah 59:17) — and Paul issues the same suit to the soldier: the breastplate of righteousness, the helmet of salvation, truth for a belt and the word for a sword (Ephesians 6:14-17).
The armour of God is not a figure borrowed from Rome; it is God’s own clothing from the prophets, lent to the man who will wear it.
A robe is not framed and admired; it is worn, kept clean, and worn again tomorrow.

[discrete]
=== Naked

To be without the garment is to be naked, and nakedness, from the garden onward, is not nudity but exposure — the covenant covering gone, the shame seen.
Laodicea is __“wretched, and miserable, and sym:sym-poor[poor], and blind, and naked”__ and does not feel it (Revelation 3:17); the warning __“lest he walk naked, and they see his shame”__ (Revelation 16:15) is aimed at a way of life, not a state of undress.
When God judges a covenant-breaker, He strips her: __“Thy nakedness shall be uncovered, yea, thy shame shall be seen”__ (Isaiah 47:3).
And Paul’s one dread for the day of resurrection is to be caught uncovered — __“if so be that being clothed we shall not be found naked”__ (2 Corinthians 5:3).
The naked are those with no righteousness to show and no covenant to plead — exactly the man at the wedding, speechless in his bare skin before the king.

So the symbol takes its place with the rest.
The sym:sym-gospel[gospel] is the covenant announced; the sym:sym-way[way], the sym:sym-truth[truth], and the sym:sym-life[life] are the covenant embodied; the sym:sym-name[name] is the covenant owned; and the garment is the covenant _worn_ — righteous acts done, granted in the blood of the Lamb and kept clean to the end.
The marriage of the Lamb is set, and the call has gone out to many.
But __“many are called… few are chosen”__ (Matthew 22:14): the chosen are the few found on the sym:sym-highway[highway] of holiness, properly dressed.
The one who walks some other road, or comes to the feast in a covering of his own cut, is the man without a garment — speechless at the door, and cast into the dark.
