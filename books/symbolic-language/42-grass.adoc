A sym:sym-mountain[mountain] is a kingdom; a sym:sym-tree[tree] is a nation; beneath them both stands the smallest of the three figures, and the shortest-lived:

[quote.scripture, Isaiah 40:6-8]
____
…All *flesh is grass*, and all the goodliness thereof is as the *flower of the* sym:sym-field[field]… The *grass withereth*, the flower fadeth… but the *word of our God shall stand for ever*.
____

Grass is a man — a single mortal, up in the morning and gone by evening. __“As for man, his days are as grass… the wind passeth over it, and it is gone”__ (Psalm 103:15-16); __“in the morning it flourisheth… in the evening it is cut down, and withereth”__ (Psalm 90:5-6).
So the three figures of a people are complete: a _mountain_ is the kingdom, a _tree_ the nation, and the _grass_ the individual who springs up and dies within it.
The New Testament simply hands the definition back: __“all flesh is as grass… The grass withereth”__ (1 Peter 1:24).

[discrete]
=== Grain Is Grass

Grass is not only the picture of frailty; it is also the field God farms.
The grains that feed the world — barley and wheat — are grasses, and with them the symbol turns from the withering blade to the standing crop.

The field is sown, and Jesus names both the seed and the soil: __“The sym:sym-seed[seed] is the word of God”__ (Luke 8:11), scattered on the hearts of men — some hard, some shallow, some good ground that bears.
The seed must die to live:

[quote.scripture, John 12:24, ASV]
____
Except a *grain* of wheat fall into the ground and *die*, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much sym:sym-fruit[fruit].
____

The word buried in a man is a seed that dies and rises into a harvest.

[discrete]
=== The Harvest

Grain is grown to be reaped, and the reaping is judgment.
The prophets fixed that meaning long before the parables — Joel's sickle thrust in because the harvest is ripe (Joel 3:13); the nations gathered __“as the sheaves into the floor”__ to be threshed by Zion (Micah 4:12-13).
So when Jesus says the same, He is not inventing an image but citing one:

[quote.scripture, Matthew 13:39]
____
…the *harvest is the end of the world*; and the *reapers are the angels*.
____

The sym:sym-field[field] is the world, the crop is mankind, and the sym:sym-harvest[harvest] is the day it is cut down.
Revelation reaps the same field with the same words — __“Thrust in thy *sickle*, and reap… for the *harvest of the earth* is ripe”__ (Revelation 14:15) — Joel’s command reissued at the end: one chain from the Prophets through the Gospels to the last book.
The harvest has a present tense too: __“Lift up your eyes, and look on the sym:sym-field[fields]; for they are *white already to harvest*”__ (John 4:35); __“The harvest truly is plenteous, but the *labourers* are few”__ (Matthew 9:37).

[discrete]
=== Wheat, Chaff, and Tares

Two different partings happen at the harvest, and the whole vocabulary is Old Testament.
The first is the grain parted from its own husk.
When the threshed wheat is thrown up into the wind, the light, empty *chaff* is carried off while the heavy grain falls back to the floor — and this is what the wicked are likened to, weightless and rootless: the chaff which the sym:sym-wind[wind] drives away (Psalm 1:4), __“chased as the chaff of the sym:sym-mountain[mountains] before the wind”__ (Isaiah 17:13) — the very kingdoms of the link:/books/symbolic-language/mountain/[Mountain] chapter, blown off like husks.
John the Baptist says it in one breath and expects to be understood: __“he will throughly purge his floor, and gather his wheat into the garner; but he will burn up the chaff with unquenchable fire”__ (Matthew 3:12).

The second parting is between two different plants.
The *tares* are not the wheat's own husk but a weed sown among it — darnel, which *mimics* the wheat so closely that the two cannot be told apart until the ear forms.
That is the enemy's work, and the Teacher's key link:/books/symbolic-language/the-parables-of-the-kingdom/[has already graded it]: the sym:sym-field[field] is the world, the good sym:sym-seed[seed] are the children of the kingdom, and the tares are the children of the wicked one (Matthew 13:38).footnote:fn36tares[Derived and graded in the chapter link:/books/symbolic-language/the-parables-of-the-kingdom/[The Parables of the Kingdom].]

The two are gathered by different means to one end.
The chaff is winnowed off by the wind; the tares are bound in bundles for the fire while the wheat alone is gathered into the barn (Matthew 13:30) — the counterfeit gathered with the counterfeit, the grain kept.
The threshingfloor is the day of judgment, and it misses nothing: neither the empty husk nor the planted lie.

Mark the mercy in the machinery:

[quote.scripture, Amos 9:9, ASV]
____
I will *sift* the house of Israel among all nations, like as *grain* is sifted in a *sieve*, yet shall *not the least grain fall* upon the earth.
____

It is the promise the Lord repeated to Peter under the same figure — sifted as sym:sym-wheat[wheat], but prayed for, and kept (Luke 22:31-32).
The shaking is real; the grain is not lost.

[discrete]
=== The Firstfruits

One grain is reaped before all the rest.
Barley ripens first, in the spring, and the law commanded that its first sheaf be brought and waved before the LORD — __“a sheaf of the firstfruits of your harvest”__ (Leviticus 23:10) — the pledge of the whole harvest to come.
That sheaf is Christ: __“now is Christ risen from the dead, and become the firstfruits of them that slept”__ (1 Corinthians 15:20), raised in the very season of the wave sheaf, the first of a harvest that rises after Him.

Barley is the humble, early grain — the loaf that is Israel tumbling into Midian's camp, the five loaves from which a multitude is fed (Judges 7:13; John 6:9).
The firstfruits are few and lowly, but they carry the whole field in promise.

[discrete]
=== The Leaven

Grain ground and baked becomes bread, and __“man doth not live by sym:sym-bread[bread] only, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of the LORD”__ (Deuteronomy 8:3) — the bread is the word.

What, then, is *leaven*?
It is bread soured.
The very consonants that spell it also spell vinegar, an embittered heart, a cruel man; leaven is the loaf turned impure — fermented, puffed up, gone off.
Leaven link:/books/symbolic-language/the-parables-of-the-kingdom/[is corrupt doctrine] working through the whole lump — purged from every house before Passover, banned from every offering on the altar (Exodus 12:15; Leviticus 2:11; Matthew 16:12; 1 Corinthians 5:6-7).footnote:fn36leaven[Derived in the chapter link:/books/symbolic-language/the-parables-of-the-kingdom/[The Parables of the Kingdom].]
So the bread offered to God is _unleavened_ — the feast kept __“with the *unleavened* sym:sym-bread[bread] *of sincerity and* sym:sym-truth[truth]”__ (1 Corinthians 5:8) — a pure and unsoured word.

[discrete]
=== Ears to Hear

Set it together and the parables come clear.
When Jesus told of a sower, of wheat among tares, of a harvest and a burning, He was not speaking a new tongue; He was speaking the oldest one.
A hearer who knew that flesh is grass, that the harvest is the day of the LORD, that the wicked are chaff and tares and the firstfruits the LORD's own, needed no interpreter — the meaning was written already in the Law and the Prophets.
That is why He could say __“Who hath ears to hear, let him hear”__ (Matthew 13:9), and why His astonishment that His own disciples link:/books/symbolic-language/the-parables-of-the-kingdom/[could not decode the sower] was just (Mark 4:13).footnote:fn36ears[That astonishment, and the exam it implies, is the argument of the chapter link:/books/symbolic-language/the-parables-of-the-kingdom/[The Parables of the Kingdom].]

The words were sown centuries before; the parable only brought in the sheaves.
To read the New Testament by the Old is to reap what was long since planted.
