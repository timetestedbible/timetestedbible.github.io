For my flesh is meat indeed, and my blood is drink indeed.
       — John 6:55

Shortly after feeding the 5,000, Jesus gave a hard teaching: "Except ye eat
the flesh of the Son of man, and drink his blood, ye have no life in you…
He that eateth my flesh, and drinketh my blood, dwelleth in me, and I in
him" (John 6:53, 56). After he said this, many of his disciples murmured —
"This is an hard saying; who can hear it?" — and "from that time many of
his disciples went back, and walked no more with him" (John 6:60, 66). It
was so bad that Jesus turned to the twelve and asked whether they would
also go away.

It was a hard saying because those who heard it did not know the symbolic
language of the scriptures, and took it over-literally, saying: "How can
this man give us his flesh to eat?" (John 6:52). Which prompts the
question: why would Jesus speak in a manner that drives people away? Why
not speak plainly?

He did it to fulfill the scriptures, which prophesied that he would speak
in parables. When he spoke publicly he always used parables — "without a
parable spake he not unto them" (Matthew 13:34) — and only with his
closest disciples did he explain their meaning. Even so, when he gave the
parables of the kingdom his own disciples did not understand, and Jesus
rebuked them: "Know ye not this parable? and how then will ye know all
parables?" (Mark 4:13).

The rebuke was fair because the key to the parable was already in the Old
Testament — the Law and the Prophets — and Jesus expected his disciples to
know them well enough to interpret it. Even this was prophesied: "I will
open my mouth in a parable: I will utter dark sayings of old: which we
have heard and known, and our fathers have told us" (Psalm 78:2-3, quoted
of Jesus in Matthew 13:35).

In other words, scripture prophesied that the message would be encrypted
in parables and decoded with the Old Testament. If you think you can read
the New Testament without an intimate understanding of the Old, chances
are you will walk away like the masses — either by interpreting it in a
way you reject, or by misinterpreting it in a way you can accept, and facing the same consequences either way.

The Bible provides two symbolic words, meat and milk, to describe its
teachings, and if you do not do the work to churn the milk into meat, you
will remain immature at best — and at worst turn away entirely, like the
crowds in Jesus' day.

The purpose of this book is to teach you how I learned to chew the word of
God and test everything. It is a repeatable, reproducible process that has
yielded more pearls of wisdom — and more appreciation for the supernatural
structure of the word of God — than I could possibly fit in one book. We
will go through the process systematically and apply it to dozens of
symbolic words, starting with meat and milk. But first we must prepare the
ground by explaining the difference between a word and a symbol.

## What Is a Symbolic Word?

When you read scripture, you read its words through the lens of your own
private dictionary and personal associations. Those associations include
not just the Webster's meaning but the slang meaning — and, for many who
grew up in church, the taught "seminary meaning." None of these are the
definitions scripture provides.

A symbolic word is a word whose scriptural meaning differs from its
dictionary meaning. When John cries, "Behold the Lamb of God, which
taketh away the sin of the world" (John 1:29), no one reaches for a
dictionary. A lamb is a young sheep; the Lamb is the sacrifice every
Passover pointed to — and every church on earth reads it so without
being told. You already speak this language; the question is only how
much of its vocabulary you were ever taught. The symbol is the idea; the
word is merely its label. And because the meaning lives in the
relationships between passages rather than in the label, it survives
translation: trade the English word for the Hebrew or the Greek and the
pattern still points to the same idea. A word earns a place in this
book's glossary only when Scripture itself fixes its meaning — defined
openly in one passage, used consistently in the rest, contradicted by
none.


The Glossary at the back of this book defines more than 120 symbolic
words, each with scriptural citations and a pointer to the chapter that
carries the full study behind it. How different are these definitions from
what is commonly taught? To measure that gap independently, I used AI as a
proxy for the consensus. A modern AI model is trained on all of scripture,
virtually all published commentary, and every sermon and forum post it can
scrape from the internet — and by its nature it produces the most popular,
most predictable answer to any question you put to it. Ask it what a
biblical symbol means, and you receive, in effect, the average of what is
taught and believed by the majority.

So I ran a blind test. One set of AI agents — Claude Fable 5, the most
advanced model available to me at this writing — was given only the bare
list of terms, with no access to this book, and asked for each term's
generally accepted symbolic meaning and the scriptural basis for it. A
second, independent set of agents then graded this book's glossary against
that consensus, term by term — and tested the citations, looking each
verse up to ask one question: would the glossary's own scriptures carry
its definition for a skeptical reader?

The results:

| Verdict | Meaning | Share |
|---|---|---:|
| Match | the book agrees with what is commonly taught | 28% |
| Refined | compatible with the common reading, but sharper | 43% |
| Divergent | the book contradicts the common teaching | 12% |
| Novel | no consensus exists; the book stakes a claim | 16% |

Read it from the top down and the table is a safety record: roughly seven
of every ten definitions land on, or sharpen, what careful readers have
always held. That is the floor that proves this method is no private
invention — scripture compared with scripture reproduces the sound
conclusions of centuries without any help from me. Then read it from the
bottom up and the table is a promise: three of every ten definitions say
something you have almost certainly never heard taught — twelve percent
overturning the common reading outright, and sixteen percent claiming
ground no tradition has claimed at all. And the reviewing AI, judging from
the citations alone — without the chapters' full arguments — graded the
supporting scriptures strong for the clear majority of definitions and
insufficient for only a handful — and those few rest on Hebrew wordplay
that no bare citation list can carry; their chapters do that work.

The method is safe enough to agree with the church wherever the church has
read carefully — and strong enough to overturn her wherever she has not.

## Supernatural Design of Scripture

The meaning of words never sits still. When the King James translators
wrote that "he who now letteth will let" (2 Thessalonians 2:7), *let*
meant to hinder — the exact opposite of what it means today, so the one
who restrains evil is now misread as the one who permits it. *Prevent*
meant to precede (1 Thessalonians 4:15), a "peculiar people" meant God's
treasured possession (1 Peter 2:9), and "replenish the earth" (Genesis
1:28) rendered a Hebrew word that means simply to fill — yet the modern
sense of filling *again* spawned a doctrine of a former world destroyed
and repopulated that the words never carried. Even *meat* has drifted: it
meant any food at all. Semantic drift happens in every language, and it
compounds in translation as each translator ratifies the drift of his own
day into a new tongue.

And drift is the gentlest of the threats. The serpent's first recorded
words are an attack not on the text but on its meaning — "Yea, hath God
said?" (Genesis 3:1) — and that has been the strategy ever since: what
cannot be deleted can be re-glossed. Change the dictionary, change the
context, change the emphasis, and the words stand untouched while the
message dies. If Scripture stored its meaning in dictionary definitions,
that attack would have succeeded centuries ago.

But meaning is not carried by words; it is carried by the relationships
among words. Modern AI is living proof of the principle: no one feeds a
translation model a dictionary — it learns the meaning of every word in
every language purely from how words relate to one another across
mountains of text, and from those relationships alone it carries an idea
faithfully between languages. Scripture's symbols are built the same way:
the meaning of a symbol is not stored in its label but established by
every place the word is used and every word it stands beside, line upon
line, across the whole of the text. (AI is only the proof of concept —
never the judge, and never the proof of any particular interpretation;
the gathering and testing remain fully in our own hands.)

A meaning stored that way is nearly indestructible. It survives
translation: change all the words, and the relationships among them —
and therefore the symbols they define — remain; the New Testament's Greek
had already passed through one layer of translation before it reached
English, and the symbols still hold. It survives corruption: to re-gloss
a meaning spread across every book, you must consistently rewrite every
occurrence in every author across every century — corrupt one verse and a
hundred witnesses convict the change. The very redundancy that hides the
meaning from the careless guards it from the corrupter. "The grass
withereth, the flower fadeth: but the word of our God shall stand for
ever" (Isaiah 40:8).

And the same redundancy is a signature. Some forty writers, across some
fifteen centuries, in multiple languages, hold one symbol grammar without
a collision: the mountain is a kingdom from Moses to Daniel's Babylon to
John's Patmos; the seed is the word in Isaiah's field and in the boat on
Galilee; the harlot, the vine, the shepherds, the wings keep their
meanings across authors who never met. Beneath that runs a second layer —
the Hebrew letters themselves, where wordplay binds symbol to symbol —
that no committee spanning a millennium could have coordinated and no
forger could retrofit, even with the most advanced computational help.
The relationships are so consistent, and so unexpected, that one cannot
help but conclude the book is supernatural. That consistency is also the
claim this book stakes its method on — a fabricated system would break
somewhere under the weight of every occurrence being gathered and tested;
this one holds when tested.


## What Is Meat?

Jesus defines the symbol out loud, as Scripture defines so many of its
symbols: "My meat is to do the will of him that sent me, and to finish
his work" (John 4:34). Meat is
not a harder topic or a deeper lecture; it is the will of the Father,
done. And what is the will? Scripture answers by recording
one moment twice. His mother and brothers stood outside the crowded house
asking for him, and Jesus answered.

> Whosoever shall **do the will of my Father** which is in heaven, the
> same is my brother, and sister, and mother.
> — Matthew 12:50

> My mother and my brethren are these which **hear the word of God, and
> do it**.
> — Luke 8:21

Same scene, same sentence — one evangelist heard "do the will of my
Father," the other wrote it down as "hear the word of God, and do it."
The will is the word, done: "I delight to do thy will, O my God: yea, thy
law is within my heart" (Psalm 40:8).

Milk is "the sincere milk of the word" (1 Peter 2:2) — the word received.
Scripture fixes who drinks it: babes, the "unskilful in the word of
righteousness," those "not able to bear" meat (Hebrews 5:13;
1 Corinthians 3:2). Nature supplies the picture — and mark that this
step is an observation, not a verse: milk is food another body has
already digested; the infant does no work, it swallows what was broken
down for it, and grows. The scriptural marks agree with the picture —
the milk-drinker has received without practicing, and his diet is good,
and first, and meant to be outgrown: "Whom shall he teach knowledge? and
whom shall he make to understand doctrine? them that are weaned from the
milk" (Isaiah 28:9).

So milk and meat are not two doctrines; they are one substance in two
states — the word received, and the word done. The bridge between them is
work: "the churning of milk bringeth forth butter" (Proverbs 30:33) — the
same milk, worked, sets solid. And Isaiah wrote the outcome of that diet
seven centuries before Hebrews did: "Butter and honey shall he eat, that
he may know to refuse the evil, and choose the good" (Isaiah 7:15) —
which is exactly how Hebrews describes the meat-eater: "those who by
reason of use have their senses exercised to discern both good and evil"
(Hebrews 5:14). Not by reason of hearing. By reason of use.

How do you tell them apart at the table? Job gives the test: "the ear
trieth words, as the mouth tasteth meat" (Job 34:3) — and tasting is
testing; *trieth* is the assayer's word. The test's verdict is even
written out in advance: "O taste and see that the LORD is good" (Psalm
34:8); "Prove all things; hold fast that which is good" (1 Thessalonians
5:21) — the tongue's verdict and the tester's verdict are one word,
*good*, and discerning "both good and evil" (Hebrews 5:14) is precisely
what the meat diet trains. Scripture keeps the register at both tables:
Peter follows the milk of the word with "if so be ye have tasted that the
Lord is gracious" (1 Peter 2:2-3), and Hebrews follows strong meat with
"tasted the good word of God" (Hebrews 6:5). "Prove all things" turns out
to be a dietary instruction.

So: if a teaching can be swallowed without tasting — without checking,
without doing — it is milk, whatever its subject. If its truth opens only
to the one who obeys it — "If any man will do his will, he shall know of
the doctrine" (John 7:17) — it is meat. Milk is fluid: it takes the shape of whatever container
receives it. Poured into the truth, it takes meat's own shape — which is
why milk never contradicts meat. But poured into a false doctrine it sets
just as agreeably, shaped to whatever the hearer wants to hear — "after
their own lusts shall they heap to themselves teachers, having itching
ears" (2 Timothy 4:3) — and that is why the babe stays "tossed to and
fro, and carried about with every wind of doctrine" (Ephesians 4:14)
until he is weaned.

The unpointed Hebrew even watches the table. Milk and fat are the same
three letters — חלב, chalav or cheleb, divided only by vowel points added
centuries later — and the fat belongs to the LORD alone: "all the fat is
the LORD's… ye eat neither fat nor blood" (Leviticus 3:16-17). So when
Ezekiel indicts the false shepherds, the pointed text reads "Ye eat the
fat… but ye feed not the flock" (Ezekiel 34:3) — and the Greek
translation made before the points existed read the same letters as "Ye
feed on the milk." Both readings convict, and together they say
everything: shepherds consuming what belongs to God, drinking down the
babes' first food, and feeding no one.

Now go back to the synagogue at Capernaum, where this book began — and
notice that Jesus began with bread. Bread is the word: "man doth not live
by bread only, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of the
LORD" (Deuteronomy 8:3; Matthew 4:4). In Hebrew the two foods overlap by
design — lechem, bread, is rendered "meat" again and again in the King
James, because both are the word served as food — and Isaiah sets them in
a single verse: the rain makes the word "seed to the sower, and bread to
the eater" (Isaiah 55:10), the same word planted or eaten. So Jesus laid
the word on the table first — "I am the bread of life" (John 6:35) — then
named it the Word made flesh — "the bread that I will give is my flesh"
(John 6:51; John 1:14) — and then finished the climb: "my flesh is meat
indeed" (John 6:55). Bread, flesh, meat: the word, embodied, done. And
beneath the Greek, the Hebrew letters lock the climb in place: flesh is
basar (בשר), and basar is also the verb of the good tidings — the
gospel-word of "him that bringeth good tidings" (Isaiah 52:7); Strong's
derives the noun from the verb. The Word made flesh is the tidings made
edible. The gospel is the meat.

"My flesh is meat indeed" emptied the room — but Jesus had handed out the key
before they left: "the flesh profiteth nothing: the words that I speak
unto you, they are spirit, and they are life" (John 6:63). Eat my flesh
meant take in my words and do them. Peter, asked whether he would leave
too, answered flesh with words — "thou hast the words of eternal life"
(John 6:68). The crowd that walked away had just been fed: full stomachs,
no comprehension. That is milk's failure mode, lived out — they received,
and never chewed.

That is why this book is called *MEAT The Bible's Symbolic Language*. It does not hand you a closed set
of conclusions to swallow; it teaches you to chew — to gather, to taste,
to test, and to do. And say the title aloud and you have the invitation
on its back cover: come — meet the Bible's symbolic language (pun intended, in the spirit of the awe-inspiring wordplay found throughout scripture).
