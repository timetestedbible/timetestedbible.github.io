Josephus keeps two clocks for Herod the Great.
One count runs from the day Rome declared him king; a shorter count runs from the day he actually took Jerusalem and his rival Antigonus was put to death.
Nearly every dated event in Herod's story hangs on which clock a verse is reading — and Josephus, the primary and often the only ancient narrative for the reign, tells us himself.
He states the rule plainest at the moment of Herod's death, where he lays both counts side by side.

[discrete]
=== The Two Counts at Herod's Death

[quote, Josephus, Antiquities 17.8.1 (Whiston)]
____
…having reigned, *since he had procured Antigonus to be slain*, thirty-four years; but *since he had been declared king by the Romans*, thirty-seven.
____

[quote, Josephus, The Jewish War 1.33.8]
____
…having reigned thirty-four years, *since he had caused Antigonus to be slain*, and obtained his kingdom; but thirty-seven years *since he had been made king by the Romans*.
____

Two books, one summary, and each count arrives wearing its own anchor.
The *37-year count* is tied to a formal act — __“declared king by the Romans”__ — the Senate's grant of the title.
The *34-year count* is tied to a conquest — Antigonus slain, Jerusalem taken, the kingdom actually in hand.
Josephus explains neither anchor, because he trusts his reader to know that the grant came first and the possession came later; he simply names which clock each number runs on.
The reign of a king, in his summary, is the thing Rome declared — the shorter figure is the corrective, and it never appears without its label.

[discrete]
=== The Qualifier Pattern

That labeling is not a habit of one sentence; it runs through both works.
Whenever Josephus intends the shorter span, he says so: __“since he had procured Antigonus to be slain,”__ __“from the death of Antigonus,”__ __“since he took Jerusalem.”__
The conquest count travels with a conquest marker, every time.
The unqualified phrases — __“in the reign of Herod,”__ __“in the seventh year of the reign of Herod”__ — carry no such marker, and the narrative shows why: from the Senate's decree onward, Josephus already calls Herod *king* and speaks of him holding *the kingdom* by Roman grant, even through the years he was still fighting for the city.
The title is the reign; the siege merely delivered what the title already named.
Not once in either work does Josephus run the shorter count unmarked.
The asymmetry is the rule: *qualification means the conquest count; no qualification means the count from the Roman declaration.*

The rule survives every objection that can be raised against it from inside the text.
If every ordinal year were secretly a conquest count, the unqualified 37-year figure could never appear — yet it stands in the benchmark itself.
Inclusive or exclusive arithmetic cannot blur the two, because the distinction is anchored in events, not in counting conventions — a declaration in Rome and a death in Jerusalem are not two ways of numbering the same day.
Context can tilt a local Judean matter toward the shorter reckoning, and precisely there Josephus supplies the marker; the summaries and broad statements revert to the default.
He wrote as a historian holding both truths — Rome's legitimacy and Judea's reality — and he kept them from tangling by a discipline as simple as it is consistent.

[discrete]
=== The Rule Josephus Keeps

*An unqualified “reign of Herod” runs from the Roman declaration; the conquest count always arrives wearing its qualifier.*
The rule comes from Josephus' own grammar and structure, with no consuls, eclipses, or coins imported to prop it up — and it gives the rest of the Herodian chronology a consistent lens.
