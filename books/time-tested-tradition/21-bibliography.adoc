The case of this book is tested three ways — against Scripture, against the surviving history, and against the sky — so the library divides the same way.
These are the sources quoted or consulted, and the work each one did.

[discrete]
=== Bible Texts

*King James Version.*
The base text: every quotation is KJV unless its citation names another version.
The Name is rendered as the LORD, as the KJV prints it, wherever the text carries it.

*The Septuagint (LXX).*
The Greek Old Testament, quoted where its readings carry the calendar's case — the first-fruits ordinances of Leviticus 23, the feast texts of Exodus, and the psalm titles that number the days of the week as days of the sabbath — with the passages linked to the full text at https://TimeTested.Bible[TimeTested.Bible].

*Westminster Leningrad Codex.*
The Hebrew Scriptures in the letters the scribes counted.
This book's Hebrew arguments stand on this consonantal text, and where the written text — the kethib — differs from what tradition reads aloud, the letters are quoted as written.

*Ele Ha-Sodot — the Cochin manuscripts, c. 1700.*
Hebrew texts of the Gospels preserved by the Jewish community of Cochin, India — this book's second witness for New Testament wording, cited where a reading turns on the Hebrew beneath the Greek.
The complete interlinear, and the evidence for the text's legitimacy as a witness, is presented at https://TimeTested.Bible[TimeTested.Bible].

*Other English versions.*
Where a more literal or clearer rendering serves, it is quoted and named in its citation: the New American Standard Bible, the English Standard Version, the New King James Version, the Amplified Bible, and the Berean Standard Bible.

=== Ancient Witnesses

*Josephus, Antiquities of the Jews and The Jewish War.*
The book's chief historical witness: the chronology of Herod the Great, the fall of Jerusalem, the temple's practice, and the sabbatical years all stand on his accounts — including direct Greek and Latin witnesses, identified by shelfmark and exact reading where they disagree.

*Benedict Niese (ed.), _Flavii Iosephi Opera_, vol. 4 (Berlin: Weidmann, 1890).*
The Greek text and critical apparatus for _Antiquities_ 18.4.6, p. 160: Greek “twentieth” and “thirty-seven,” with the Latin variants “twenty-second,” “thirty-two,” and “thirty-five” recorded at the disputed words.

*Bamberg, Staatsbibliothek, Msc.Class.78, fol. 223v.*
A Latin _Antiquities_ manuscript from the middle third of the ninth century, directly preserving “the twenty-second year” followed by the separate reign-length reading “thirty-two years.”

*Cassiodorus, _Institutions_ 1.17.1.*
Cassiodorus's own statement that he had Josephus's _Antiquities_ translated into Latin by his associates; evidence for the sixth-century origin of the translation, not a claim that the surviving Bamberg copy was written then.

*Josephus, _Antiquitates Iudaicae_, Latin editions of Venice (1499) and Basel, Froben (1524).*
Pre-1544 printed witnesses to the 22nd-year Latin reading; their differing reign totals are reported rather than silently harmonized with the Greek thirty-seven.

*Philo of Alexandria.*
The first-century witness to the appointed times as Alexandria's Jews kept them.

*The Mishnah, the Babylonian Talmud, Sifrei Devarim, and Rashi.*
The tradition's own testimony, called to establish what the inherited calendar claims for itself — and when its witnesses were compiled.

*The Dead Sea Scrolls.*
The Essene calendar texts: proof that first-century Israel did not agree on one calendar.

*Jubilees, 1 Enoch, Jasher, the Epistle of Barnabas, and the Sibylline Oracles.*
Cited as historical witnesses to what ancient communities taught and practiced, not as Scripture.

*Augustine of Hippo, Letter 102.*
The elder church's sight of the sign of Jonah — the ship, the whale, the sepulchre — and its limit, weighed in The Sign of Jonah.

*The Roman historians.*
Dio Cassius (Roman History — the planetary-hours account of how the days took their names), Appian, and Censorinus.

=== The Sky and the Record

*NASA eclipse calculations and Stellarium.*
The astronomy that makes the book's claims testable: eclipse dates fixed to the day, and the ancient sky reconstructed over any city and date.
The heavens are the one witness no scribe could revise.

*Antonio Pigafetta's journal of the first circumnavigation.*
The day lost at sea — the record that exposes what a fixed dateline assumes.

*Jack Finegan, Handbook of Biblical Chronology (rev. ed. 1998).*
The standard chronological reference, quoted where the traditional case concedes its own difficulties.

*Richard A. Parker, The Calendars of Ancient Egypt (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1950).*
The standard account of the Egyptian lunar month — beginning at the old crescent's morning invisibility — the received consensus this book tests.

*Contemporary teaching quoted for examination.*
Jim Staley, 119 Ministries, Ellen G. White, and Michael Rood — quoted as the positions this book tests, in their own words.
