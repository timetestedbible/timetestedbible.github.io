=== Herod's Appointment

[discrete]
==== Introduction

The appointment of Herod as king by the Roman Senate is a pivotal event in Herodian chronology, marking the start of his regal (de jure) reign.
This chapter focuses exclusively on the direct evidence from Flavius Josephus' accounts in _Antiquities of the Jews_ and _The Jewish War_, without relying on external dates or secondary sources.
We examine the narrative details of Herod's journey to Rome, the appointment itself, and the key assumptions underlying common interpretations.

A central point of contention is Josephus' reference to the consular year of Caius Domitius Calvinus (second time) and Caius Asinius Pollio (first time), traditionally linked to the appointment.
However, a close reading shows this reference pertains to the year when the sequence of events began—the Parthian invasion and Herod's initial flight—not the completion of the appointment.
This distinction counters common statements that pin the appointment directly to that consular year, often overlooking the documented delays in Herod's journey.
By highlighting these delays, we reveal how assumptions about travel speed lead to misattributions.

[discrete]
==== Section 1: Direct Evidence from Josephus – The Journey to Rome

Josephus provides a detailed, step-by-step account of Herod's escape from Judea and journey to Rome, emphasizing obstacles that extended the timeline.
These details are the primary direct evidence, drawn from _Antiquities_ Book 14 and parallel passages in _War_ Book 1.

[discrete]
====== The Parthian Invasion and Initial Flight

The journey begins with the Parthian invasion of Judea, which forced Herod to flee:

[quote, _Antiquities_ 14.14.2]
____
Now after Herod was come into Egypt, he was in distress what to do; because he heard that his brother Joseph was dead...
But as he was in great distress, Cleopatra would not let him go; but encouraged him to undertake another voyage...
So he set sail from thence to Pamphylia.
____

Herod's overland escape route included stops at Masada (to secure his family), Idumea, and through Arabia (where King Malchus initially refused aid), before reaching Alexandria in Egypt.
This phase alone involved significant time—logistical arrangements, negotiations, and travel across hostile terrain—spanning weeks.

[discrete]
====== The Shipwreck and Storm

En route from Alexandria, Herod encountered a severe storm:

[quote, _Antiquities_ 14.14.3]
____
[Herod] sailed away in a storm, which fell upon him as he sailed, with great danger; and he was sorely shaken with the tempest, and was thereby in distress...
So he set sail from thence to Pamphylia: and falling into a violent storm, he had much ado to escape to Rhodes; with the loss of the ship's burden.
____

This "violent storm" caused the loss of cargo and nearly the ship itself, forcing Herod to Rhodes in a damaged state.
The narrative underscores the peril and disruption, implying substantial time lost at sea and upon arrival.

[discrete]
====== Refitting in Rhodes

In Rhodes, Herod faced further delays due to the city's war-damaged state (from Cassius' campaigns) and the need to rebuild his vessel:

[quote, _Antiquities_ 14.14.3]
____
And there it happened that two of his friends, Sappinas and Ptolemeus, met with him.
And as he found that city very much damaged in the war against Cassius, though he were in necessity himself, he neglected not to do it a kindness: but did what he could to recover it to its former state.
He also built there a three-decked ship; and set sail thence, with his friends, for Italy: and came to the port of Brundusium.
____

Herod not only refitted his own ship but aided Rhodes' recovery, sourcing timber, hiring crew, and awaiting supplies.
This refitting process—building a "three-decked ship"—would require months, involving skilled labor, materials procurement, and construction in a post-war environment.

No explicit mention of winter occurs, but the sequence (storm during sailing season, refitting in late year) aligns with ancient Mediterranean travel norms, where voyages halted during mare clausum (closed sea, November–March) due to seasonal storms.

[discrete]
====== Arrival in Rome and the Appointment

Upon reaching Italy (Brundusium), Herod proceeded to Rome:

[quote, _Antiquities_ 14.14.4]
____
And when he was come to Rome, all his friends met him...
So a senate was convocated: and Messala first, and then Atratinus introduced Herod into it...
This seemed good to all the senators: and so they made a decree accordingly.
____

The appointment was swift—Herod obtained the kingship unexpectedly within seven days in Italy.
Antony and Octavian supported him due to prior alliances and strategic needs against the Parthians.

[discrete]
==== Section 2: The Consular Reference – Evidence for the Start of the Journey, Not the Appointment

Josephus provides a single direct reference to the consular year in the context of Herod's appointment:

[quote, _Antiquities_ 14.14.5]
____
And thus did this man receive the Kingdom; having obtained it on the hundred eighty fourth olympiad; when Caius Domitius Calvinus was consul the second time; and Caius Asinius Pollio [the first time.]
____

This statement follows the appointment description but does not explicitly date the Senate's decree itself.
Instead, a plain reading within the narrative flow ties it to the broader sequence of events initiated by the Parthian invasion and Herod's flight—the "obtaining" process began in that consular/Olympiad year.

[discrete]
====== Why the Reference Pertains to the Journey's Start

* *Narrative Placement*: The consular/Olympiad mention comes at the end of the journey account, summarizing the entire process from invasion to appointment.
Josephus often uses such markers for the onset of major sequences (e.g., invasions or campaigns), not isolated endpoints.
* *The "Obtaining" Language*: The phrase "having obtained it" encompasses the full effort—escape, delays, travel, and decree—starting with the crisis in Judea during that year.
* *No Precise Appointment Timestamp*: Josephus does not say "the Senate decreed on [date]" but frames the obtaining within the consular year, implying the year's association with the initiating events (Parthian threat, flight).
* *Parallel Usage in Josephus*: In other contexts (e.g., Pompey's conquest), Josephus uses consular years for campaign starts, allowing flexibility for completions.

[discrete]
====== Countering Common Misstatements

Many interpretations state the appointment occurred in the consular year of Calvinus and Pollio, treating it as the decree's date.
This overlooks:

* The journey's documented duration—weeks of overland travel, storm recovery, months of refitting—making same-year completion improbable without assuming minimal delays.
* Josephus' intent: The reference anchors the story's beginning (invasion/flight), consistent with his historiographical style of marking event origins.

This misstatement arises from assuming instantaneous travel, ignoring Josephus' emphasis on obstacles as narrative evidence of extended time.

[discrete]
==== Section 3: Key Assumptions in Interpreting the Evidence

Interpretations hinge on assumptions about the journey's pace and the consular reference's scope.

[discrete]
====== Assumption 1: Rapid Journey Without Significant Delays

Common views assume Herod's trip—from Judea to Rome—was swift (weeks/months), fitting within one consular year.
However, direct evidence contradicts this:

* Overland phase: 4–6 weeks minimum.
* Sea voyage: Storm-induced losses required full refit.
* Refitting: Building a new ship in war-torn Rhodes implies 3+ months.

This assumption minimizes Josephus' details to force a compressed timeline.

[discrete]
====== Assumption 2: Consular Year Strictly Dates the Appointment

This treats the reference as pinpointing the Senate decree, ignoring narrative context.
Direct evidence shows it frames the "obtaining" process from start (invasion/flight) to end.

[discrete]
====== The Evidence-Based Alternative

Without these assumptions, the journey's delays (storm, refitting) extend the timeline, placing the appointment after the consular year's events began.
This aligns with Josephus' emphasis on hardships as integral to the story.

[discrete]
==== Conclusion

Josephus' direct evidence—detailed journey obstacles—establishes a prolonged process from Parthian crisis to Roman decree.
The consular reference anchors the start of this sequence, not the appointment, countering common misattributions that assume rapid travel.
By discarding key assumptions of haste and precise dating, we gain a chronology faithful to the text: a multi-month ordeal culminating post-delays.
This framework resolves apparent tensions, prioritizing Josephus' narrative over imposed timelines.

[discrete]
=== The Battle of Actium

[discrete]
==== Introduction

Josephus provides a crucial chronological anchor by dating the Battle of Actium (September 2, 31 BC) to Herod's 7th regnal year.
This reference is particularly significant because:

. *It uses unqualified "reign" language* - meaning it refers to regnal years (from appointment), not de facto years (from capture), per the pattern established in link:/books/time-tested-tradition/herod-regal-vs-defacto/[Herod: Regal vs De Facto].

. *It provides an independent test* - The Battle of Actium is a well-established historical date (31 BC) that can verify which regnal year system Josephus is using.

. *It exposes a contradiction* - The 4 BC consensus position cannot make this reference work with their proposed dates without abandoning Josephus's explicit "reign" language.

. *It reveals a foundational assumption* - The 4 BC position's 40 BC appointment date depends on an unproven assumption that Herod completed his journey before winter closed the Mediterranean Sea, despite documented shipwreck and delays.

'''

[discrete]
==== Josephus's Account of the Battle of Actium

Josephus records a major earthquake in Judea that occurred during the Battle of Actium:

[quote, Josephus, Antiquities 15.5.2]
____
Now at this time it was that the fight happened at Actium, between Octavius Caesar and Antony, in the seventh year of the reign of Herod...
____

The earthquake caused significant destruction:

* Approximately 10,000 casualties (Antiquities) or 30,000 (Jewish War)
* Destroyed much of Judea's cattle
* Demolished houses
* Herod's army was spared (encamped in open fields)
* Arab enemies attacked Jewish ambassadors, believing Judea was destroyed

The Battle of Actium itself is historically fixed at *September 2, 31 BC* - one of the most precisely dated events in ancient history, marking the decisive naval battle between Octavian (later Augustus) and Mark Antony.

'''

[discrete]
==== The Foundational Assumption: Journey Timeline

Before addressing the Battle of Actium discrepancy, it's crucial to understand the *foundational assumption* that underlies the 4 BC position's 40 BC appointment date.

[discrete]
====== The 40 BC Appointment Assumption

The traditional 40 BC appointment date *depends entirely* on an unproven assumption: that Herod could complete his entire journey from Alexandria to Rome before winter closed the Mediterranean Sea, *despite documented obstacles*.

[discrete]
====== Documented Obstacles

Josephus records several delays that make a 40 BC completion unlikely:

. *Parthian Invasion Timing*: Invasion escalated around Shavuot (July 1, 40 BC with full moon calendar)
. *Overland Journey*: Escape to Alexandria via Masada, Petra, Rhinocolura (4-6 weeks) → arrival mid-August 40 BC
. *Shipwreck and Storm*:
+
[quote, Antiquities 14.14.3]
____
[Herod] sailed away in a storm, which fell upon him as he sailed, with great danger; and he was sorely shaken with the tempest, and was thereby in distress.
____

. *Refitting in Rhodes*: Hiring crew, sourcing timber, awaiting supplies (3+ months) → extends to late October/early November 40 BC
. *Mare Clausum (Closed Sea)*: Mediterranean winter season began in November - voyages rare and perilous until March

[discrete]
====== The Timeline Reality

* *Mid-August 40 BC*: Arrival in Alexandria
* *Late October/Early November 40 BC*: Refitting complete in Rhodes
* *November 40 BC*: Mare clausum begins - sea closes for winter
* *March 39 BC*: Safe sailing resumes

*Result*: Herod likely *wintered in Rhodes* (as historical parallels like Paul's shipwreck in Acts 27 illustrate the risks).
This pushes the journey completion to *spring 39 BC*, not late 40 BC.

[discrete]
====== The 4 BC Position's Burden

The 4 BC position requires proving that Herod:

* Defied the documented storm and shipwreck
* Completed refitting in less than 3 months
* Risked winter Mediterranean sailing (against all historical precedent)
* Completed the journey before November 40 BC

*All without a shred of direct evidence for such haste.*

This is *speculation built on idealized assumptions* to preserve the 4 BC death consensus, not evidence-based reconstruction.

[discrete]
====== The Book's Position

The book's 39 BC appointment date:

* *Accounts for all documented delays* (storm, refitting, winter)
* *Fits realistic ancient travel timelines*
* *Requires no special pleading* or unproven assumptions
* *Uses the journey timeline as evidence*, not an assumption to overcome

*This foundational assumption is critical*: The 4 BC position's entire timeline rests on an unproven assumption about journey speed, while the book's position uses the documented journey timeline as supporting evidence.

'''

[discrete]
==== The Chronological Test

[discrete]
====== Book's Position (38 BC Regnal Start)

If Herod's first regnal year began in *spring 38 BC* (from appointment in 39 BC):

* *Year 1*: Spring 38 BC to Spring 37 BC
* *Year 2*: Spring 37 BC to Spring 36 BC
* *Year 3*: Spring 36 BC to Spring 35 BC
* *Year 4*: Spring 35 BC to Spring 34 BC
* *Year 5*: Spring 34 BC to Spring 33 BC
* *Year 6*: Spring 33 BC to Spring 32 BC
* *Year 7*: *Spring 32 BC to Spring 31 BC*
* *Year 8*: Spring 31 BC to Spring 30 BC

*Battle of Actium*: September 2, 31 BC

*Result*: The Battle of Actium (September 2, 31 BC) occurs *after* Spring 31 BC (the end of Year 7), placing it in *Year 8* (Spring 31 BC to Spring 30 BC), not Year 7.

*Timeline calculation*:

* Year 7 ends: Spring 31 BC (approximately April/May 31 BC)
* Battle of Actium: September 2, 31 BC
* *Discrepancy*: Approximately *5 months* into Year 8, not a full year

This is an *imperfect fit* - the battle is about 5 months into Year 8, not Year 7 as Josephus states.
However, this is still much closer than the 4 BC position, and the discrepancy may be due to:

* Josephus using approximate/rounded language ("seventh year" as approximate)
* Inclusive counting methods
* The battle occurring near the boundary between Year 7 and Year 8 (only 5 months into Year 8)

Year 7 ended in Spring 33 BC, *over two years before* the battle.
This is a *major discrepancy* - the battle is 3 years off from Josephus's statement.

'''

[discrete]
==== The 4 BC Position's Dilemma

The 4 BC consensus faces a severe contradiction:

. *Josephus explicitly says "seventh year of the reign"* - This is unqualified language, meaning it refers to regnal years (from appointment), not de facto years (from capture), per the pattern established in link:/books/time-tested-tradition/herod-regal-vs-defacto/[Herod: Regal vs De Facto].

. *If they use regnal years from 40 BC*:
* Year 7: Spring 34 BC to Spring 33 BC
* Battle of Actium: September 2, 31 BC (in Year 10)
* *Discrepancy*: Battle is *3 years* after Year 7 ends ✗

. *If they switch to de facto years from 37 BC* (abandoning the explicit "reign" language):
* First de facto year: Spring 36 BC (from fall 37 BC capture, spring-to-spring reckoning)
* Year 7: Spring 30 BC to Spring 29 BC
* Battle of Actium: September 2, 31 BC (in Year 6)
* *Discrepancy*: Battle is *1 year* before Year 7 begins ✗

. *If they use inclusive accession-year counting from 37 BC capture* (treating "reign" as de facto with inclusive counting):
* Year 1 (accession): 37 BC (fall capture)
* Year 2: 36 BC
* Year 3: 35 BC
* Year 4: 34 BC
* Year 5: 33 BC
* Year 6: 32 BC
* Year 7: 31 BC
* Battle of Actium: September 2, 31 BC (in Year 7) ✓
* *However*, this requires:
** Abandoning "reign" = regnal years (contradicts explicit language)
** Using de facto years instead (contradicts "reign" language)
** Using inclusive accession-year counting (deviation from convention)
** *Three deviations from standard practice* to make it work

* Year 1 (accession): 40 BC
* Year 7: 34 BC
* Battle of Actium: September 2, 31 BC (in Year 10)

* The pattern established at Herod's death (37 years = regnal, 34 years = de facto)
* The unqualified "reign" language (which defaults to regnal per link:/books/time-tested-tradition/herod-regal-vs-defacto/[Herod: Regal vs De Facto])

* The foundational principle that unqualified "reign" = regnal years

'''

==== Additional Context: Herod's Relationship with Octavian

After the Battle of Actium, Herod quickly switched allegiance from Antony to Octavian (Augustus).
Josephus records that Herod met with Octavian at Rhodes shortly after Actium, where he successfully secured his position despite having supported Antony.
This meeting likely occurred in 30 BC, during Herod's 8th or 9th regnal year (if counting from 38 BC), which fits the narrative timeline perfectly.

The earthquake during Actium and Herod's subsequent diplomatic pivot to Octavian are part of the same historical sequence, all occurring within the framework of Herod's regnal years as established by his Roman appointment.
