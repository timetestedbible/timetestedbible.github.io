[#atlas-herod-appointment]
Josephus stamps Herod's appointment with a Roman date: the 184th Olympiad, in the consulship of Calvinus and Pollio — the year we call 40 BC.
The received chronology reads that stamp as the date of the Senate's decree, counts Herod's reign from it, and arrives at the familiar 4 BC death.
But Josephus did not record a decree with a date; he recorded a journey — flight, storm, shipwreck, a winter's shipbuilding — and set his time-stamp at the end of the whole account.
This chapter walks that journey at the pace Josephus describes, asks what his consular reference actually dates, and then puts both readings to an independent test: the one event in Herod's reign whose date no one disputes, the Battle of Actium.

[discrete]
=== The Journey to Rome

The story begins with the Parthian invasion of Judea, which escalated around Shavuot — early summer of 40 BC — and drove Herod from Jerusalem.
He moved his family to the fortress of Masada, crossed Idumea, and sought help in Arabia, where King Malchus refused him; only then did he turn toward Egypt, reaching Alexandria after four to six weeks of overland flight through hostile country.
Cleopatra tried to keep him for a campaign of her own; he declined and put to sea late in the season.

[quote, Josephus, _Antiquities_ 14.14.3]
____
[Herod] sailed away in a storm, which fell upon him as he sailed, with great danger; and he was sorely shaken with the tempest, and was thereby in distress…
So he set sail from thence to Pamphylia: and falling into a violent storm, he had much ado to escape to Rhodes; with the loss of the ship's burden.
____

The sea nearly ended the story.
Herod reached Rhodes with his cargo gone and his ship unfit for the crossing to Italy — and Rhodes itself was in no state to help quickly.

[quote, Josephus, _Antiquities_ 14.14.3]
____
And as he found that city very much damaged in the war against Cassius, though he were in necessity himself, he neglected not to do it a kindness: but did what he could to recover it to its former state.
He also built there a three-decked ship; and set sail thence, with his friends, for Italy: and came to the port of Brundusium.
____

A three-decked ship is not an afternoon's repair.
In a city still rebuilding from Cassius' war, sourcing timber, hiring shipwrights, and fitting out a vessel of that class is a work of months — and those months land exactly where the ancient sailing calendar closes.
From roughly November to March the Mediterranean was _mare clausum_, the closed sea; voyages ceased, and the rare captain who defied the season courted the fate Paul's shipwreck in Acts 27 illustrates.
A fugitive who reached Alexandria in late summer, lost his ship's burden in a storm, and then built a new ship in a war-damaged port did not also cross to Italy before winter.
The narrative Josephus wrote has Herod wintering at Rhodes and sailing with the spring.

When the sea opened, the rest moved quickly.

[quote, Josephus, _Antiquities_ 14.14.4]
____
And when he was come to Rome, all his friends met him…
So a senate was convocated: and Messala first, and then Atratinus introduced Herod into it…
This seemed good to all the senators: and so they made a decree accordingly.
____

Antony and Octavian, needing a reliable hand against Parthia, carried the matter through the Senate at once — Herod had his kingship within seven days of landing in Italy.
The delays all belong to the road and the sea, not to Rome; on this evidence the decree falls in the spring of 39 BC.

[discrete]
=== What the Consular Year Dates

Only after telling the whole story does Josephus reach for his date:

[quote, Josephus, _Antiquities_ 14.14.5]
____
And thus did this man receive the Kingdom; having obtained it on the hundred eighty fourth olympiad; when Caius Domitius Calvinus was consul the second time; and Caius Asinius Pollio [the first time.]
____

Notice what the sentence dates.
It does not say the Senate decreed in that year; it says Herod _obtained_ the kingdom — and the obtaining, as Josephus has just spent a chapter showing, ran from the Parthian invasion through the flight, the storm, the winter at Rhodes, and the voyage to Rome.
Josephus habitually sets a consular or Olympiad marker at the head of a sequence — the year a war began, a campaign opened, a crisis broke — and lets the sequence run past it.
The consulship of Calvinus and Pollio dates the year the crisis began and the obtaining started: 40 BC, the invasion and the flight.
Reading it as the decree's own date requires everything the narrative denies — a swift road, a harmless storm, a refit measured in days, and a winter crossing no ancient captain would make.
The traditional 40 BC appointment is not evidence; it is an assumption of haste laid over a story whose every detail testifies to delay.

With the appointment in the spring of 39 BC, Herod's first regnal year begins, on Josephus' spring-to-spring reckoning, in the spring of 38 BC — the epoch this book's chronology runs on.

[discrete]
=== The Battle of Actium

[#atlas-herod-actium]
The reconstruction can now be tested, because Josephus ties one year of Herod's reign to the best-dated event in the ancient world.

[quote, Josephus, _Antiquities_ 15.5.2]
____
Now at this time it was that the fight happened at Actium, between Octavius Caesar and Antony, in the seventh year of the reign of Herod…
____

The occasion for the note is a catastrophe at home: that spring an earthquake shattered Judea, killing ten thousand by the count in _Antiquities_ (thirty thousand in _War_), destroying houses and cattle — Herod's army survived only because it was encamped in open fields, and the Arabs, believing Judea broken, murdered the Jewish ambassadors sent to them.
The battle itself is fixed: September 2, 31 BC, Octavian's fleet against Antony's — one of the most precisely dated events of antiquity, and one that owes nothing to Herodian chronology.
It depends on no eclipse, no death date, no son's reign.
Whatever count Josephus was keeping, this verse lets us check it — and the language he uses is the unqualified __“reign,”__ which, as link:/books/time-tested-tradition/herod-regal-vs-defacto/[Herod: Regal vs De Facto] established, is his regal count from the appointment, not the de facto count from the capture of Jerusalem.

Run the count from each candidate epoch and watch where September of 31 BC lands:

.Where Actium falls under each reckoning
[.prevalence-table%unbreakable, frame=topbot, grid=none, cols="4,3,4", options="header"]
|===
| Reckoning of the seventh year | Its span | Actium — September 2, 31 BC

| Regal from spring 38 BC (this book) | Spring 32 – spring 31 BC | About five months past the seventh year's edge
| Regal from 40 BC (the consensus) | Spring 34 – spring 33 BC | Three years late — the battle falls in the tenth year
| De facto from the consensus's 37 BC capture | Spring 30 – spring 29 BC | A year early — the seventh year has not yet begun
|===

Neither reading is perfect, but they do not miss by the same amount.
On this book's epoch the battle falls about five months past the boundary of the seventh year — the kind of gap a spring-to-spring reckoning produces at a year's edge, or that rounded language absorbs.
On the consensus epoch the battle misses the seventh year by three full years, an error no rounding explains.
The consensus has one escape: abandon the regal reading and count de facto years from the 37 BC capture with inclusive accession-year counting, which does put a seventh year at 31 BC.
But that escape costs three deviations at once — treating Josephus' unqualified __“reign”__ as de facto against his own established pattern, switching count systems mid-argument, and adopting an inclusive accession convention Josephus does not use for the earlier kings.
Three deviations against one small boundary approximation: the test is not close.
If Josephus was rounding, he was rounding from this book's timeline.

The verse also closes the ring of the larger reconstruction.
A regal epoch of spring 38 BC puts Actium at the edge of the seventh year; the capture of Jerusalem __“twenty-seven years to the day”__ after Pompey fixes 36 BC; thirty-four de facto years from 36 BC lands Herod's death in 1 BC.
One epoch, three independent statements of Josephus, no contradictions.

[discrete]
=== After the Battle

Actium ended Antony, and Herod — Antony's man — moved fast.
Josephus records that he sailed to meet Octavian at Rhodes, laid his crown at the victor's feet, and was confirmed in his kingdom despite his history: the meeting falls in 30 BC, Herod's eighth or ninth regal year on this book's count, exactly where the narrative expects it.
The earthquake, the battle, and the pivot to Octavian are one sequence, and they run on the same clock as everything else in his reign — the clock that started when a fugitive who had wintered at Rhodes finally stood before the Senate in the spring of 39 BC.
