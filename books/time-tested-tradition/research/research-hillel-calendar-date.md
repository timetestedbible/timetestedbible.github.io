# Research: Date of the Fixed Calculated Hebrew Calendar (358 vs. 350 AD)

**Question.** Chapter 4 (`04-alleged-authority-of-sanhedrin.adoc`, line ~193) dates the fixed
calculated calendar to **358 AD**; chapter 5 (`05-where-does-the-day-start.adoc`, line ~213) says
**350 AD**. Which is right, and how should the book print it?

**Short answer.** The tradition's own date is **AM 4119 = 358/359 CE** (Seleucid year 670). If one
year must be printed, **359 AD** is the best-supported single figure. "350 AD" is not an attested
date in the literature; it is a rounding of "the 350s" (common in Sabbatarian/Church-of-God
sources) or an echo of Maimonides' looser dating to "the days of Abaye and Rava" (c. 320–350 CE).
Modern scholarship (Sacha Stern) holds even 359 is too early for the calendar as we know it — the
fixed calendar evolved gradually and reached its present form only in the 9th–10th century. That
caveat *strengthens* the book's argument (the calculated calendar came centuries after the temple),
so a light hedge like "traditionally dated to 359 AD" is both accurate and safe.

---

## 1. The traditional attribution: Hillel II, AM 4119 = 358/359 CE

**Claim of the tradition.** The patriarch Hillel II (Hillel ben Yehuda the Nasi) instituted the
fixed calculated calendar in the Jewish year **4119 from creation**, i.e. **358/359 CE** (the AM
year ran autumn 358 → autumn 359). The tradition's own citation is the **Seleucid year 670**, which
likewise spans 358/9 CE.

**Source chain (verified).** The tradition is *not* in the Talmud. It first appears in a
**responsum of R. Hai Gaon** (written 992 CE / AM 4752), which survives because **R. Abraham bar
Hiyya** quoted it in his *Sefer ha-Ibbur* (1123; ed. Filipowski, London 1851, p. 97). It was then
transmitted by later authorities (Nachmanides' glosses to Sefer HaMitzvot; Isaac Israeli's *Yesod
Olam*, Toledo 1310).

- J. Jean Ajdler, "A Short History of the Jewish Fixed Calendar: The Origin of the Molad,"
  *Hakirah* 20 (2015) — <https://hakirah.org/vol20Ajdler.pdf>:
  > "Indeed according to a tradition quoted in the name of R' Hai Gaon, the present Jewish calendar
  > was introduced by the patriarch Hillel II in the Jewish Year 4119 AM (anno mundi, from
  > creation), 358/359 CE."

  Footnote 1: "*Sefer ha-Ibbur* by R' Abraham bar Hiyya edited by Filipowski, London 1851, p. 97
  quotes a responsum of R. Hai Gaon dated from 4752 AM = 992 C.E. reporting this tradition."

  Footnote 15: "the name of Hillel II is not mentioned in the Talmud and it is not certain at all
  that he had a direct part in this calendar. Maimonides does not mention him and is probably not
  aware of the tradition reported by R' Hai Gaon."

- Wikipedia, "Hillel II" — <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillel_II>:
  > "This tradition first appears in a responsum of R. Hai Gaon (written in 992) cited by
  > R. Abraham bar Hiyya in his *Sefer Ha'ibbur* (written in 1123)." The citation gives "670 of the
  > Seleucid era, which corresponds to 358/9 CE." (Stern adds that Hai Gaon's responsum may have
  > attributed only the **19-year cycle**, not the whole calendar, to Hillel.)

- Traditional religious sources print **4119 = 359 CE** as the single year. Peninei Halakha,
  "The Fixed Calendar and the Permanent Enactment" — <https://ph.yhb.org.il/en/12-09-03/>:
  > "Ever since then (4119 in the Jewish calendar, 359 CE in the civil calendar), the Jewish people
  > have kept track of the months and years based on the system set up by Hillel II."

- The classic reconstruction (Jaffe, followed by Ajdler) puts the calendar's inception on
  **Monday, 15 March 359 CE** — the Molad of Nisan 4119, chosen near a solar eclipse that day
  (Ajdler, *Hakirah* 20, pp. 137, 140). So even within the tradition, the operative moment falls in
  **359**, not 358.

**Why sources say "358" vs "359".** AM 4119 and Seleucid 670 both straddle the CE year boundary;
"358/9" is the academically precise form. Writers who pick one year split: 358 (part of the AM year)
or 359 (the Nisan in which the calculated calendar is reckoned to have begun; also Britannica's
"c. 359" and the usual religious-source figure). **359 is the better single year.**

## 2. Where does "350 AD" come from?

No primary or scholarly source dates Hillel II's institution to 350. Two real things lie behind the
figure:

1. **"The 350s AD" rounded down.** Sabbatarian/Church-of-God literature the author's milieu draws
   on uses the decade phrase, e.g. Christian Biblical Church of God, "Calculated Hebrew Calendar
   FAQ" — <https://www.cbcg.org/calendar/calculated-hebrew-calendar-faq.html>: "all of the
   calculations were released in the 350s AD by Hillel the Second." Likewise Frank Nelte, "The
   Origin of the Jewish Calendar" — <https://www.franknelte.net/article.php?article_id=102>: "the
   calculations which Hillel II made public in the 350's A.D. …" A "the 350s" phrase easily hardens
   into "350 AD" in later retelling.

2. **Maimonides' alternative dating.** Rambam (Hilkhot Kiddush HaChodesh 5:3 —
   <https://www.chabad.org/library/article_cdo/aid/947922/jewish/Kiddush-HaChodesh-Chapter-Five.htm>)
   never mentions Hillel II; he dates the switch to calculation to "the days of **Abaye and Rava**"
   (c. 320–350 CE), when "the land of Israel was destroyed, and no permanent court was left." Some
   writers render that as "c. 350."

So "350 AD" is not a defensible printed date; it should be corrected to match chapter 4.

## 3. The scholarly caveat (Sacha Stern): the fixed calendar came even later

Modern scholarship holds the calendar was **not** fixed in one act in Hillel II's day; it evolved
gradually and reached its present form only in the 9th–10th century.

- Sacha Stern, *Calendar and Community: A History of the Jewish Calendar, 2nd Century BCE – 10th
  Century CE* (Oxford, 2001) — <https://global.oup.com/academic/product/calendar-and-community-9780198270348>.
  Stern, writing on the Cambridge Genizah blog (2024) —
  <https://genizahfragments.lib.cam.ac.uk/2024/03/02/bad-calculations-another-missing-link-in-the-history-of-the-rabbinic-calendar/>:
  > the fixed rabbinic calendar "was instituted at some point in the ninth century" and "its
  > attribution to a Hillel in the mid-fourth century is a medieval tradition that has long been
  > disproved in modern scholarship."

- Key evidence (Ajdler, *Hakirah* 20; Wikipedia "Hillel II"):
  - A calculated-but-semi-empirical calendar was already being sent from the court of Tiberias to
    Babylonia "from about 325 onwards" (Ajdler p. 137) — i.e. calculation predates 359, but not the
    present rules.
  - The Talmud shows Rosh Hashanah could still fall on Sunday in the time of Rav Yemar (427–432;
    B. Niddah 67b) — impossible under today's rules.
  - A Cairo Geniza letter from a Babylonian exilarch shows the year **835/6 CE** laid out
    differently from the present calendar (Ajdler p. 134: "almost five hundred years after the
    inception of the fixed calendar of Hillel, the fixed calendar in its present-day form had still
    not yet been instituted").
  - The **Saadia Gaon – Ben Meir controversy of 921–924** proves the rules were still disputed
    then; Ajdler: "our modern calendar in its final form was definitively not instituted before
    922-924."
  - The current 19-year leap cycle (3-6-8-11-14-17-19) and the 29d 12h 793-ḥalakim molad were
    "probably adopted at about the end of the eighth century" (Ajdler n. 19, citing Stern 2001,
    pp. 197, 204).

**Bearing on the book.** The book's argument needs only "the calculated calendar came centuries
after the temple." That holds on *every* reckoning: the earliest traditional date (359 CE) is ~290
years after the temple fell, and the calendar as actually used today is 500–850 years later still.
The scholarship is an ally, not a threat — worth a footnote, not a fight.

## 4. Recommendation

**Print 359 AD in both chapters**, hedged as traditional:

- Chapter 4 (line ~193): "…the fixed calculated calendar **traditionally instituted in 359 AD**…"
  (or "traditionally dated to 358/359 AD"). The clause "over 200 years after the Sanhedrin was
  disbanded" remains true and if anything understated.
- Chapter 5 (line ~213): "…the Jews have used a calculated calendar going all the way back to
  **359 AD** (by traditional reckoning)…"

Optional strengthening footnote (either chapter): *The traditional date rests on a responsum of Hai
Gaon (992 AD) quoted by Abraham bar Hiyya (1123 AD); the Talmud never credits Hillel II with the
calendar. Modern scholarship (Sacha Stern, Calendar and Community, Oxford 2001) finds even 359 too
early: Geniza documents show the calendar's present rules were still unsettled in 835/6 AD and were
finalized only after the Saadia–Ben Meir controversy of 921–924 AD. On any reckoning, the
calculated calendar postdates the temple by centuries.*

If the author prefers zero hedging in the body text, "359 AD" alone is the single defensible year;
avoid 350 (unattested) and prefer 359 over 358 (the AM-4119 Nisan inception, and the year religious
sources themselves print).
