# Evidence Tree: Herod, the Nativity, and the AD 32 Crucifixion

## Purpose

This document organizes the complete chronological argument as a dependency tree. It distinguishes source observations from calculations, interpretations, corroboration, and proposed historical identifications. That distinction matters because several observations may support one intermediate date without becoming independent proofs of the final conclusion.

The argument being tested is:

```text
Herod died in 1 BC
  -> Jesus was born in spring 2 BC
  -> John began in fall AD 28
  -> Jesus was baptized shortly before Passover AD 29
  -> Jesus proclaimed the Year of Favor in fall AD 29
  -> John 2's first Passover was AD 30
  -> John's three explicit Passovers end in AD 32
  -> the crucifixion occurred at Passover AD 32
  -> AD 32 becomes a fixed test of the competing calendar models
```

## Evidence labels

- **[P] Primary observation** — wording or data found directly in an ancient text, inscription, coin, or astronomical event.
- **[A] External anchor** — a date fixed without depending upon the conclusion under examination.
- **[D] Deduction** — arithmetic or a necessary chronological consequence.
- **[C] Corroboration** — evidence that fits a conclusion but does not establish it independently.
- **[H] Historical hypothesis** — a plausible identification or reconstruction not stated directly by a surviving source.
- **[I] Interpretation** — an exegetical, linguistic, calendrical, or typological reading.
- **[R] Rival reading** — a serious alternative that must be weighed.
- **[!] Dependency warning** — a point that must not be counted again as independent evidence downstream.

## Branch A — Tiberius, John, Jesus, and the age-thirty threshold

### A1. Tiberius's fifteenth year

```text
[P/A] Augustus dies August 19, AD 14.
  Sources: Tacitus, Suetonius, Cassius Dio, Josephus.

[P/A] Tiberius succeeds Augustus as emperor in AD 14.
  +-- Roman histories calculate his sole reign from this transition.
  +-- Josephus's total reign also measures from the AD 14 succession.

[D] Fifteenth regnal year:
  AD 14 accession -> fifteenth year begins in late AD 28
                  -> continues into late AD 29.

[P] Luke 3:1-2 places the beginning of John's mission in that year.

[D] Hard chronological window:
  John begins sometime within approximately late AD 28-late AD 29.
```

The standard accession count does not, by itself, prove that John began in fall AD 28 rather than later in the regnal year. Fall AD 28 becomes the narrower result when the birth and age branches are added.

### A2. Jesus was approaching thirty

```text
[P] Luke 3:23:
  Jesus was "beginning" (archomenos), "about thirty" (hosei eton triakonta).

[I] Linguistic direction:
  +-- "Ministry" is supplied by many English translations; no such noun
  |   appears in the Greek clause.
  +-- ἦν + ἀρχόμενος can be read as "was beginning," allowing the sense
  |   that Jesus was approaching the thirty-year threshold.
  `-- hosei by itself expresses approximation; it does not independently
      specify whether the number is approached from below or above.

[P/C] Early reception:
  +-- Irenaeus, Against Heresies 2.22.5, reports the understanding that
  |   Jesus had not completed thirty at baptism.
  `-- Epiphanius later calculates baptism at twenty-nine years, ten months.

[I/C] Cultural and scriptural setting:
  +-- Numbers 4 repeatedly uses thirty for entry into full Tabernacle duty.
  +-- Joseph stands before Pharaoh at thirty (Genesis 41:46).
  +-- David begins to reign at thirty (2 Samuel 5:4).
  `-- Thirty therefore functions as a meaningful service and authority
      threshold, not merely a loosely rounded biographical number.

[D] Best-supported reading:
  Jesus was close to, but had not yet completed, thirty at baptism.
```

**Qualification:** Numbers 8:24 and 1 Chronicles 23:24, 27 give lower ages for other forms or periods of Levitical service. The evidence supports a significant thirty-year threshold; it does not establish a universal rule that every priestly or prophetic act was legally forbidden before thirty.

### A3. The reconstructed birth and ministry sequence

```text
[P] Luke 1 places John's conception approximately six months before Jesus's.
[P] Luke identifies John as the son of the priest Zechariah.

[I] Ministry-age application:
  John's call is aligned with the thirty-year full-service pattern in
  Numbers 4, although Luke never explicitly states John's age at his call.

[D/H] If Jesus was born near Passover 2 BC:
  +-- John was born approximately fall 3 BC.
  +-- John reaches thirty approximately fall AD 28.
  +-- This coincides with the opening of Tiberius's fifteenth year.
  +-- Jesus remains twenty-nine until approximately Passover AD 29.
  `-- A baptism shortly before that Passover fits Luke's "beginning/about 30."

[P] Jesus undergoes forty days of wilderness testing after baptism.

[D/I] Proposed order:
  fall 3 BC       John born
  spring 2 BC     Jesus born
  fall AD 28      John turns thirty and begins
  early spring 29 Jesus baptized shortly before thirty
  spring 29       Jesus reaches thirty during/near the preparation interval
  fall AD 29      Luke 4 Year-of-Favor proclamation
```

**Independence assessment:** Tiberius's fifteenth year is an independent Roman anchor. The exact fall AD 28 start, spring 2 BC birth, and pre-Passover AD 29 baptism form a mutually reinforcing synthesis rather than three independent facts.

**Wording limit:** “John began at thirty in fall AD 28” is the result of the six-month birth relation plus the service-age interpretation. The direct statement is that John began within Tiberius's fifteenth year.

## Branch B — Herod's capture, reign, age, eclipse, and death

### B1. Jerusalem captured in 36 BC

```text
[P/A] Pompey captured Jerusalem in 63 BC.
  +-- Fixed by Roman consular chronology and multiple ancient accounts.

[P] Josephus states that Herod took Jerusalem on the same fast day
    after twenty-seven years.

[D] Elapsed-year arithmetic:
  63 BC -> 36 BC = twenty-seven elapsed years.

[D] Proposed result:
  Herod captured Jerusalem on the Day of Atonement in 36 BC.
```

#### Evidence that must be reconciled

```text
[P/R] Josephus also names the consuls Agrippa and Gallus: 37 BC.
[P/R] Josephus places the event in the 185th Olympiad: 40-36 BC.

[H] TTT reconciliation:
  Josephus sometimes dates a narrated campaign from its beginning.
  The consular/Olympiad markers may therefore anchor the siege's beginning,
  while "twenty-seven years on the same day" dates its completion.

[R] Conventional reconciliation:
  The immediate grammar can be read as dating the capture itself to 37 BC,
  with "twenty-seven years" handled inclusively or approximately.

[weight]
  TTT gives the same-day elapsed interval priority because it is the more
  specific relative measurement. The consular marker remains the strongest
  objection and must not be represented as though it were absent.
```

### B2. Regnal spans lead from 36 BC to 1 BC

```text
[P] Josephus gives two reign totals at Herod's death:
  +-- thirty-seven years from Roman appointment;
  `-- thirty-four years from capture of Jerusalem.

[D] From the proposed local chronology:
  capture:              fall 36 BC
  first effective year: spring 35 BC
  thirty-fourth year:   spring 2 BC-spring 1 BC
  death:                early 1 BC

[D] From the proposed Roman chronology:
  appointment:          spring 39 BC [H]
  first full year:      spring 38 BC
  thirty-seventh year:  spring 2 BC-spring 1 BC
  death:                early 1 BC

[C] Both reign totals converge on the same result if the proposed start
    dates and spring-to-spring, non-accession counting are accepted.
```

**Dependency warning:** The two reign totals are two measurements in the same Josephan summary. They cross-check two proposed starting points, but they are not wholly independent witnesses.

### B3. Roman appointment and Josephus's narrative compression

```text
[P] Josephus narrates:
  Parthian crisis -> Herod's flight -> Alexandria -> storm at sea
  -> damaged/refitted ship at Rhodes -> Rome -> Senate appointment.

[A] The crisis begins in the consular setting of 40 BC.

[H] TTT journey reconstruction:
  +-- The overland flight, storm damage, Rhodes refit, and winter navigation
  |   can extend the journey into spring 39 BC.
  `-- Josephus's concluding consular reference can characterize the whole
      obtaining-of-the-kingdom narrative from its beginning rather than
      date only the Senate vote.

[P/C] Josephus demonstrably inserts earlier family history retrospectively
      elsewhere in the same narrative complex.

[D/H] Proposed appointment:
  spring 39 BC -> first full regnal year spring 38 BC.

[R] Immediate-subject reading:
  The consular statement most naturally modifies Herod's reception of the
  kingdom in Rome and therefore dates the Senate action to 40 BC.

[weight]
  A 39 BC appointment is a harmonizing reconstruction supported by the
  narrated travel obstacles and Josephus's compression pattern. It is not
  an explicit date printed by Josephus.
```

### B3a. Battle of Actium as a relative-fit test

```text
[P] Josephus associates the Battle of Actium with Herod's seventh year.
[A] Actium occurred September 2, 31 BC.

[D/C] TTT regnal model:
  first full regnal year spring 38 BC
  -> year 7 = spring 32-spring 31 BC
  -> Actium falls approximately five months into year 8.

[D/R] Conventional Roman-appointment model:
  first regnal year spring 40 BC
  -> year 7 = spring 34-spring 33 BC
  -> Actium falls in approximately year 10.

[weight]
  Neither spring-to-spring calculation is exact. The TTT model is much closer
  and therefore receives relative support, but Actium is consistency evidence,
  not a clean independent discriminator that lands exactly in year 7.
```

### B3b. Josephus's 107-year high-priest summary

```text
[P] Josephus counts twenty-eight high priests and 107 years from the
    "days of Herod" until Titus captured and burned Jerusalem in AD 70.

[D/C] A span beginning around Herod's proposed Roman regnal era in 38 BC
      approaches 107 elapsed years to AD 70.

[R] The first Herodian high-priest appointment follows the capture of
    Jerusalem, and different start points plus inclusive counting allow
    both major chronologies to approach Josephus's rounded total.

[weight]
  The figure fits the TTT framework somewhat better when "days of Herod"
  refers broadly to his regnal era, but the book itself recognizes that the
  number is close enough to fit both models. It is low-weight consistency
  evidence, not an independent proof of 1 BC.
```

### B4. Herod's age as a constraint

```text
[P] Antiquities 14.9.2:
  Antipater appointed Herod over Galilee when Herod was fifteen.

[P] Antiquities 17.6.1 / War 1.33.1:
  Herod was nearly/about seventy during his final illness.

[D] If death = 1 BC:
  birth approximately 71-70 BC
  -> age fifteen approximately 56-55 BC.

[A] Independent Roman chronology:
  Gabinius restores Ptolemy XII in 55 BC and then settles Judea.

[P] Josephus, War 1.8.7:
  Gabinius settled the government as Antipater wanted.

[H/C] Identification:
  This 55 BC Antipater-directed settlement is the closest specifically
  attested office-making setting for Antipater's appointment of young Herod.

[P/C] Josephus later narrates Caesar's elevation of Antipater and Herod's
      mature judicial and military activity in 47 BC.

[D/C] With a birth around 70 BC, Herod is approximately twenty-three in
      47 BC, fitting the later Sanhedrin/Sextus/promotion phase better than
      an initial boyhood commission.
```

#### Fair comparison

```text
1 BC reconstruction:
  near seventy -> born ~71-70 -> fifteen in ~56-55
  -> intersects the Antipater-directed settlement of 55 BC.

4 BC reconstruction:
  near seventy -> born ~74-73 -> fifteen in ~59-58
  -> can appeal to Gabinius's broader council reorganization around 57 BC,
     or can widen "near seventy" so that a 55 BC commission yields age ~66.

Assessment:
  The age evidence fits 1 BC more tightly, but the 55 BC appointment remains
  a cumulative identification; no surviving source states its date directly.
```

### B5. The eclipse and the interval before Passover

```text
[P] Josephus reports a lunar eclipse on the night after the executions.
[P] He then narrates Herod's worsening illness, travel, attempted suicide,
    death, funeral, public mourning, and events before the next Passover.

[A/R] Other eclipses in the broad candidate window include total eclipses
      in March and September 5 BC. The March eclipse leaves a short interval;
      the September eclipse leaves roughly seven months and requires a death
      chronology earlier than either principal model.

[A] Principal 4 BC candidate: March 13
  +-- shallow partial eclipse;
  `-- approximately twenty-nine days before the commonly reconstructed
      Passover.

[A] Principal 1 BC candidate: January 9-10
  +-- deep total eclipse, conspicuous for approximately 99 minutes total;
  +-- approximately ninety-two days before the commonly reconstructed
  |   Passover;
  `-- TTT's selected intercalation/calendar can extend the interval to
      approximately 114 days.

[D/C] The 1 BC eclipse supplies ample room for Josephus's intervening events.
[D/R] The 4 BC interval is possible only with a compressed sequence or a
      different calendar placement of Passover.

[weight]
  The eclipse does not identify the year by itself because Josephus gives no
  absolute date. Its conspicuity and the narrative interval favor 1 BC once
  the two candidates are compared.
```

### B6. Philip's death notice and coins

```text
[P/R] Extant Greek text tradition:
  Philip dies in Tiberius's twentieth year after a thirty-seven-year reign.
  -> commonly supports the 4 BC Herodian chronology.

[P/R] Early Latin tradition:
  Philip dies in Tiberius's twenty-second year.
  -> attested by the ninth century and in pre-1544 printed Latin editions.

[P] Latin reign totals vary: thirty-two, thirty-five, and twenty-two.
[P] Greek text gives thirty-seven years.
[P/C] Philip issued a Year 37 coin.

[H/C] TTT reconstruction:
  Latin twenty-second-year death notice + Greek/coin-supported Year 37
  -> a chronology compatible with Herod's death in 1 BC.

[!]
  No witness presently verified contains both "twenty-second year" and
  "thirty-seven years" together. The reconstruction combines independently
  transmitted elements and must be presented as such.

[weight]
  The manuscript evidence removes any claim that the twentieth-year reading
  is uncontested. It does not make the combined 22/37 reading a directly
  preserved text.
```

### B7. Nativity and enrollment convergence

```text
[P] Luke places Jesus's birth while Herod is alive.
[P] Luke describes an apographe that brings Joseph and Mary to Bethlehem.
[P] Josephus records a Judean oath to Caesar and Herod near Herod's end.
[P/A] Res Gestae 35 records Augustus as Father of the Fatherland in 2 BC.
[P/A] OGIS 532 records a broad Paphlagonian loyalty oath in a date formula
      convertible to March 3 or March 2 BC, depending on reckoning.

[H] Proposed administrative connection:
  Luke's enrollment may belong to the same empire-wide loyalty setting.

[D/H] Proposed result:
  journey to Bethlehem in early spring 2 BC
  -> Jesus born near Passover 2 BC
  -> John born six months earlier in fall 3 BC.

[C] This converges with the Tiberius/age-thirty calculation.

[!]
  No surviving source explicitly says Luke's enrollment was this oath.
  OGIS 532 does not mention Judea, ancestral-city travel, or an apographe.
  The connection is plausible explanatory fit, not direct identification.
```

## Branch C — The forty-six-year Temple bridge

This is the crucial bridge between the Herodian chronology and the first Passover in John's Gospel.

```text
[D] Herod captures Jerusalem: fall 36 BC.
  `-- First full effective year: spring 35 BC.

[P] Josephus, Antiquities 15.380:
  Herod undertakes the Temple project in the eighteenth year.

[I] Contextual choice of reign:
  eighteenth year from effective possession of Jerusalem.

[D] Year calculation:
  year 1  = spring 35-spring 34 BC
  year 18 = spring 18-spring 17 BC.

[P] Josephus says Herod first gathered materials, wagons, workers,
    priestly garments, and trained one thousand priests.

[H] Construction start:
  announcement/preparation in approximately 18 BC
  -> demolition and physical reconstruction approximately spring 17 BC.

[P] John 2:13, 20 places the forty-six-year statement at Passover.

[D] No-year-zero arithmetic:
  spring 17 BC -> spring 1 BC = 16 years
  spring 1 BC  -> spring AD 1 = 1 year
  spring AD 1  -> spring AD 30 = 29 years
  total                           46 years.

[D] Result:
  John 2's Passover = spring AD 30.
```

### What this branch proves and what it assumes

- It is not independent of the 36 BC capture and the chosen interpretation of Herod's eighteenth year.
- Josephus directly establishes preparation before demolition, but he does not say that preparation lasted exactly one year. Spring 17 BC is a reasoned earliest construction date.
- Once those upstream decisions are accepted, the forty-six-year arithmetic gives a strong and precise AD 30 result.
- This branch should appear under Herod's chronology and feed the crucifixion date; it should not be counted again as a separate independent proof of both.

## Branch D — John's three explicit Passovers

```text
[P] John 2:13
  Passover at the Temple forty-six-year statement.
  `-- AD 30 from Branch C.

[P] John 6:4
  A later Passover during Jesus's ministry.
  `-- AD 31 at the earliest.

[P] John 11:55; 12-19
  The final Passover at which Jesus dies.
  `-- AD 32 at the earliest.

[D/I] Consecutive-Passover sequence:
  first explicit Passover   AD 30
  second explicit Passover  AD 31
  crucifixion Passover      AD 32.
```

John 5:1's unnamed feast is not needed for this count. Treating it as another Passover would lengthen the ministry. Likewise, John does not explicitly say that no Passover occurred between the three named feasts. The three texts establish **AD 32 as the earliest crucifixion year** once John 2 is fixed to AD 30. The exact AD 32 conclusion additionally adopts the economical narrative reading that the named Passovers are consecutive and that John 5:1 is not another Passover.

### Why AD 30 is excluded

```text
Tiberius year 15 begins late AD 28
  -> John must begin after that point
  -> Jesus is baptized after John's appearance
  -> Jesus's first John-narrated Passover cannot precede that sequence
  -> Temple chronology places that first Passover in AD 30
  -> two later annual Passovers remain
  -> crucifixion cannot be AD 30 or AD 31
  -> AD 32 is the earliest date satisfying the full tree
  -> no omitted Passover yields the exact AD 32 result.
```

An AD 30 crucifixion therefore requires at least one major alteration:

1. count Tiberius from an otherwise unexpressed earlier co-regency;
2. move John and Jesus earlier than the ordinary fifteenth-year sequence;
3. reinterpret the forty-six Temple years; or
4. collapse explicitly distinct Passovers into the same year.

## Branch E — Convergence on AD 29 and AD 32

### E1. The sabbatical cycle and the Year of Favor

```text
[P/A] Solomon's Temple construction is placed approximately 966 BC.
[P] 1 Kings 6:1 places it 480 years after the Exodus.
[D] Exodus approximately 1446 BC.
[P] Israel remains in the wilderness forty years.
[D] Jordan crossing approximately 1406 BC.

[I] Cycle assumption:
  the land-sabbath count begins with entry into the land and remains
  recoverable as a continuous seven-year cycle.

[P/C] Josephus calls Herod's Jerusalem capture a sabbatical year.
[D/C] The proposed 36/35 BC capture aligns with the reconstructed cycle.
[D] Continuing the same cycle identifies AD 29 as a seventh year.

[P] Luke 4 joins Isaiah's favorable year with proclamation of liberty.
[I] Deuteronomy 15 and Leviticus 25 connect release/liberty with sabbatical
    and Jubilee conclusions.
[I/C] Jesus's Nazareth proclamation therefore fits fall AD 29.
```

**Dependency warning:** If the 36/35 BC capture is used to calibrate the sabbatical cycle, that alignment cannot then be counted as an independent proof of the capture. The branch becomes more independent only to the extent that the 1406 BC phase and other ancient sabbatical notices fix the cycle without Herod.

### E2. Daniel's 490 years

```text
[A] Artaxerxes I accedes in late 465 BC.
[A/D] Persian accession-year reckoning:
  accession period to Nisan 464 BC
  -> year 1 begins Nisan 464
  -> year 7 begins Nisan 458 BC.

[P] Ezra 7 places Ezra's departure and royal authorization in year 7.
[D] Decree/journey date: spring-summer 458 BC.

[I] Daniel 9:24's seventy sevens are applied inclusively to the atonement
    accomplished by Jesus.

[D] Inclusive count:
  year 1 begins spring 458 BC
  -> year 490 begins spring AD 32
  -> the atoning death falls by Passover AD 32.
```

The 458 BC Persian date can be investigated independently. Its application as the first of Daniel's 490 years, the choice of Ezra's decree, and the inclusive interpretation of the atonement endpoint are exegetical decisions. This is strong convergence if those decisions are established, but it is not a bare astronomical date.

### E3. Astronomical and transmitted darkness reports

```text
[P] Synoptic Gospels report darkness from noon until approximately 3 PM.

[P/C] Thallus survives only through a later quotation saying the darkness
      was explained as an eclipse.

[P/C] Phlegon survives through later quotations associating an extraordinary
      eclipse and earthquake with the fourth year of the 202nd Olympiad
      (approximately July AD 32-July AD 33).

[A] Astronomical calculation:
  a solar eclipse occurred April 28, AD 32.
  +-- greatest eclipse occurred elsewhere;
  +-- Jerusalem's small partial phase occurred in the morning;
  `-- it cannot naturally explain three hours of midday darkness.

[I/C] Under TTT's full-moon-start calendar, the proposed Passover falls on
      the same civil date as the independently calculated eclipse.

[weight]
  This is date-symbolism and calendar corroboration, not a physical
  explanation of the Gospel darkness and not an independent proof by itself.
```

## Branch F — Calendar consequences after AD 32 is fixed

The calendar test must be downstream of the chronology. Otherwise, choosing AD 32 because it fits a calendar and then using AD 32 to prove that calendar would be circular.

```text
[upstream conclusion]
Roman date + Herodian Temple date + three Passovers -> AD 32.

[P/I] Passion chronology requires the proposed Passover weekday and the
      Gospel's burial/resurrection interval.

[P] Josephus places First Fruits on the sixteenth of the first month.

[A] Astronomical lunar phases for AD 32 are calculable independently.

[test]
  +-- dark-conjunction month start
  +-- first-visible-crescent month start
  `-- full-moon/morning month start

[D]
  Calculate the fourteenth, sixteenth, weekly position, and eclipse relation
  under each model without changing the already-fixed year.

[conclusion claimed by TTT]
  The full-moon/lunar-Sabbath model gives the most coherent fit to the AD 32
  Passion date and the book's other historical calendar tests.
```

This downstream test can discriminate among calendar models only if the following are independently defended:

1. AD 32 is fixed without using the desired calendar result.
2. The Passion weekday and inclusive day count are correctly reconstructed.
3. First Fruits was fixed to the sixteenth in the relevant Temple practice.
4. The astronomical phase calculations are performed consistently for Jerusalem.
5. The same month-start rules are applied to every historical test rather than changed case by case.

## Branch G — Calendar plurality, sectarian power, and survivorship

This branch governs how the surviving evidence may be used. It does not identify the Temple calendar directly. It tests whether later majority practice can be treated as proof of universal first-century practice.

### G1. Multiple calendars existed in Second-Temple Judaism

```text
[P] The calendrical and priestly-course documents among the Dead Sea Scrolls
    preserve a schematic 364-day system (including 4Q320-4Q330).

[P] Jubilees 6:32-38 polemicizes against a competing lunar reckoning.
    A polemic against another calendar presupposes calendrical disagreement.

[P] 1 Enoch 72-82 preserves another detailed astronomical/calendar scheme.

[P] Other Jewish evidence reflects lunisolar month systems and locally
    determined dates.

[D] The Second-Temple evidence is not calendrically uniform.

[D] Therefore no calendar can be assigned to every Jewish sect merely
    because it later became dominant or left the most surviving records.
```

The existence of competing systems is direct evidence of plurality. Whether every preserved schematic calendar was continuously implemented in ordinary community life is a separate question and is not required for the narrower conclusion that calendrical uniformity cannot be presumed.

### G2. Josephus documents unequal sectarian influence

```text
[P] Josephus, Antiquities 13.10.6:
  +-- Pharisees transmitted ancestral observances not written in Moses.
  +-- Sadducees rejected those observances and limited obligation to the
  |   written law.
  +-- Sadducees persuaded chiefly the rich.
  `-- Pharisees had the multitude on their side.

[P] Josephus, Antiquities 18.17:
  +-- Sadducean doctrine was held by few, including people of high dignity.
  `-- When Sadducees held office, they followed Pharisaic positions because
      the population would not otherwise tolerate them.

[P/C] Acts 5:17 associates the high priest's circle with the Sadducees.
[P/C] Josephus, Antiquities 20.199, identifies the high priest Ananus with
      the Sadducean position.

[D] The sources support a real social asymmetry:
  popular influence leaned Pharisaic, while Sadducees were strongly
  represented among aristocratic and priestly officeholders.
```

- [Josephus, _Antiquities_ 13.10.6](https://www.sefaria.org/The_Antiquities_of_the_Jews.13.10.6)
- [Josephus, _Antiquities_ 18.17](https://lexundria.com/j_aj/18.17/wst)

**Qualification:** “Sadducee” and “priest” are not interchangeable categories. Not every priest was a Sadducee, and not every Sadducee is shown to have been a priest. The evidence supports strong Sadducean representation among high-status and priestly leadership, not total identity between the groups.

### G3. Public observance did not necessarily reveal private interpretation

```text
[P] Josephus explicitly says Sadducean officeholders sometimes conformed to
    Pharisaic positions because of pressure from the multitude.

[D] An observed public practice can therefore document accommodation rather
    than the officeholder's own preferred interpretation.

[D] Even evidence of a public Temple observance cannot automatically prove:
  +-- that every priest agreed with it;
  +-- that the Sadducean interpretation was identical to it; or
  `-- that no minority or internal priestly calendar existed.
```

This is not speculation imposed upon Josephus; accommodation under popular pressure is the behavior he directly describes. Applying that observation to a particular calendar remains an inference and must be supported by calendrical evidence.

### G4. Destruction created an asymmetric surviving record

```text
[P] Rome destroyed the Temple and its institutional center in AD 70.

[P/I] The Sadducean movement, whose influence was closely connected with the
      Temple and aristocratic office, does not continue as the principal
      transmitter of later Jewish law.

[P/I] Rabbinic literature preserves and develops traditions substantially
      continuous with the Pharisaic stream.

[I] Expected survivorship effect:
  +-- the popular, dispersed, adaptable stream produces later communities;
  +-- the centralized Temple stream loses its institution and archives;
  `-- the surviving corpus becomes weighted toward the surviving stream.

[D] Later numerical dominance of Pharisaic/rabbinic evidence is therefore
    not an unbiased sample of all earlier sectarian or Temple practice.
```

“Survivorship bias” is a methodological diagnosis, not a newly discovered ancient quotation. Its factual basis is the destruction of the Temple establishment, the disappearance of the Sadducees as an independently transmitting institution, and the survival of the rabbinic stream.

### G5. What the branch establishes

```text
Multiple calendars
  + unequal sectarian popularity
  + documented Sadducean accommodation
  + destruction of the Temple establishment
  + later rabbinic survivorship
  ------------------------------------------------
  = later rabbinic uniformity cannot establish universal earlier uniformity.
```

This blocks the following argument:

```text
Most surviving later Jewish evidence uses practice X.
Therefore every first-century sect and the Temple used practice X.
```

That inference commits both a composition error and a survivorship-selection error. It moves without evidence from a later surviving majority to every member of an earlier diverse population.

### G6. What the branch does not establish

```text
It does not, by itself, prove:
  +-- that the Sadducees used a lunar Sabbath;
  +-- that the Temple began months at the full moon;
  +-- that every public Temple rite concealed a different priestly view; or
  `-- that the later rabbinic evidence is historically worthless.

It does establish:
  +-- later tradition cannot be treated as universal by default;
  +-- absence from the surviving majority corpus is not disproof;
  `-- TTT's positive calendar case must be tested on its own primary,
      scriptural, chronological, and astronomical evidence.
```

The branch therefore functions as an evidentiary control. It removes “the later majority did otherwise” as a sufficient refutation, but it does not replace the positive branches of the argument.

## Independence map

### Genuinely separate starting anchors

1. **Roman imperial chronology** — Augustus's death and Tiberius's accession.
2. **Pompeian chronology** — the externally fixed 63 BC capture of Jerusalem.
3. **Josephus's Herodian notices** — relative intervals, reign totals, ages, eclipse narrative, and Temple year.
4. **Lukan chronology** — Tiberius's fifteenth year, the six-month birth relation, Jesus's age wording, and narrative sequence.
5. **Johannine chronology** — forty-six Temple years and three named Passovers.
6. **Persian chronology** — Artaxerxes's accession and seventh year.
7. **Astronomy** — independently calculated lunar and solar events.
8. **Second-Temple sectarian evidence** — competing calendar texts and Josephus's description of Pharisaic and Sadducean influence.

### Related observations that should not be double-counted

- Herod's thirty-four- and thirty-seven-year totals are paired measurements from the same death summary.
- The 36 BC capture, spring 35 BC first year, Temple eighteenth year, construction start, and AD 30 Passover form one dependent chain.
- A spring 2 BC birth, John turning thirty in fall AD 28, and Jesus being under thirty in spring AD 29 are one birth-age chain.
- The Year 37 coin and the thirty-seven-year Greek reign total corroborate the same relative regnal number.
- AD 30, AD 31, and AD 32 are not three independent proofs; they are the consecutive result of three distinct Gospel Passovers after the first is dated.
- The AD 32 solar eclipse supports the calendar/date fit only after the chronology and month-start model are supplied.
- Calendar plurality and survivorship defeat a presumption of uniformity; they do not independently identify TTT's proposed Temple calendar.

## Exact arithmetic checks

Ancient chronology crosses directly from 1 BC to AD 1; there is no historical year zero.

```text
Pompey to Herod:
  63 BC -> 36 BC = 27 elapsed years.

Herod's effective reign:
  spring 35 BC -> spring 1 BC = 34 elapsed years.

Herod's Roman reign:
  spring 38 BC -> spring 1 BC = 37 elapsed years.

Jesus's age:
  spring 2 BC -> spring AD 29 = 30 elapsed years.
  Therefore a baptism shortly before the spring anniversary is < 30.

John's age:
  fall 3 BC -> fall AD 28 = 30 elapsed years.

Temple:
  spring 17 BC -> spring AD 30 = 46 elapsed years.

Daniel inclusive year:
  year 1 begins spring 458 BC;
  year 490 begins spring AD 32.
```

## Claims that require especially careful wording

1. **Do not say that Josephus explicitly dates Herod's appointment to 39 BC.** Say that the journey reconstruction places the Senate action in spring 39 BC.
2. **Do not say that a source explicitly appoints Herod over Galilee in 55 BC.** Say that his two ages imply the mid-50s and the 55 BC Antipater-directed settlement supplies the closest Roman-dated setting.
3. **Do not say that a surviving Philip manuscript reads 22/37.** Say that the Latin tradition supplies twenty-two, while the Greek tradition and coin support thirty-seven.
4. **Do not say that Luke explicitly calls the enrollment a loyalty oath.** Say that the oath and enrollment evidence forms a plausible 3-2 BC administrative convergence.
5. **Do not say that _hosei_ alone means "less than."** The under-thirty reading rests on the whole construction, early reception, cultural threshold, and chronology.
6. **Do not say that Scripture universally forbids sacred work before thirty.** Numbers 4 gives thirty for full Tabernacle duty, while other passages give lower Levitical thresholds.
7. **Do not call the AD 32 eclipse a natural explanation for the Gospel darkness.** Its Jerusalem phase was small and in the morning; the argument concerns date alignment.
8. **Do not count the Temple branch twice.** It is the single bridge from the Herodian chronology to the AD 30 Passover and then to AD 32.
9. **Do not call every converging observation independent.** Independence belongs to the starting anchors, not every downstream calculation.
10. **Do not say that John explicitly excludes intervening Passovers.** AD 32 is the earliest result; the exact result adopts the consecutive-feast reading.
11. **Do not use survivorship bias as direct proof of the lost practice.** It explains why the surviving corpus may be unrepresentative; the content of the proposed Temple practice still requires positive evidence.
12. **Do not equate every priest with the Sadducees.** The sources establish substantial Sadducean association with high-status and priestly leadership, not complete identity.

## Bottom-line formulation

The strongest form of the case is cumulative and comparative:

> Roman evidence places Tiberius's fifteenth year in late AD 28-29, when Luke says John began. Luke's age construction, its early reception, and the reconstructed spring 2 BC birth place Jesus's baptism shortly before his thirtieth birthday in spring AD 29. Independently, Josephus's twenty-seven-year interval from Pompey's 63 BC capture places Herod's capture of Jerusalem in 36 BC; his reign totals, age notices, eclipse narrative, and the alternative Philip tradition collectively favor a death in 1 BC. That Herodian chronology places the Temple project in 18/17 BC, so John's forty-six-year statement identifies the first explicit Passover as AD 30. John's next two explicit Passovers make AD 32 the earliest crucifixion year, and the consecutive-feast reading identifies it exactly. The 458 BC decree count, the AD 29 sabbatical reconstruction, and the AD 32 astronomical signs corroborate the result with varying degrees of dependence and interpretation. Once AD 32 is fixed by the chronological branches, it can be used—without circularity—to test the competing calendar models. Finally, directly attested calendar plurality, Josephus's account of Pharisaic popular power and Sadducean accommodation, the destruction of the Temple establishment, and rabbinic survivorship prevent later majority practice from being treated as proof of universal earlier Temple practice.
