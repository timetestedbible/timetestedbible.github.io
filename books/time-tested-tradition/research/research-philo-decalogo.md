# Research: Philo, De Decalogo 96 — the cities that counted the seventh from the moon (2026-08-06)

Purpose: full documentation behind the TTT chapter-10 section "The Cities
That Counted the Seventh from the Moon." Greek text from Cohn's critical
edition (Cohn–Wendland vol. IV, pp. 290–291) via the Open Greek and Latin
First1KGreek digitization; English base translation Yonge. All apparatus
entries quoted verbatim from Cohn.

## The text

**Greek (Cohn), Decal. 96:**

> Τέταρτόν ἐστι παράγγελμα τὸ περὶ τῆς ἱερᾶς ἑβδόμης, ἵν᾿ εὐαγῶς καὶ ὁσίως
> ἄγηται. ταύτην ἔνιαι μὲν τῶν πόλεων ἑορτάζουσιν ἅπαξ τοῦ μηνὸς ἀπὸ τῆς
> κατὰ θεὸν νουμηνίας διαριθμούμεναι, τὸ δὲ Ἰουδαίων ἔθνος συνεχῶς
> [ἡμέρας] ἓξ διαλείποντες αἰεί.

("ἡμέρας" is Mangey's addition; the manuscripts read "leaving six between."
The feminine participle διαριθμούμεναι is Mangey's correction of the
manuscripts' masculine διαριθμούμενοι.)

**English (Yonge), ¶96:** "The fourth commandment has reference to the
sacred seventh day, that it may be passed in a sacred and holy manner. Now
some states keep the holy festival only once in the month, counting from
the new moon, as a day sacred to God; but the nation of the Jews keep
every seventh day regularly, after each interval of six days."

Context: De Decalogo expounds the Ten Commandments in order; §§82–95 are
the third commandment (oaths — §95 ends on the perjurer being defiled);
§96 opens the fourth, and this one sentence is everything Philo says about
who keeps the seventh and how; §§97–98 give the creation rationale.

## The apparatus (Cohn IV, p. 290, line 8), verbatim

> κατὰ θεὸν corruptum; κατὰ σελήνην vel κατὰ σεληνιακὸν κύκλον (cf.
> 252,12) conicio, κατὰ ζ′ coni. Christophorson (τῆς νουμηνίας κατὰ ζ′
> Mang.); διαριθμούμεναι Mang.: διαριθμούμενοι codd.

Facts established:

1. **The manuscripts are unanimous**: every Greek manuscript and the
   Armenian version read κατὰ θεόν ("according to God"). The
   "corruption" is an editorial judgment about sense, not a variant.
   Cohn kept κατὰ θεόν in his printed text; all repairs live in the notes.
2. **Every proposed repair runs toward the moon or toward sevens**:
   Christophorson (16th c.) κατὰ ζ′ ("by seven"); Mangey (18th c.) τῆς
   νουμηνίας κατὰ ζ′ ("from the new moon by seven"); Cohn (1902) κατὰ
   σελήνην ("by the moon") or κατὰ σεληνιακὸν κύκλον ("by the lunar
   cycle").
3. **αἰεί ("always") is secure.** The page's "αἰεὶ om. DR" entry binds to
   page-line 1 (§95's closing sentence, the oath passage); §96's αἰεί at
   line 9 shows only the spelling variant ἀεί (ms F) and διαλιπόντες (MF).

## Basis of the repairs

- Diagnosed fault (sense only): διαριθμούμεναι ("counting") begs a
  counting standard; "the new moon according to God" answers with
  theology where the syntax wants arithmetic; Philo nowhere else calls a
  noumenia "kata theon."
- Cohn's basis — Philo's own idiom: his "cf. 252,12" resolves to **De
  vita Mosis II.223** (same volume, p. 252 line 12): τῷ δὴ μηνὶ τούτῳ
  περὶ τεσσαρεσκαιδεκάτην ἡμέραν, μέλλοντος τοῦ **σεληνιακοῦ κύκλου**
  γίνεσθαι **πλησιφαοῦς**, ἄγεται τὰ διαβατήρια … τὸ Χαλδαϊστὶ λεγόμενον
  Πάσχα — "in this month, about the fourteenth day, when the lunar cycle
  is about to become full-lighted, the Crossing-feast is kept… called in
  Chaldean Pascha." Weak paleography (ΣΕΛΗΝΗΝ ≉ ΘΕΟΝ), hence apparatus
  only.
- Christophorson/Mangey's basis — paleography: numerals are letters with
  a stroke (ζ′ = 7); θεόν is a nomen sacrum abbreviation (θ̄ν̄). Path:
  numeral ζ′ misread as θ (confusable in minuscule) → θ′ falsely expanded
  to θεόν. Two standard error classes stacked; no direct Philonic
  parallel for the phrase.

## Three full translations (differences bolded)

A — manuscripts as transmitted: "…some of the cities celebrate it once in
the month, counting from the new moon **as fixed by God**; but the nation
of the Judeans keeps it continuously, leaving six [days] between, always."

B — Cohn's conjecture: "…counting from the new moon **by the moon / by
the lunar cycle**…" (i.e., the astronomical month-head, not a drifted
civil calendar).

C — Christophorson/Mangey: "…celebrate it, counting **by sevens** from
the new moon, once in the month…"

### The two construals of "once in the month" under C

- C1 — "once" modifies the celebrating: one seventh-day feast per month,
  the month's 7th day. Matches the Greek civic hebdomē (Apollo's day;
  Hesiod, Works and Days 770–771; Aristobulus apud Eusebius, Praep. Ev.
  13.12 already equates Hesiod's "holy seventh" with the Sabbath).
  Adverb-next-to-verb favors this.
- C2 — "once" modifies the counting: the cities **re-anchor the count
  once each month** at the new moon, then count by sevens through it —
  the 7th, 14th, 21st, 28th — while the Judean nation runs its sevens
  continuously, never re-anchoring. Word order permits it (the phrase
  sits between verb and participle). Under C2 the contrast is two
  arithmetics of the same seventh: anchored sevens vs unanchored sevens.
- Guard: ἅπαξ means "once," never "every" (idiom: ἅπαξ τοῦ ἐνιαυτοῦ,
  "once in the year," LXX Ex 30:10; Heb 9:7). "Every 7th within the
  month" is available only via C2's construal of a conjectural reading.

## Grammar notes

- **ταύτην** — "THIS [sacred seventh]": the antecedent is the fourth
  commandment's seventh. Philo equates the cities' moon-counted
  observance and the commandment's seventh as one institution with two
  reckonings.
- **τὸ Ἰουδαίων ἔθνος** — "the nation of the Judeans": his precise
  ethnos-term; Philo uses "Hebrews" (Ἑβραῖοι) for the ancients and the
  language. The cities are not called pagan in the sentence.
- **κατὰ θεόν** stands in attributive position (τῆς κατὰ θεὸν
  νουμηνίας): it modifies the NEW MOON, not the festival. Yonge's "as a
  day sacred to God" relocates it.

## Noumenia is a phase-open term

νουμηνία = "new-MONTH day" (νέος + μήν): the technical term for the day
heading the month; σελήνη (the luminary) is absent from Decal. 96, and
"month" in "once of the month" is μηνός. The LXX renders chodesh with
noumenia (1 Sam 20:5, 18; Num 28:11), so the Greek inherits whatever
chodesh denotes; the phase question passes through untouched. Proof the
term needed glossing: Spec. Leg. II.41 lists "the noumenia **after the
conjunction** (μετὰ σύνοδον) according to the moon" — a gloss that would
be redundant if noumenia inherently meant conjunction-day. And that
anchor-gloss is itself unstable: κατὰ σελήνην R : κατά τινα σελήνην F :
κατά τινας σελήνην M; καθ᾿ ἑκάστην σελήνην coni. Mang.; Nicetas' excerpt
omits μετὰ σύνοδον … νέαν entirely. **In both places where Philo defines
a month-anchor, the anchor-words are the least stable words in the
sentence.**

Related LXX datum: Ps 81:3 [LXX 80:4] renders the keseh clause ἐν εὐσήμῳ
ἡμέρᾳ ἑορτῆς — "on the WELL-MARKED day of our feast" (εὔσημος,
conspicuous): the translators heard the feast-day as the visible-sign
day.

## The honest counterweight

Philo's own community: month anchored after the conjunction (Spec. II.41)
with Pascha "about the fourteenth day, when the lunar cycle is about to
become full-lighted" (Mos. II.223), and the seventh kept συνεχῶς … αἰεί
("continuously… always") at six-day intervals (Decal. 96). Within Philo,
the majority Judean reckoning is conjunction-anchored; the moon-counted
seventh belongs to "some cities," and Philo does not say how those cities
defined their noumenia. The month-boundary behavior of the Judean count
(what happens at new moon days) is not narrated anywhere in Philo.

## Assessment (the witness profile)

Philo (~20 BC–50 AD; De Decalogo written within Jesus's generation) is a
hostile witness in the technical sense: his own nation kept the other
reckoning; he reports the cities' practice in passing, without polemic,
at civic scale (ἔνιαι τῶν πόλεων — whole cities, plural), and identifies
it as THE sacred seventh of the fourth commandment (ταύτην). Minimum
defensible claim: **two reckonings of the same holy seventh coexisted in
the first century, one anchored to the new moon, practiced at the scale
of whole cities — attested by a writer whose own community kept the
other.** The steelman (cities = Apollo's hebdomē) must surrender either
the manuscripts (whose "according to God" is nonsense for Apollo) or the
emendations (which spell out moon/sevens counting); it cannot keep both.
