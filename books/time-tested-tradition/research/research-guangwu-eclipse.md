# Research: the Guangwu (Jianwu 7) "eclipse" record and the crucifixion darkness

**Date:** 2026-07-13
**Question:** Does the 7th year of Emperor Guangwu correspond to AD 31 or AD 32, and does the
Hou Hanshu eclipse/darkness record support a 31 AD event, a 32 AD event, or neither?
**Chapter under review:** `18-appointed-times.adoc`, subsection "Chinese Records of Cross
Eclipse" (lines ~1064–1111), plus the dependent "dog didn't bark" paragraph (lines ~1056–1062).

**Bottom line:** Jianwu 7 = AD 31 (the book's year math is right), but the record itself is the
ordinary, modern-computation-verified **annular solar eclipse of 10 May AD 31** — a new-moon
event five to six weeks after Passover, in the wrong year now that the crucifixion is proven to
AD 32, and dressed in English "quotes" that are partly mistranslated and partly nonexistent in
the Chinese source. **Recommendation: (c) remove the section.** Details below.

---

## 1. The claim as the book states it

The chapter quotes four passages attributed to the "History of Latter Han Dynasty" (Hou Hanshu
後漢書), Vol. 1, Chronicles of Emperor Guang Wu, 7th year, and "Annals №18":

1. "Yin and Yang have mistakenly switched, and the sun and moon were eclipsed. The sins of all
   the people are now on one man. Pardon is proclaimed to all under heaven."
2. "In the day of Gui Hai, the last day of the (3rd) month, there was a solar eclipse. [The
   emperor] avoided the Throne Room, suspended all military activities and did not handle
   official business for five days."
3. "Eclipse on the day of Gui Hai, Man from Heaven died."
4. "During the reign of Emperor Guang Wu, on the day of Bing Yin of the fourth month of Jian Wu,
   a halo–a rainbow–encircled the sun."

The book then argues:

- "By inclusive counting, the 7th year of Guang Wu is 31 AD." (Correct — see §2.)
- The Chinese year "occurred at a New Moon near the winter solstice"; the first new moon after
  the winter solstice of 30 AD was ~Jan 11, so "the last day of the 3rd month would have been
  around April 10th." (Wrong for the Han era — see §2.)
- NASA shows no eclipse in the "third month," so the recorded third-month darkness "was likely
  the supernatural eclipse at the cross," and the May 10 eclipse was a separate fourth-month
  event; since two eclipses one month apart are "impossible," the record is anomalous; since the
  darkness fell on the *last* day of a dark-moon-start Chinese month, Passover fell on the dark
  phase, so the Hebrew month must start at full moon.
- The section header ties it to "Passover week in 31 AD."

The claims trace to the author's own Medium article,
[The Practically Indisputable Case for 31 AD Crucifixion of Jesus](https://bytemaster.medium.com/practically-indisputable-case-for-31-ad-jesus-crucifixion-and-2024-jubilee-968af9b51a30),
and before that to apologetics literature (see §5).

## 2. Era conversion: Jianwu 7 = AD 31 (civil year ≈ 11 Feb AD 31 – late Jan AD 32)

- Guangwu proclaimed himself emperor **5 August AD 25**; the Jianwu 建武 era runs AD 25–56, so
  by inclusive regnal counting Jianwu 1 = 25, Jianwu 7 = **31**.
  ([Wikipedia: Emperor Guangwu of Han](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Guangwu_of_Han))
- Regnal years follow the Chinese civil lunisolar year. Since the Taichu reform of 104 BC the
  Han civil year begins **not** at the first new moon after the winter solstice (that is the old
  Zhou-era "Yin calendar" convention the book quoted) but on the new moon nearest Lichun —
  "typically the second and sometimes third new moon after the winter solstice," i.e. late
  January–February, the same convention as the modern Chinese New Year.
  ([Wikipedia: Chinese calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_calendar))
- Therefore Jianwu 7 month 1 began ≈ **11 February AD 31** (new moon), and the months ran:
  month 1 ≈ Feb 11–Mar 12, month 2 ≈ Mar 13–Apr 10, month 3 ≈ Apr 11–**May 10**. The last day
  (hui 晦) of the third month = **10 May AD 31**.
- The book's "April 10" figure came from starting the year one lunation too early (Jan 11). The
  record itself arbitrates: the text dates the eclipse to sexagenary day **guihai 癸亥 (day 60)**,
  and 10 May AD 31 *is* a guihai day and *is* an actual eclipse day (§3). April 10 is neither.
- The Julian range of Jianwu 7 is thus roughly **11 Feb AD 31 – 30 Jan AD 32**. Nothing in
  Jianwu 7's spring can be a 32 AD event; spring AD 32 is Jianwu 8.

## 3. The record is the natural annular eclipse of 10 May AD 31 — verified by sinologists

**The original annals text** (Hou Hanshu, Annals of Guangwu, Jianwu 7;
[ctext.org, 光武帝紀下](https://ctext.org/hou-han-shu/guang-wu-di-ji-xia)):

> 癸亥晦，日有食之，避正殿，寑兵，不聽事五日。詔曰：「吾德薄致災，謫見日月，戰慄恐懼…」
>
> "On the guihai day, the last day of the month, the sun was eclipsed. [The emperor] avoided the
> main hall, stood down the troops, and did not attend to business for five days. An edict said:
> 'My meager virtue has brought down calamity; the reproof is seen in the sun and moon. I
> tremble in fear…'"

**The treatise text** (Hou Hanshu, Treatise 18, Five Phases part 6 — the book's "Annals №18";
[ctext.org, 五行六](https://ctext.org/hou-han-shu/wu-xing-liu)):

> 七年三月癸亥晦，日有蝕之，在畢五度。畢為邊兵。秋，隗囂反，侵安定。…
>
> "Seventh year, third month, guihai, last day: the sun was eclipsed, at 5 du in the Net (Bi 畢,
> the Hyades). The Net governs border troops. In autumn, Wei Xiao rebelled and invaded Anding…"

**The scholarly identification.** David Pankenier, "On the Reliability of Han Dynasty Solar
Eclipse Records," *Journal of Astronomical History and Heritage* 15(3) (2012), pp. 200–212
([PDF](https://www.lehigh.edu/~dwp0/Assets/images/Pankenier%20eclipses%20JAH2.pdf)), Later Han
table, entry #4:

> **31 May 10 | 0.721 | 04850** — "VII.3 癸亥[60]晦 (5 in Net); observable at Luoyang. ✓"

That is: Jianwu 7, month 3, sexagenary day 60 (guihai), last day of the month = **10 May AD 31**,
magnitude **0.721 at the Han capital Luoyang**, NASA catalog eclipse **#04850**, check-marked as
confirmed by modern computation. Pankenier's paper concludes the Han eclipse records are
remarkably reliable, with eclipses falling on hui (last day) or shuo (first day) of the lunar
month — i.e., at conjunction, as expected for dark-moon month starts.

Independent corroborations of the identification:

- The recorded solar position "5 du in the Net (Hyades)" fits the sun's early-May position in
  AD 31 (precession-adjusted); in late March–early April the sun was nowhere near the Hyades.
- The recorded weekday-independent sexagenary day guihai (60) lands on 10 May AD 31.
- The prognostication attached in the treatise ("border troops") was read as fulfilled by Wei
  Xiao's rebellion that same autumn — a mundane domestic omen-reading, with no hint that the
  scribes considered the event anomalous beyond the usual eclipse ritual.

Observations:

- **#04850 (10 May AD 31, annular, greatest at 41N 136E — Sea of Japan)** swept northeast Asia;
  at Luoyang it was a deep 72% partial. This is *exactly* the Hou Hanshu Jianwu 7/3/guihai
  record. It is the only eclipse visible in China in Jianwu 7.
- **AD 32 has only four small partial eclipses, all near the poles** (Greenland, far Southern
  Ocean, arctic Siberia at magnitude 0.05). None was visible in China or in Judea. The Hou
  Hanshu accordingly records **no eclipse at all in Jianwu 8 (= AD 32)** — Pankenier's table
  jumps from 31 May 10 straight to 40 Apr 30.
- Incidentally, #04852 and #04853 (29 Mar and 28 Apr AD 32) are exactly one lunation apart —
  the book's claim that two solar eclipses one month apart are "impossible" is false as stated
  (they simply can't both be central at the same place). The point is moot anyway, because the
  book's "second eclipse" never existed: the fourth-month bingyin record is a **sun halo**
  (a separate entry in the treatise, a 暈/rainbow-ring phenomenon), not an eclipse.

## 5. The English "quotes" are apologetics artifacts

- **"The sins of all the people are now on one man"** is the summer amnesty edict, dated
  fourth month, renwu day (about a month after the eclipse), Annals of Guangwu:
  夏四月壬午，詔曰：「比陰陽錯謬，日月薄食。**百姓有過，在予一人**，大赦天下。」 — "Recently yin
  and yang have gone awry and sun and moon were dimmed/eclipsed. If the people have faults,
  **the responsibility rests on me, the One Man**. A great amnesty is proclaimed to all under
  heaven." 「在予一人」 ("on me, the One Man") is the standard classical formula of imperial
  self-blame — *yu yiren* 予一人 is the emperor's own ancient self-designation, echoing the
  Shangshu/Analects formula "If the myriad regions have sin, it lies on me, the One Man." An
  eclipse was an omen against the ruler; taking the blame and declaring an amnesty was the stock
  Confucian response. It is the emperor talking about **himself**, not a report of a distant
  crucified redeemer.
- **"Eclipse on the day of Gui Hai, Man from Heaven died"** — no such sentence exists in the
  treatise entry (quoted in full in §3: eclipse position, "the Net governs border troops," Wei
  Xiao's rebellion). The line appears only in apologetics retellings and cannot be traced to the
  Chinese text.
- **The halo "quote"** is genuine but is a *halo*, a different phenomenon in a different entry.
- The circulating English package traces to Chan Kei Thong, *Faith of Our Fathers: God in
  Ancient China* (2006) and derivative blog posts
  ([Global China Center review](https://www.globalchinacenter.org/analysis/2007/03/20/faith-of-our-fathers-god-in-ancient-china),
  [creation.com defense](https://creation.com/defending-faith-of-our-fathers),
  [example circulation 1](https://proselyteofyah.wordpress.com/2022/02/23/historical-evidence-of-the-crucifixion-darkness/),
  [example circulation 2](https://theunapologeticgeneration.wordpress.com/2019/09/27/the-case-of-christ-and-the-chinese-emperor/)).
  Even sympathetic reviewers of Thong fault him for "unduly read[ing] biblical ideas into
  historical events and documents." None of these sources supplies a scholarly translation, the
  original Chinese, or a calendar conversion.

## 6. Why the argument fails on every leg

1. **Year:** Jianwu 7 = AD 31. The book's own crucifixion year is now proven to **AD 32**
   (spring, Passover), which falls in Jianwu 8 — a year in which the Hou Hanshu records **no
   eclipse and no darkness whatsoever**, matching NASA (no eclipse visible in China in AD 32).
   The record cannot be "amended to 32"; there is nothing in the Chinese archive for 32.
2. **Date within the year:** with the correct Han calendar, the "last day of the third month,
   guihai" is 10 May AD 31 — five to six weeks after any Passover reckoning (traditional Nisan 14
   ≈ late March AD 31; even a late intercalated Passover ≈ late April). It is not Passover week.
3. **Nature of the event:** it was a computed, catalogued, natural **annular eclipse at new
   moon** (NASA #04850), a 72% partial at the capital — an event the court astronomers treated
   with routine omen protocol. It is not an anomalous three-hour darkness; a 0.72-magnitude
   partial does not even noticeably darken the sky.
4. **The "no third-month eclipse" anomaly** that made the record look supernatural was an
   artifact of starting the Chinese year one lunation early (the pre-Han Zhou convention instead
   of the Han Taichu convention). Correct the year start and the record snaps onto the NASA
   eclipse to the exact day, sexagenary day name, and solar position in the Hyades. The anomaly
   — the entire basis for calling it "the supernatural eclipse at the cross" — evaporates.
5. **The full-moon-calendar inference** ("darkness on the last day of a dark-moon Chinese month
   ⇒ Passover was at the dark phase ⇒ Hebrew months start at full moon") depended on the event
   being the crucifixion darkness. Since it is a garden-variety new-moon eclipse in May, it says
   nothing about Passover or the Hebrew month. (The one sound observation in the section — that
   Han months demonstrably began at the dark moon, since eclipses always fall on hui/shuo — is
   true but does no work for the book's thesis.)
6. **The quotes:** the emotionally load-bearing lines ("sins... on one man," "Man from Heaven
   died") are respectively a stock imperial self-blame amnesty formula and a phantom with no
   Chinese source. Any competent critic can shred this in one paragraph, and the debunk is
   already circulating.

## 7. Recommendation: (c) REMOVE — with two small collateral edits

**Remove the entire "Chinese Records of Cross Eclipse" subsection** (18-appointed-times.adoc,
lines ~1064–1111, including the unresolved `// FIGURE: NASA eclipse table` placeholder at
line ~1102, which becomes moot). The author's instinct is right, and the case is stronger than
"32 is now proven over 31":

- Wrong year (31, not 32) **and** wrong season (May, not Passover) **and** wrong moon phase for
  the book's argument (new moon, exactly as a natural eclipse requires) **and** compromised
  quotes. There is no version of this argument that survives; amending the year is impossible
  because the AD 32 Chinese record is empty.
- Worse than useless: it is the single most debunkable passage in the chapter. A hostile reader
  who checks Pankenier or the NASA catalog gains a cudgel against the book's genuinely strong
  material.
- Do **not** replace it with an argument from Chinese silence about a 32 AD darkness (the
  "Sherlock" logic cuts the wrong way: if the darkness was visible worldwide, Han silence in
  Jianwu 8 is a problem, not a proof; if it was local to the land of Judea, Chinese records are
  simply irrelevant either way).

**Collateral edits required when removing:**

1. The preceding paragraph (lines ~1056–1062) leans on this record: "Astronomers ... did record
   an unusually long and dark solar eclipse" (line ~1060) refers to the Guangwu record and must
   be cut or reworked; the Sherlock "dog didn't bark" framing can survive only in its weaker
   form (no astronomer flagged a full-moon darkness) if the author still wants it — and even
   that invites the reply that no astronomer flagged any darkness in AD 32 at all.
2. Check the rest of the book for echoes: the phrase "Passover week in 31 AD" (line ~1068) and
   the "May 10th, 31 AD eclipse" (line ~1104) appear only in this section of this chapter, but
   the "Why Didn't Yeshua correct the Calendar?" section (line ~1123) also says "kept a full
   moon based Passover **in 31 AD**" — that year reference needs updating to 32 AD as part of
   the edition-wide 31→32 sweep regardless.
