# Response to Scholarly Critique: Best Counter-Examples (Non-Gordon)

Critiques of the Hebrew-original thesis (e.g. that the HG / Cochin-type texts are back-translations from Greek, or polemical Hebraizations from the 17th–19th centuries) rightly stress Cochin manuscript dating, the absence of a chain to antiquity, and the possibility that later scribes inserted OT idioms and “restored” Jewish flavor. That framework can explain *some* markers but not all. Below we acknowledge the main critique points, then cite the notes’ own rebuttal on Revelation 2:26/27 and list the strongest counter-examples that Gordon did not emphasize and that are hardest to explain as back-translation. All citations are to verse-level evidence in `data/hg-chapters/` and `data/hebrew-gospels-notes.json`.

---

## 1. Acknowledging the critique

**Cochin dating and polemical Hebraization.** 17th–18th century MSS (e.g. Ezekiel Rahabi II), no attested chain to antiquity, and the Cambridge catalog’s description of Cochin as a “Hebrew translation… for polemical purposes” are valid. Later Hebrew NTs did insert OT idioms, verbatim quotes, and semantic shifts to “restore” a Jewish flavor. That explains *some* markers but not the set that require the Hebrew to say something the Greek does not say, or to use words that no translator from the Greek would choose.

**Revelation 2:26/27 and Psalm 2:9.** Critics argue that the Hebrew follows the Greek’s “error”: Greek ποιμανεῖ (shepherd/lead) is said to reflect a misreading of Hebrew Psalm 2:9 (רעה “shepherd” vs. רעע “break”), so that the Hebrew text’s use of “lead” (ינהג) would show dependence on Greek. The HG notes address this directly in `data/hg-chapters/Revelation-2.json` (verse 26): the HG uses ינהג (“lead”), and the notes state that “the Greek ποιμανεῖ aligns with the Hebrew Revelation’s ‘lead’ rather than with the Psalm’s ‘break’ — **suggesting the Greek follows the Hebrew Revelation’s reading, not the MT of Psalm 2**.” So the same alignment can be read as Greek translating a Hebrew tradition that had already reinterpreted the Psalm (lead/govern vs. break). The second half of Rev 2:26 preserves **verbatim** Psalm 2:9b (כְּכְלִי יוֹצֵר תְּנַפְּצֵם — “like a potter’s vessel you shall shatter them”); a back-translator from Greek would have to invent that exact OT phrase. In the same verse block, Rev 2:27 has HG **“the dawn” (השחר)** vs. Greek **“the morning star” (τὸν ἀστέρα τὸν πρωϊνόν)**. The notes: “A back-translator from Greek would write כּוֹכַב הַבֹּקֶר (‘star of the morning’), not shachar.” So 2:27 alone is a strong one-way marker that the critique does not address.

## 3. Conclusion

These markers are not merely “Hebraizing” choices a back-translator could make. They require (a) the Hebrew to say something the Greek does not say (Jude 16, James 5:16), or (b) the use of words that no translator from the Greek would choose (worm, torches, refined, horse, adversaries), or (c) dependence on Hebrew consonantal reasoning (Rev 14:8). That does not prove an ancient Hebrew autograph, but it does push back on a “~90% back-translation” estimate for *these* readings unless one posits a very unusual back-translator who repeatedly substituted different content and different words. The notes’ direction argument on Rev 2:26 (Greek follows the Hebrew Revelation’s reading; verbatim 2:9b; dawn vs. morning star in 2:27) is a direct counter to the claim that the Hebrew follows the Greek’s Psalm 2:9 “error.”
