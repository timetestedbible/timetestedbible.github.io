Around 1380, a Spanish rabbi named Shem Tov ben Isaac ben Shaprut copied a complete Hebrew Gospel of Matthew into his polemical treatise Even Bochan ("The Touchstone") — and then, in his marginal comments, argued against readings in his own text. A man does not forge a document and then refute it; he transmits what he received. Six centuries later George Howard published the critical text from nine manuscripts, and what it preserves touches some of the most load-bearing verses in the New Testament.

This article walks the four readings that matter most, letter by letter, and then answers the question every reader should ask: could this text be a back-translation, or a forgery crafted to serve a doctrine? Each reading below also has its own study page that will appear beside the verse as you read Matthew in the reader.

The Seat of Moses — Do As He Says

The scribes and the Pharisees sit in Moses' seat: All therefore whatsoever they bid you observe, that observe and do; but do not ye after their works: for they say, and do not. Matthew 23:2-3 (KJV)

Read that way, Jesus commands obedience to the Pharisees' teaching — and then spends the rest of the chapter demolishing it: their oath-rulings overturned (vv. 16-22), their burdens condemned (v. 4). One chapter earlier He warned against "the leaven of the Pharisees… their doctrine" (Mat 16:12), and He had already convicted them of "making the word of God of none effect through your tradition" (Mat 15:6). The Greek reading contradicts its own chapter. The Hebrew does not:

על כסא משה ישבו הפירושים והחכמים. ועתה כל אשר יאמר לכם שמרו ועשו ובתקנותיהם ומעשיהם אל תעשו שהם אומרים והם אינם עושים. "Upon the seat of Moses sit the Pharisees and the sages. And now, all that he says to you, keep and do; but by their takkanot and their deeds do not do — for they say, and do not." Hebrew Matthew (Shem Tov) 23:2-3

Two words carry the whole difference. The verb is יאמר — singular: all that he says — Moses, the Torah read from the seat — where the Greek reads plural, "whatever they say." And what is forbidden is their תקנות — takkanot: not a loose word for "works" but the technical rabbinic term for enacted ordinances — the man-made legislative layer, the "tradition of the elders" by its own name. The verse commands exactly what the rest of the book teaches: do Moses' law; do not do their tradition.

One vav
The entire doctrinal difference between "he says" and "they say" is a single letter: יאמר vs יאמרו. The same jot-level variance that separates "they pierced" from "like a lion" in Psalm 22:16. A Greek translator normalizing the verb to the nearer plural noun produces the Greek reading in one step; the reverse direction requires invention.

Small confirming touch in the same discourse: in verse 5 the Hebrew says they enlarge their ציציות — tzitziot, naming the fringes of Numbers 15:38 outright where English readers see only "borders of their garments."

After Those Days — No "Immediately," No "Tribulation"

Immediately after the tribulation of those days shall the sun be darkened, and the moon shall not give her light, and the stars shall fall from heaven… Matthew 24:29 (KJV)

Whole prophetic systems hang on that "immediately" and that "tribulation." The Hebrew carries neither:

ובאותה שעה אחרי הימים ההם יחשך השמש והירח לא יגיה אורו והכוכבים יפלו מהשמים וחיל השמים יתנדדו. "And in that hour, after those days, the sun shall darken, and the moon shall not give forth its light, and the stars shall fall from the heavens, and the host of the heavens shall be shaken." Hebrew Matthew (Shem Tov) 24:29

No εὐθέως, no "tribulation of" — Howard's own English prints "(the tribulation of)" in parentheses because the Hebrew does not have it. The signs are pinned to a time: "in that hour, after those days." Two verses later the gathering comes "with a shofar and a great voice… from the four winds of the heavens" (v. 31), and the fig-tree lesson ends "near, at the gates" — לשערים (v. 33).

"From That Day, No One Knows"

Heaven and earth shall pass away, but my words shall not pass away. But of that day and hour knoweth no man, no, not the angels of heaven, but my Father only. Matthew 24:35-36 (KJV)

The most-quoted line in all prophecy — quoted to end every conversation about timing. Here is the Hebrew:

והשמים והארץ יעברו. ומהיום ההוא ומהעת ההיא אין מי שיודע ולא מלאכי השמים אלא האב בלבד. "And the heavens and the earth shall pass away. And from that day and from that time, there is none who knows — not even the messengers of the heavens — but the Father alone." Hebrew Matthew (Shem Tov) 24:35-36

Three things, all in the letters. First, the מ prefix sits on both nouns — u-me-ha-yom ha-hu u-me-ha-et ha-hi, "from that day and from that time" — not the Greek's "concerning that day." The verse states from when the not-knowing runs, and "that day" points backward to the clause just spoken: the day heaven and earth pass away. Second, there is no "neither the Son" — the Hebrew names the angels and the Father only. Third, the manuscript's own section break falls after verse 36, binding "from that day" to the heaven-and-earth clause rather than to the Noah comparison that follows — which in the Hebrew reads "the days of the Son of Man," not "the coming."

The Bridegroom — and the Bride

מלכות שמים דומה לעשר בתולות שלקחו נרותיהן ויצאו לקראת חתן וכלה. "The kingdom of heaven is like ten virgins who took their lamps and went out to meet a bridegroom and a bride." Hebrew Matthew (Shem Tov) 25:1 — the reading also of Codex Bezae, the Old Latin, and the Syriac

The virgins are not the bride — they go out to meet her. They are the bride's espoused companions, the cast of the royal wedding psalm: "the virgins her companions that follow her… shall enter into the king's palace" (Ps 45:14-15). And the Hebrew keeps giving down the parable: five are עצלות כסילות, "lazy fools," five "diligent and wise" (v. 2); the bridegroom "tarried" in Habakkuk 2:3's exact verb, where the vision is for the appointed time (v. 5); the midnight cry opens with the Passover-stroke formula of Exodus 12:29 (v. 6); the foolish confess "our lamps are put out" — נדעכו, the verb Scripture reserves for "the lamp of the wicked" (Prov 13:9) — while the wise virgin's portrait is "her candle goeth not out by night" (Prov 31:18). The ready enter "into the chuppah" — the bridegroom's canopy of Psalm 19:5 — and "the gate was shut" (v. 10). And verse 13 ends: "you do not know the day nor the hour when the bridegroom will come" — the watch is for a wedding procession.

Could It Be a Forgery?

The right question — and it has a layered answer.

The text is not Shem Tov's creation. His marginal comments attack readings in his own Matthew (after 21:9 he faults it against Zechariah 9:9). A polemicist does not forge a text and then refute it. It carries ancient textual DNA no fourteenth-century Spaniard could assemble: Howard catalogs agreements with the Gospel of Thomas (22 readings), Codex Bezae (15), Marcion (17), the Old Latin (48), the Old Syriac, and the Pseudo-Clementines — witnesses lost or obscure until modern recovery. And the documented drift runs the other way: the later Hebrew copies (du Tillet 1555, Münster) were revised toward the Greek and Latin. Where this text deviates from the Greek, that is the earlier stratum, not the later hand.

The forgery test, applied to 23:3
Ask who had motive. The text's custodians were rabbinic Jews who lived by the takkanot — "do what Moses says, not their takkanot" in Jesus' mouth is a reading against the transmitters' own interest: the reading a rabbinic copyist would soften toward the Greek (which validates rabbinic authority), never invent. A Christian back-translator had no exemplar for it — his Greek and his Vulgate both read plural. Nobody with motive had the source; nobody with the source had motive.

Honesty requires the other column too: this is a medieval copy, with abbreviations, an occasional Romance gloss, harmonistic touches, and a real scholarly dispute (Howard's "ancient substratum" against Petersen's "medieval harmony tradition"). So the right posture is the one this site takes with the Septuagint and the Dead Sea Scrolls: an elder witness to be weighed reading by reading — never a new autograph. For the four readings above, every directional test — the harder reading, the transmitters' interest, the one-letter mechanics, and coherence with the rest of the canon — points the same way: old Hebrew readings the Greek flattened.
