Peninim: Why Pearl Fits Better Than Ruby

Six times the Hebrew Bible uses peninim (H6443). Six times the King James Version prints rubies. That familiar English word feels settled until the Strong’s panel is opened: its own definition is “probably a pearl (as round).” Which object did the writers mean—ruby, coral, pearl, or merely some precious material?

The short answer: the surviving evidence does not permit absolute certainty, but it does make pearl a responsible and probably preferable rendering. Coral is the strongest competing identification. Ruby is a traditional English choice, not the conclusion demanded by the Hebrew or by the ancient translations. The Time Tested Bible therefore proposes pearl in all six passages and leaves the evidence attached to the change.

Four AI Judges and the Heart of the Earth

The Sign of Jonah chapter of MEAT makes a claim with consequences: that “the heart of the earth” in Matthew 12:40 is Jerusalem — the city set “in the midst of the nations” (Ezekiel 5:5) — and not the tomb. Read that way, the three days and three nights measure the Son of Man under the city’s power, from His seizure outside its gates to the third day, and the count comes out exactly. But the reading also serves a calendar argument downstream, which invites a fair objection: does the interpretation secretly depend on the calendar it later supports?

The experiment: freeze the chapter, cut out the entire calendar-application section, and put the remaining textual and symbolic case before four AI models — Claude (Anthropic), Gemini (Google), GPT (OpenAI), and Grok (xAI) — each asked independently whether Jerusalem or the grave better explains the evidence supplied. None saw the Passover-14th or First Fruits material; searches of the saved requests confirmed it. The result: a strict three-of-four majority persuaded, with one substantive dissent.

The Method

Each provider had earlier accepted, in a separate run, the groundwork finding it was building on — the sea as the mass of the nations, Scripture’s own definition (“the waters… are peoples, and multitudes, and nations,” Revelation 17:15). For this test, each received only its own accepted groundwork plus the chapter’s Jonah evidence: Ezekiel’s navel of the land, Jonah’s own mirrored phrase “the heart of the seas,” and the structural parallels between Jonah’s descent and the arrest narrative. The chapter’s later section applying the sign to Passover and First Fruits was marked and omitted from every rendered request, so the calendar could not vote.

Claude, Gemini, and Grok judged that Jerusalem explains the supplied evidence better than the grave or Hades alternatives. The majority is a strict one: no judge saw another judge’s reasoning, and no request contained the calendar.

The Dissent Worth Reading

GPT’s objection was not a defense of the tomb reading. It was narrower and better: Jesus was seized, crucified, and buried outside Jerusalem’s walls — He “suffered without the gate” (Hebrews 13:12) — and the evidence supplied had not sufficiently established the shift from geographic location to custody under Jerusalem’s authority. The chapter’s answer is the custody argument itself: the sign begins when the city’s officers seize Him and ends when its power is forced to give Him back; Golgotha stood outside the wall, but the writ that sent Him there issued from within it. Whether that answer satisfies is exactly the kind of question a reader should test — against Scripture, not against a model’s vote.

What This Does and Does Not Prove

It does not prove the reading. No panel of models can: the authority never moves from the scriptures cited, and those any reader can check. What the experiment establishes is one specific, checkable fact: the Jonah reading persuades on the textual and symbolic evidence alone, without the calendar — so the symbol and the calendar stand as separate witnesses, and their convergence is a convergence, not a circle. A modern AI model is trained on the published consensus; persuading three of four away from the consensus tomb-reading, using nothing but the texts, is a measure of the evidence’s weight, not a substitute for weighing it yourself.

The Berean rule applies to this post too: the models’ verdicts are not evidence. The texts are. Search the scriptures daily, whether these things are so (Acts 17:11) — the chapter lays out every verse, and the experiment record preserves every request and verdict for anyone who wants to audit the method.
