The Second Day of the Sabbath

When studying Scripture, grammar matters — and our English translations hide a fundamental truth that changes how key texts read. It is a truth readily acknowledged by those who speak Hebrew: to this day the synagogue announces its daily psalm as “the first day in the sabbath,” “the second day in the sabbath” — the week itself called the sabbath. But because the same truth presses against sabbath tradition, it is quietly passed over.

Conclusion Upfront

When you hear the word “sabbath” you probably think of the day of rest. I will demonstrate that a consistent grammar rule runs through all of Scripture — verified against every occurrence — that clearly distinguishes when sabbath means a span of seven days and when it means the day of rest itself. When translation conflates the two, it fundamentally changes the meaning of key texts. In other words: the sabbath is often the sabbath-week, and the week is named after the day that makes it special.

Three Psalm Titles the Translators Left Behind

The Greek Septuagint — the Bible of the apostles' generation — titles its psalms by the days of the week. Watch what it calls the week:

“A Psalm of David, for the first of the sabbaths (tēs mias sabbatōn).”LXX Psalm 23(24):1 — the identical phrase the Gospels use for the resurrection morning (Matthew 28:1, mia sabbatōn)
“A Psalm of praise for the sons of Korah, on the second of the sabbath (deutera sabbatou).”LXX Psalm 47(48):1
“A Psalm of David, on the fourth of the sabbaths (tetradi sabbatōn).”LXX Psalm 93(94):1

The first of the sabbath, the second of the sabbath, the fourth of the sabbath — days numbered as days of a span called the sabbath. The day of rest itself is the seventh day of the sabbath, and the Greek psalter has that too: “a psalm for the sabbath day” (LXX Psalm 91(92):1). The week belongs to the sabbath the way a month belongs to its new moon.

Hebrew says the same thing with one compact idiom: shabbat b'shabbato — literally “the sabbath within its sabbath” (Numbers 28:10). Saying shabbat twice would be redundant if the word only ever meant a day. It is perfect sense if the day crowns the week named after it — the sabbath-day nested inside its sabbath-week, exactly parallel to chodesh b'chodsho, the new moon within its month.

Scripture Names Spans After Their Defining Day

This is not a quirk of the sabbath. Scripture routinely identifies a span of days by the single day that defines it.

The Passover — one day's sacrifice names the seven-day feast, stated flat:

“In the first month, in the fourteenth day of the month, ye shall have the passover, a feast of seven days.”Ezekiel 45:21
“Now the feast of unleavened bread drew near, which is called the Passover.”Luke 22:1

The month — named after the chodesh, the “new moon.” Chodesh means renewal, yet it is Hebrew's word for the whole month: “even a whole month (chodesh yamim — 'a new-moon of days'), until it come out at your nostrils” (Numbers 11:20); “he abode with him the space of a month (chodesh yamim)” (Genesis 29:14). The exact analog of Passover→Unleavened Bread: one renewal day names a span of thirty.

So When Is the Sabbath a Week, and When Is It a Day?

I have done a complete scan of every occurrence in Scripture — all 108 uses of shabbat, all 11 of shabbaton, all 68 of the Greek sabbaton, catalogued and classified — and the pattern is consistent. The grammar distinguishes days that belong to something from days that are merely counted toward something. Watch it work in the harvest count:

“…ye shall bring a sheaf of the firstfruits of your harvest unto the priest: and he shall wave the sheaf before the LORD… on the morrow after the sabbath (mimochorat ha-shabbat) the priest shall wave it… ye shall eat neither bread, nor parched corn, nor green ears, until the selfsame day that ye have brought an offering.”Leviticus 23:10–14
“And ye shall count unto you from the morrow after the sabbath (mimochorat ha-shabbat), from the day that ye brought the sheaf of the wave offering; seven sabbaths shall be complete (sheva shabbatot temimot): even unto the morrow after the seventh sabbath (mimochorat ha-shabbat ha-shvi'it) shall ye number fifty days.”Leviticus 23:15–16

Only a span can be complete (temimot); a day simply is or is not. And the proof that these counted sabbaths are week-units is that Moses restates the same command in the other vocabulary:

“Seven weeks (sheva shavuot) shalt thou number unto thee: begin to number the seven weeks from such time as thou beginnest to put the sickle to the corn. And thou shalt keep the feast of weeks (chag shavuot) unto the LORD thy God…”Deuteronomy 16:9–10; see also Exodus 34:22 (“the feast of weeks”) and Numbers 28:26 (“after your weeks be out”)

Same command, same count, same festival — sabbath in Leviticus, weeks in Deuteronomy, and the feast is named for what was counted.

Confirmed by the Greek Septuagint

“…then shall ye bring a sheaf, the firstfruits of your harvest, to the priest; and he shall lift up the sheaf before the Lord, to be accepted for you. On the morrow of the first day the priest shall lift it up.”LXX Leviticus 23:10–11 — tē epaurion tēs prōtēs, “on the morrow of the first”

Where the Hebrew reads mimochorat ha-shabbat, the third-century-BC translators wrote “the morrow of the first” — the day after the feast's first day. The first day of Unleavened Bread is the 15th; its morrow is the 16th, the second day of the feast, every year. Two things make this witness weighty rather than merely interesting: it is an interpretation baked into a translation — the Alexandrian translators didn't preserve an ambiguity, they resolved it, telling us how the priesthood of their era read the verse — and it is the oldest surviving reading of Leviticus 23:11, older than the Masoretic vowel points by roughly a millennium. It also agrees with every other second-temple witness: Josephus's explicit “sixteenth day” (Antiquities 3.250), the Targums' “after the festival day,” and the Mishnah's omer on 16 Nisan even when it overrode a weekly sabbath. And the LXX resolves the anchor while keeping the count in units — its very next verses render Leviticus 23:15 as “seven whole weeks.” Launch from the feast's first-day morrow; count seven complete week-units.

The Structure of Time

Now we arrive at the structure of time itself. Isaiah 66:23 is usually flattened to “from one new moon to another, and from one sabbath to another” — but the Hebrew has an internal structure, and one translation kept it. Julia E. Smith (1876):

“And it was as often as the new moon in its new moon, and as often as the Sabbath in its Sabbath…”Isaiah 66:23 — midei-chodesh b'chodsho u'midei shabbat b'shabbato (מִדֵּי־חֹדֶשׁ בְּחָדְשׁוֹ וּמִדֵּי שַׁבָּת בְּשַׁבַּתּוֹ)

The new moon belongs to its month; the sabbath belongs to its week. This possessive-distributive idiom is a closed family in Hebrew, and it always nests a marker-day inside its recurring span:

  • yom b'yomo — “day in its day”: Exodus 5:13, 19; 16:4 (the manna); Leviticus 23:37 (“every thing upon his day”); 1 Kings 8:59; 2 Kings 25:30; Ezra 3:4; Nehemiah 11:23; 12:47; Jeremiah 52:34; Daniel 1:5…
  • shabbat b'shabbato — the sabbath within its sabbath-week: Numbers 28:10; Isaiah 66:23
  • chodesh b'chodsho — the new moon within its month: Numbers 28:14; Isaiah 66:23
  • shanah b'shanah — year by year: Deuteronomy 15:20; 1 Samuel 1:7; 7:16; Zechariah 14:16 (the nations going up “year by year” to keep Tabernacles)…

Day, week, month, year — each span owned by its marker. And once that structure is visible, a door opens on a text everyone thought they knew: the new moon's own feast, and its second day.

The Second Day of the New Moon Feast

One of the loudest complaints against lunar sabbath reckoning is the accusation that it produces “non-days” or “transition days” — days that belong to no week. But the plain text of Scripture shows the renewed-moon feast can be two days long. There are no unaccounted-for days on the calendar; Scripture names them.

“And David said unto Jonathan, Behold, to morrow is the new moon (ha-chodesh), and I should not fail to sit with the king at meat: but let me go, that I may hide myself in the field unto the third day at even.”1 Samuel 20:5
“Then Jonathan said to David, To morrow is the new moon: and thou shalt be missed, because thy seat will be empty.”1 Samuel 20:18
“So David hid himself in the field: and when the new moon was come, the king sat him down to eat meat.”1 Samuel 20:24

Our first clue: David plans around a feast that outlasts one evening — he will hide “unto the third day.” Three times the KJV says new moon. Then, four verses later, the same Hebrew word suddenly becomes something else: “the second day of the month” (vv. 27, 34). In Hebrew, “new moon” and “month” are one word — chodesh — and the translator chooses which English you see. So which is it: the second day of the month, or the second day of the new moon feast? The grammar answers, and you don't need Hebrew to see it. You need one syllable.

The Difference Is One Syllable

Hebrew puts a one-letter word in front of chodesh to tell you what kind of statement it is making. La- means “to” — it measures. Ha- means “the” — it names. English does the same job with different tools: “day two of March” is an address on a calendar; “day two of the wedding” belongs to an event. Read the two phrases word by word — they differ in exactly one place:

Haggai 1:1 — a date (this pattern appears 110 times in Scripture)
b'yomechadla-chodesh
on dayoneTO the month
1 Samuel 20:34 — the feast (this pattern appears four times in all — see below)
b'yomha-chodeshha-sheni
on the day ofTHE new moonthe second

Say them out loud. A date measures its distance to the month: “day one to the month.” Jonathan's day belongs to the feast: “the day of the new moon — the second.” Nobody counting a calendar date says “the day of THE month”; you only talk that way about a day an event owns — the morning of the wedding, the second day of the feast.

The Bible's Own Control Group

The phrase yom ha-chodesh — “day of THE new moon” — appears exactly four times in the Hebrew Bible. The phrase itself is identical every time; only what follows it changes. Watch the KJV column:

VerseThe identical phrase…what followsThe KJV translates it
Ezekiel 46:1on the day of the new moon—“in the day of the new moon” ✓
Ezekiel 46:6on the day of the new moon—“in the day of the new moon” ✓
Exodus 40:2on the day of the new moonha-rishon — “the first”“on the first day of the first month”
1 Samuel 20:34on the day of the new moonha-sheni — “the second”“the second day of the month”

Same four words at the front of all four verses. Where the phrase stands alone, the translators render it honestly: the day of the new moon. The moment a “first” or “second” is attached, they switch to “month” — but nothing in the Hebrew switched. Attach the ordinal without flinching and 1 Samuel 20:34 reads exactly as written: Jonathan “did eat no meat on the day of the new moon — the second.”

The Bible Counts Feast Days Exactly This Way

Is “the second day of a feast” even a biblical way of counting? It is the standard way. Numbers 29 sets up the Feast of Tabernacles using both grammars, five verses apart — the date formula to say when the feast falls, then plain ordinals to count the feast's own days:

VerseGrammarIt saysDay of the month
Numbers 29:12date formula — la-chodesh“on the fifteenth day to the month”the 15th
Numbers 29:17feast ordinal“and on the second day…”the 16th
Numbers 29:20feast ordinal“and on the third day…”the 17th

Read the middle row again: “the second day” is the sixteenth of the month. No reader has ever confused the second day of Tabernacles with the 2nd of the month — because once a feast is running, Scripture counts its days, not the calendar's. It does this everywhere: the twelve-day dedication of the altar is counted “the first day… the second day…” through twelve princes (Numbers 7:12, 18), anchored not to a date but to an event — “the day that Moses had fully set up the tabernacle” (7:1). Ezekiel's seven-day altar cleansing runs the same way: “on the second day thou shalt offer a kid of the goats” (Ezekiel 43:22, 25), with no calendar date in the passage at all. When 1 Samuel 20:34 says Jonathan ate nothing on “the day of the new moon, the second,” it is doing what Numbers and Ezekiel do on every feast — with one difference: it doesn't leave the span to context. It names it.

Exodus 40:2 — Both Grammars in One Verse

One verse in the Torah uses both systems side by side, and it settles what each one is for. Here is how the KJV gives it:

“On the first day of the first month shalt thou set up the tabernacle of the tent of the congregation.”Exodus 40:2

Now the Hebrew, which is two phrases, word by word:

Phrase one — names the day
b'yomha-chodeshha-rishon
on the day ofTHE new moonthe first
Phrase two — gives the date
b'echadla-chodesh
ononeTO the month

Phrase two, b'echad la-chodesh, is the Bible's standard way of saying “on the first day of the month” — it appears seventeen times, and in the other sixteen it always stands alone: “in the first month, on the first day of the month, the waters were dried up” (Genesis 8:13); “on the first day of the second month… they assembled all the congregation” (Numbers 1:1, 18); “in the fortieth year, in the eleventh month, on the first day of the month, Moses spake” (Deuteronomy 1:3). The formula needs no helper. Notice also what the English did with the two phrases: the KJV harvested “the first” from one and “day… of the month” from the other, wove them into a single smooth date, and quietly discarded the overlap — so the English reader is never shown that there are two statements in the verse at all.

How Do We Know “the First” Isn't the First Month?

Because Hebrew already has its ways of saying that — and this isn't any of them. Run three checks.

Check 1 — watch the Bible write this exact date elsewhere. Scripture says “on the first day of the first month” in attested shapes, all built on la-chodesh, the date-word:

VerseHebrewWord for word
Ezra 7:9b'echad la-chodesh ha-rishon“on one to the month, the first”
2 Chronicles 29:17b'echad la-chodesh ha-rishon“on one to the month, the first”
Exodus 40:17ba-chodesh ha-rishon… b'echad la-chodesh“in the first month… on one to the month”
Ezekiel 45:18ba-rishon, b'echad la-chodesh“in the first, on one to the month”

Notice where “the first” lives when it numbers the month: on la-chodesh or on the standalone month-slot. Putting the month's number directly on the date-word is routine — “the fifteenth day to the seventh month” (Leviticus 23:34) and thirty more like it. Scripture dates by month-ordinal 48 times, ba-chodesh ha-rishon specifically ten times — and zero of the 48 put a yom in front of the month. When a date needs the word “day,” it puts it on the number — “on the fourteenth day to the month” — never on the month. So if Exodus 40:2 meant to number the month, the slot was open one word later: b'echad la-chodesh ha-rishon, Ezra's exact sentence. Instead, “the first” sits fused to yom ha-chodesh — a phrase that appears in no date, anywhere.

Check 2 — ask the King James translators what this ordinal counts. The phrase “day/morrow of the new moon” carries an ordinal exactly three times in Scripture. Watch what the KJV does each time:

VerseHebrew ordinalKJV renders it
1 Samuel 20:27ha-sheni“the second day”
1 Samuel 20:34ha-sheni“the second day”
Exodus 40:2ha-rishon“the first month”

In Samuel, the translators render the ordinal as a day — they had no choice; the story is one feast, one evening after another, and “the second month” would be nonsense. (They even supplied “of the month” out of thin air — there is no la-chodesh in either verse.) Only in Exodus 40:2 does the same ordinal on the same phrase suddenly become the month's number. Same grammar, two treatments — and the day-reading is the one the translators themselves concede everywhere the context speaks.

Check 3 — read the ordinal one way and watch every verse fall in line. Count days of the new moon in all three, and the family is uniform:

“On the day of the new moon — the first — on day one to the month — set up the tabernacle.” (Exodus 40:2)
“On the morrow of the new moon — the second [day] — David's place was empty.” (1 Samuel 20:27)
“Jonathan… did eat no meat on the day of the new moon — the second.” (1 Samuel 20:34)

First day, second day, second day. Nothing supplied, nothing flipped, and the two halves of Exodus 40:2 each keep their own job — the feast's own count on one side, the calendar address on the other, the same division of labor as “the sabbath, the seventh day of the week.” And notice what the ordinal gives away: nobody says “the first day of the new moon” unless the new moon can have a second. The command in Exodus quietly presupposes the very thing 1 Samuel narrates. These are the only two ordinals ever attached to the phrase in all of Scripture: the first, and the second — a matched set, witnessed from opposite ends of the canon. Torah names day one; the historian names day two. We know the phrase is a real observance-term and not a fancy date, because the same words with nothing attached are exactly what Ezekiel calls the day the temple's east gate swings open: “on the sabbath it shall be opened, and in the day of the new moon it shall be opened” (Ezekiel 46:1).

The Third Day in the Field Is a Working Day

Now read the second-day verses as written, and follow the story to its end:

“And it came to pass on the morrow of the new moon, the second [day], that David's place was empty: and Saul said unto Jonathan his son, Wherefore cometh not the son of Jesse to meat, neither yesterday, nor to day?”1 Samuel 20:27
“So Jonathan arose from the table in fierce anger, and did eat no meat on the day of the new moon, the second: for he was grieved for David…”1 Samuel 20:34

Go back to David's plan, and look at what the Hebrew actually says. “Unto the third day at even” (v. 5) — the word “day” is not in the Hebrew. It reads ad ha-erev ha-shlishit: “until the evening, the third.” Just an ordinal, hanging on a count. Jonathan uses the same shorthand setting the signal: “about to morrow, the third” (ka'et machar ha-shlishit, v. 12 — again, no “day”). The third of what? Of the only thing this chapter counts. And here is the fact that seals it: in a chapter that is all about days at the king's table, the Bible's date formula — la-chodesh, the form used 110 times — appears zero times. Nothing in 1 Samuel 20 is a calendar date. Every time-marker is counted from the feast — none from the calendar:

PositionThe text saysWhat kind of dayVerse
1“to morrow is the new moon” … “when the new moon was come, the king sat him down to eat”feast — day one of the new moonvv. 5, 24
2“the morrow of the new moon, the second” … “the day of the new moon, the second”feast — day two of the new moonvv. 27, 34
3“until the evening, the third” … “to morrow, the third”the working day (v. 19)vv. 5, 12

But doesn't a count of three make the feast three days long? No — and Torah itself is the proof. An ordinal series in Hebrew measures position from its anchor, not the length of the anchor. Tabernacles is “a feast unto the LORD seven days” (Numbers 29:12), yet the very same count proceeds to “on the eighth day ye shall have a solemn assembly” (29:35) — and Leviticus does it inside a single verse: “Seven days ye shall offer an offering made by fire unto the LORD: on the eighth day shall be an holy convocation” (Leviticus 23:36). The count runs past the feast, and you know where the feast ended because the day's label changes. Counting a short wait from an anchor is simply how Hebrew speaks: “sanctify them to day and to morrow… and be ready against the third day” (Exodus 19:10–11).

One more door to close: if the count can outrun the feast, could the feast be just one day — with “the second” being merely position two, a working day like the third? The text answers four times. Saul himself sets the feast-table on both days: “Wherefore cometh not the son of Jesse to meat, neither yesterday, nor to day?” (v. 27) — a seat is only conspicuously empty at a meal. The uncleanness excuse is built to last exactly one day — “unclean until the even” (Leviticus 11:24) — which is why Saul holds his peace on day one (“surely he is not clean,” v. 26) and erupts on day two: the trap only springs if day two carried the same obligation as day one. The narrator's labels agree: when Scripture's counts run past a feast, the extra day is a bare ordinal with its own name — “they kept the feast seven days; and on the eighth day was a solemn assembly” (Nehemiah 8:18) — never called a day of the feast. Here it is day two that bears the feast's name, “the day of the new moon, the second,” and day three that bears the other label. And Jonathan's fast only signifies at a feast: refusing the king's meat is a protest worth a verse; skipping lunch on a working day is not. The feast was two days — not because the count says so, but because everything else does.

Then the narrator tells you, in two Hebrew words, what the third day is:

“And when thou hast stayed three days, then thou shalt go down quickly, and come to the place where thou didst hide thyself when the business was in hand…”1 Samuel 20:19

“When the business was in hand” is the translators' paraphrase of b'yom ha-ma'aseh — “on the working day.” That phrase occurs exactly one other place in all of Scripture, and the other place is its dictionary entry:

“The gate of the inner court… shall be shut the six working days (yemei ha-ma'aseh); but on the sabbath it shall be opened, and in the day of the new moon (yom ha-chodesh) it shall be opened.”Ezekiel 46:1

Ezekiel splits the calendar into kinds of days: working days, and the days the gate opens — the sabbath and the day of the new moon. First Samuel 20 runs on the same kinds. Day one: the new moon — the king holds the feast. Day two: the second day of the new moon — the feast continues, and an empty seat can no longer be excused. Day three: the working day — and in its morning Jonathan walks out to the field with his bow (v. 35), the arrows fly, and David flees for his life. The feast was two days. The narrator counted them for you — first, second, and back to work — and never once reached for a date.

Every Day Belongs to Something

Now assemble the whole structure, because the grammar has been drawing one picture all along. Read Ezekiel's gate law again: it knows three kinds of days, and gives no rule for collisions between them — the six working days, the sabbath, the day of the new moon. Each day of the calendar is one of the three. The days of the week belong to the sabbath — the Greek Bible titles its psalms “for the first of the sabbaths,” “on the second of the sabbath,” “on the fourth of the sabbaths,” and “for the sabbath day” itself. The days of the month belong to the new moon — “the day of the new moon, the first” (Exodus 40:2), “the day of the new moon, the second” (1 Samuel 20:34). The days of the feasts belong to their feasts — “on the second day… on the third day…” of Tabernacles, which fall on the 16th and 17th of the month and are never confused with the 2nd and 3rd.

This is why the loudest objection to lunar reckoning — “you'd have leftover days, non-days, transition days!” — dissolves on contact with the text. Scripture names those days. They are the days of the new moon: one when the renewal is plain, a second when the observance requires it — feasted at the king's table, the temple gate standing open — and then “the working day” resumes and the weeks begin their count. There are no orphan days on God's calendar. Every day belongs to something: to the new moon that opens the month, or to a sabbath that crowns its week.

Conclusion

One small grammar rule, applied consistently, and the texts sort themselves. A day counted to the month (la-chodesh) is a date — an address, nothing more. A day of the something (yom ha-shabbat, yom ha-chodesh, “the second day” of a feast) belongs to an observance, and the observance owns it. And spans are named for the day that defines them: the seven-day feast is called “the Passover” for its sacrifice, the month is called chodesh for the renewal that opens it, and the week is called “the sabbath” for the rest that crowns it — “seven sabbaths shall be complete” is the law's own arithmetic, restated by Moses as “seven weeks.”

And the opening claim — that Hebrew speakers have always known this — you can verify in any synagogue this week. The daily psalm is announced to this hour with the ancient formula: “Today is the first day in the sabbath… the second day in the sabbath…” — the week itself called the sabbath, its days numbered as its days, exactly as the Greek translators titled the psalms twenty-two centuries ago and exactly as the Gospel writers dated the resurrection: “on the first of the sabbaths.” The grammar was never lost. It was translated over.

The second day of the sabbath. The second day of the new moon. Same grammar, two spans — the week that belongs to its rest, the month that belongs to its renewal. Once you see whose days these are, you cannot unsee it — and the calendar of Scripture stands whole, with every day accounted for.
