The Renewed Faithful Witness

English Bibles call the recurring calendar marker the New Moon. The Hebrew word translated that way is chodesh, formed from the verb chadash, "renew," so this study will call it the Renewed Moon while asking which lunar state Scripture identifies. Job describes witnesses renewed and brought again facing the speaker. Psalm 89 identifies the moon as the faithful witness and uses the same facing relation in its sun-and-throne description. In the sky, that relation appears at full moon, when the moon stands opposite the sun and presents its whole illuminated face to earth. Psalm 81 then calls for the shofar at the Renewed Moon, at the full moon, for the singular day of the feast. Together, these passages identify the beginning of the month with the moon restored to its complete visible testimony.

Step One: "New Moon" Means a Moon Renewed

The phrase New Moon is familiar, but the English word new can hide the most useful part of the biblical term. The Hebrew noun translated "new moon" or "month" is chodesh. It is derived from the verb chadash, which means to renew, rebuild, or bring again. "Renewed Moon" therefore keeps the word's verbal connection visible in English.

English IdeaHebrew TermFunction in the Argument
To renew, restore, or bring againchadash, H2318The action from which the calendar term is formed
New Moon, month — literally the Renewed Moonchodesh, H2320The recurring calendar marker whose lunar state must be identified

The word "renewed" does not, by itself, identify a phase. It tells us what question Scripture must answer: What is being renewed, and what does its restored condition look like? The next passages answer both parts.

Step Two: Scripture Defines the Lunar Witness

“His seed shall endure forever, and his throne like the sun facing me. Like the moon it shall be established forever—a faithful witness in the sky. Selah.”Psalm 89:36–37 — close rendering from the Masoretic Hebrew

Psalm 89 does more than use the moon as a general picture of duration. It places a promised throne under the paired images of sun and moon, and it assigns the moon an office: the faithful witness in heaven. The moon's faithfulness is not light generated from itself. Its testimony is received light made visible through the night.

The speaker is not implicit. In the two preceding verses God says "my covenant," "my lips," and "I have sworn," then continues with the throne "like the sun facing me." The Hebrew word is negdî, and that exact form will appear again in Job's description of renewed witnesses. The facing relation is therefore not an unspecified orientation between two objects; it describes something presented before a speaker. Psalm 89 has supplied three parts before Job is opened:

  • The relevant heavenly body is the moon.
  • Its defined office is faithful witness.
  • The paired throne-and-sun description includes the spatial relation facing me.

Step Three: Job Defines the Renewal of a Witness

"Thou renewest thy witnesses against me…"Job 10:17

In plain English, Job says, "You renew your witnesses facing me." The lexical references below verify the three links without requiring the reader to decipher a Hebrew sentence.

English LinkLexical ReferenceWhy It Matters

Your witnessesed, H5707Psalm 89 uses the same witness noun for the faithful witness in heaven
Facing menegdi, H5048Psalm 89 uses the exact form when it places the throne as the sun "before me"

Job's immediate subject is his affliction: successive witnesses appear against him. That non-lunar setting makes the clause useful rather than weak. Job is not trying to prove a calendar and therefore supplies an independent verbal construction: renew the witnesses facing me.

The object being renewed is the lunar witness, and its restored relation is "facing me." Psalm 89 identifies the moon as the faithful witness, while Job describes renewed witnesses as brought again facing the speaker. The geometry can now determine which lunar phase satisfies both conditions.

Step Four: Light, Witness, and Geometry Identify the Full Moon

The sky now supplies the concrete meaning of the scriptural relation. At full moon, the moon is opposite the sun as viewed from earth, and its complete visible face is illuminated. The moon does not originate that light; it faithfully presents the light it has received. The faithful witness has been brought again into its whole visible office.

"The same came for a witness, to bear witness of the Light… He was not that Light, but was sent to bear witness of that Light."John 1:7–8

John states the light–witness relation directly. A witness is not the source of the light; the witness's office is to make that light known. This supplies the scriptural definition behind the lunar geometry: the moon does not testify by producing light of its own, but by faithfully presenting the light it receives.

The Tabernacle Preserves the Same Relation

“In them hath he set a tabernacle for the sun.”Psalm 19:4, ASV

“See… that thou make all things according to the pattern that was showed thee in the mount.”Hebrews 8:5, ASV; quoting Exodus 25:40

“Without the veil which is before the testimony, Aaron and his sons shall keep it in order from evening to morning before Jehovah: it shall be a statute for ever.”Exodus 27:21, ASV

Edut—the Testimony, including the tablets placed in the ark—and panim, the word-family behind lifnei, “before” or “facing,” occur together in fifteen verses. The repeated pairing ties the Testimony to what is placed or presented before it, and the Torah gives that relation a consistent architecture. The manna is laid up before the Testimony (Exodus 16:34). Incense is placed before the veil and before the Testimony where God meets with Israel (Exodus 30:6, 36; 40:5). Aaron's rod is brought again before the Testimony and kept there as a sign against the rebels (Numbers 17:4, 10). The entire structure is called the tabernacle of the Testimony or witness (Exodus 38:21; Numbers 17:7).

At the center of that facing-arrangement is the lamp. The ner is supplied with oil and kept in order outside the veil—the veil that stands before the Testimony—from evening to morning, continually, as a perpetual ordinance (Exodus 27:20–21; Leviticus 24:2–3). Even Moses descends carrying the two tablets of the Testimony while his face shines (Exodus 34:29), joining testimony, face, and received light in one scene.

“For the commandment is a lamp; and the law is light.”Proverbs 6:23

“Command the children of Israel, that they bring unto thee pure oil olive beaten for the light, to cause the lamp to burn always.”Exodus 27:20

“I will give power unto my two witnesses… These are the two olive trees, and the two candlesticks standing before the God of the earth.”Revelation 11:3–4

The roles must be kept distinct. The tablets called the Testimony contain the covenant commandment: the law and light being borne. The lamp does not originate that testimony; it presents it. Pure beaten oil is poured into the lamp and consumed so that the light becomes visible. Revelation confirms the witness-function by naming the two witnesses as both olive trees and lampstands. The oil spent in burning is active testimony, and the lamp is its visible bearer through the night. In the heavenly correspondence, then, the lamp corresponds to the Moon, not the Sun. The Sun supplies the greater light; the Moon bears that received light from evening to morning.

This pattern does not select the lunar phase by itself; it independently tests the phase already derived from the renewed-witness language. The sanctuary lamp is not the source: it is the night-long bearer of light supplied through consumed oil. The Moon has the same office in the sky. Dark conjunction supplies no visible lunar lamp; the first crescent bears a fragment of light for the early part of the night; only the full Moon presents the whole faithful witness from evening to morning. The geometry and the sanctuary architecture therefore give the same answer.

“For there are three who bear witness, the Spirit, and the water, and the blood: and the three agree in one.”1 John 5:8, ASV

“The sun became black as sackcloth of hair, and the whole moon became as blood.”Revelation 6:12, ASV

Blood is not silent in Scripture. Abel's blood cries from the ground (Genesis 4:10), and Revelation joins the blood of the Lamb to the word of testimony (Revelation 12:11). First John states the definition directly: the blood bears witness. Revelation then applies the image to the whole moon. The faithful lunar witness can therefore testify in received light and in blood.

A blood-colored lunar eclipse is possible only at full moon, when the moon stands opposite the sun. This does not require every prophecy of the moon becoming blood to describe an astronomical eclipse. It does establish that the visible geometry of a blood witness requires a full moon; it cannot occur at dark conjunction.

“The moon and stars to rule by night; for his lovingkindness endureth for ever.”Psalm 136:9, ASV

Genesis assigns the lesser light authority over the night; Psalm 136 states the relationship more tightly by placing the moon and stars together as its rulers. Every visible phase can share some portion of the night with the stars, but only the full moon rises around sunset and sets around sunrise. It alone spans the night from evening to morning as a whole lunar face among the stars. If the appointed light is to rule the night rather than briefly appear within it, fullness is the geometry that completes the office.

"I am the light of the world… It is also written in your law, that the testimony of two men is true. I am one that bear witness of myself, and the Father that sent me beareth witness of me."John 8:12, 17–18

John 8 places light, testimony, and the requirement for two witnesses inside one argument. Genesis 1:14–16 likewise appoints the heavenly lights for signs and then identifies the two great lights. Psalm 89 names the moon as a faithful witness. Taken together, the calendar sign is not established merely by an invisible Moon occupying a calculated position beside the Sun; it is presented through two distinct heavenly lights.

The distinction is important. The moon's sunward hemisphere receives light during every phase, including conjunction. That fact alone would make "facing the sun" phase-neutral. The scriptural relation concerns the witness presented before the speaker or observer. At dark conjunction, the moon appears in the same direction as the sun while its illuminated hemisphere is turned away from earth. Its visible face is dark, so ordinarily only the sun supplies a visible light. During total solar eclipse, the dark moon obscures the sun, leaving neither appointed light visible as a complete luminous disk. At full moon, the two lights stand in opposite directions and the moon presents its whole face toward earth—normally in received light and, during lunar eclipse, in blood.

Near the full-moon boundary, one light descends toward the western horizon as the other rises in the east. Both appear as whole circular signs of nearly the same apparent size, facing one another across the observer. The renewed witness, the facing relation, the two lights, and their visible testimony therefore converge on the full moon rather than the dark conjunction.

Step Five: Psalm 81 Names the Event

"Blow the trumpet at the new moon, at the full moon, on our feast day."Psalm 81:3, ASV

The ASV says "trumpet," but the Hebrew names a shofar specifically. That distinction matters, so the analysis will retain the Hebrew instrument rather than flatten it into the broader English category. Psalm 81 is where the definitions meet the calendar. The Hebrew noun translated "full moon" is keseh. That is its standard lexical meaning, and its only other occurrence also names a full-moon date: "He will come home at the full moon" (Proverbs 7:20, ASV). There is no need to derive the phase from a hypothetical meaning of "covered." The noun names the full moon directly.

There is no word for "and" between "at the Renewed Moon" and "at the full moon." There is one command, one shofar, two adjacent descriptions of its timing, and one singular feast day. The direct reading is:

Blow the shofar at the Renewed Moon — at the full moon — for the day of our feast.
The Renewed Moon, the full moon, and the feast day identify one event.

The conclusion is nested rather than merely cumulative. Remove any later inference and the earlier definitions remain independently established. Restore the links, and they converge on one state:

Psalm 89 places that relation in the throne–sun–moon field, and the sky resolves it as the moon restored to the opposite station with its whole reflected face visible. Psalm 81 calls that state the full moon and places both descriptions within the one feast event.

Did the Proof Discover the Sign—or Rationalize a Preference?

A chain assembled from Scripture can still be a rationalization if its definitions were selected to protect a conclusion. The next test is therefore adversarial: what is the strongest case that can be made from Scripture for beginning the month at the dark conjunction or at the first visible crescent? Only after that case is stated positively can the three readings be compared fairly.

The dark conjunction and the first crescent are not the same sign. Conjunction is the invisible astronomical alignment that ends one lunar cycle and begins another. The first crescent appears afterward, when enough of the moon's illuminated surface becomes visible near the western horizon. The two readings share a general boundary in the lunar cycle, but their evidence must not be combined as though they identify one event.

The Strongest Case for the First Visible Crescent

God made the lights for signs and appointed times (Genesis 1:14), and he appointed the moon for seasons (Psalm 104:19). Chodesh belongs to the word-family of renewal. The first crescent is the first visible return of lunar light after the moon's disappearance; it is therefore a natural candidate for a visible sign of renewal. Exodus 12:2 calls the present chodesh the head of the months and uses the demonstrative "this." Read as a displayed example, the verse can be understood as directing Moses to the form that should mark day one. Two human witnesses can then establish that the form was actually seen, following the general law that a matter is established by two or three witnesses (Deuteronomy 19:15). Count forward from that crescent, and the feasts dated near the fifteenth fall near the full moon. Taken together, these claims would produce a coherent crescent calendar—but only if the claims are well formed, independently substantiated by Scripture, and do not depend on the calendar they are being used to prove.

The Strongest Case for the Dark Conjunction

The moon wanes toward disappearance and then begins a new cycle at conjunction. Unlike first visibility, which varies with weather, terrain, latitude, and atmospheric conditions, the alignment is a definite astronomical event. First Samuel 20:5 says, "Tomorrow is the new moon," showing that the calendar date could be known in advance rather than discovered only after a sighting. Psalm 81:3 can also be read by deriving keseh from the idea of covering: blow the shofar when the moon is hidden on the feast day. On this reading, conjunction is the objective beginning and the later crescent is merely its first visible announcement.

What the Classical Jewish Reading Actually Claims

The classical rabbinic argument is primarily a crescent argument, not a claim that the invisible conjunction itself was sighted. Menachot 29a interprets the word "this" in Exodus 12:2 to mean that God showed Moses the required form of the new moon. Rashi makes the form explicit as the moon in the first stage of its renewal and describes the command as, in effect, seeing that form and sanctifying the month. This is the strongest traditional claim because it supplies exactly what the written verse does not describe: a displayed lunar phase.

The tradition also reads Psalm 81 in the same system. Rosh Hashanah 8a asks which feast occurs when the chodesh is covered and identifies Rosh Hashanah, the first day of the seventh month. This supplies the hidden-moon reading of keseh. The later calculated calendar preserves the inherited beginning of the month; it does not derive conjunction as a separate sign from another verse.

"This month shall be unto you the beginning of months: it shall be the first month of the year to you."Exodus 12:2

The distinction between text and interpretation must remain visible. Exodus says "this month"; it does not record God displaying the moon, identify a crescent, command anyone to look for it, or specify how much light must be visible. Even Rashi first gives the traditional displayed-moon reading and then acknowledges the verse's plain sense: the present month is first in the sequence, so the next is second and the next third. The crescent scene is the rabbinic interpretation of the demonstrative, not a second statement preserved in the verse.

Classifying the Rival Claims

The issue is not whether each claim can fit a dark- or crescent-based calendar. The issue is whether it independently identifies that calendar's starting phase. Here circular means that the proposed calendar first generates a phase and then uses that generated phase to prove the calendar. Ad hoc means that a special meaning or event is supplied for this argument without an independent statement in the written text.

The Two-Date Reading of Psalm 81

Hebrew poetry can coordinate phrases without writing an explicit "and," so grammar alone cannot make a two-date reading impossible. But separating Psalm 81 into two occasions requires the reader to supply that distinction. The one-event reading uses the noun's standard meaning, the singular feast day, the renewed-witness definition, and the geometry without adding a second date.

The "First Stage of Renewal" Counter-Reading

The first crescent really is visible returning light. The question is not whether renewal has begun, but whether Scripture defines the month's sign as the first detectable step. Exodus 12:2 does not say so. Job's construction does not stop at "renew"; it adds the witnesses facing the speaker. Psalm 89 identifies the lunar witness and places the same facing relation in the throne-and-sun line; Psalm 81 then names the full moon. Selecting the first fragment requires the conclusion first; following the completed definition leads to the whole witness at opposition.

Why "Covered" Does Not Redefine the Full Moon

The positive argument does not derive keseh from the verb kasah, "to cover." It begins with the noun's standard meaning: keseh is the full moon in Psalm 81:3 and Proverbs 7:20. A proposed root relationship is secondary; it cannot replace the established meaning of the noun with an invisible conjunction.

“He obscures the face of the full moon and spreads his cloud over it.”Job 26:9 — close rendering of the consonantal text

Job supplies the controlled meaning of “covered.” The unpointed noun is כסה, the same written form used for the full moon in Psalm 81. The object has a face, and God spreads his cloud over it. The surrounding passage remains in the visible creation: earth suspended in space (verse 7), waters bound in clouds (verse 8), the boundary between light and darkness (verse 10), and the heavens made clear by his breath (verse 13). In that setting, the direct physical reading is a full moon whose visible face is obscured by an external cloud.

The traditional pointing reads the noun as kisseh, “throne,” and the KJV consequently prints “the face of his throne.” The consonantal word itself has no possessive suffix; “his” is supplied from the surrounding references to God's cloud and works. Reading keseh, “full moon,” requires no supplied possessor and is adopted by many modern translations. More importantly, it follows the immediate nouns and actions: a face in the heavens with a cloud spread over it.

Scripture therefore supplies more than a disputed root proposal. The pointed Hebrew text distinguishes keseh, “full moon,” from kisse, “throne,” but the nouns share the same unpointed consonantal forms. At the level of the older consonantal text, there is no visible difference between the base forms for “throne” and “full moon”: both can appear as כסא or כסה. Context must decide which sense is active.

The vowel points are a traditional interpretation, not an additional scriptural witness. They can document how the Masoretes read the consonants, but they cannot decide an ambiguity by their own authority. A traditional reading does not gain independent evidentiary weight merely because it was preserved early, printed first, or became familiar. Under the method used throughout this study, the reading must instead be established by the consonantal text, its immediate context, and independent scriptural witnesses.

Psalm 89 names the throne, places it "like the sun facing me," says it is established "like the moon," and immediately calls the celestial body a faithful witness in the sky. The unpointed throne/full-moon form is therefore not an isolated pun imported from elsewhere. It sits inside an expressly solar and lunar sentence, giving the word a controlled double resonance: the enduring throne and the full lunar throne facing the sun across the earth, presenting its complete illuminated face as witness.

Job 26:9 consequently answers the hidden-moon argument rather than supporting it. The thing named is the full moon; the thing doing the covering is God's cloud. “Covered” describes what happens to a visible full-faced object. It does not redefine keseh as the naturally dark conjunction, where no illuminated lunar face is presented to be covered.

This bypasses the debate over hypothetical meanings of “covered.” Psalm 81 and Proverbs name the full moon directly. Job then shows the full moon's face being covered by cloud. Psalm 89 confirms the larger identification through the throne, sun, moon, faithful witness, and facing relation. The texts distinguish the named object from the external act performed upon it: keseh is the full moon; the cloud covers its face.

What This Argument Does Not Depend On

  • It does not infer the month’s beginning from an assumed phase on the fifteenth day.
  • It does not depend on later Jewish sighting procedures or a calculated conjunction.
  • It does not require Exodus 12:2 to contain an unrecorded demonstration of a lunar shape.
  • It does not claim that every use of chadash means "complete" in isolation.
  • It does not depend on choosing among hypothetical meanings of "covered" for keseh.
  • It does not treat the Masoretic vowel points as original consonantal Scripture or allow that tradition to decide the reading by itself.
  • It does not depend on pictographic meanings assigned to Hebrew letters.
  • It does not depend on the noonday-darkness pattern, eclipses at Passover, or any other secondary calendar parallel.

Those other patterns can still be tested after the sign has been identified. The Groping at Noonday study asks what follows if the visible full moon is the head of the month and conjunction its middle. But the definition of the Renewed Moon should not be buried beneath that larger symbolic argument. The renewed-witness path stands first.

Testing a Possible Three-Witness Correspondence

First John names three that bear witness: the Spirit, the water, and the blood. This raises a worthwhile question: do these three witnesses correspond to the sun, stars, and moon? The proposed arrangement must be tested rather than completed by association.

Spirit and Sun

Evidence: “God is Spirit” (John 4:24), and “Jehovah God is a sun” (Psalm 84:11). The same subject supplies the correspondence: God is Spirit, and God is symbolized by the sun.

Assessment: This is an established correspondence, but not an exclusive one. Scripture also pictures the Spirit as living water, seven lamps, and seven eyes (John 7:38–39; Revelation 4:5; 5:6). Solar imagery can likewise present God through Christ and through those who reflect his glory (Matthew 17:2; 13:43). The sun can therefore symbolize God as Spirit without requiring every solar passage to refer specifically to the Spirit.

Water and Stars

Evidence: The waters beneath the harlot are peoples and multitudes (Revelation 17:15; compare Isaiah 17:12–13). Abraham's seed is multiplied as the stars (Genesis 15:5; 22:17; Deuteronomy 1:10). Both images can therefore describe a multitude.

Assessment: This is a possible shared pattern, not an established equation. The prophetic waters represent nations, while the stars in the cited promises represent Abraham's covenant seed. First John's “water” is not identified with Revelation's harlot-waters, and no passage equates water with stars. The proposed link requires an unstated move from peoples generally to the covenant multitude specifically.

Blood and Moon

Evidence: Blood bears witness (1 John 5:8), and the whole moon becomes as blood (Revelation 6:12). A visible blood-colored lunar eclipse requires a full moon.

Assessment: This is a strong correspondence. Both the witness-language and the visible sign are stated, although First John does not itself identify the blood with the moon. The final pairing remains an inference, but it rests on explicit verbal and geometric connections.

The test therefore establishes the Spirit–sun correspondence and finds a strong blood–moon correspondence. The unresolved water–stars link prevents the complete three-part arrangement from entering the primary proof. It remains an observation unless that middle correspondence can be established with the same clarity as the other two.

The Weight of the Evidence

The comparison leaves two simple findings:

  1. Every positive case for beginning at dark conjunction or the first crescent requires special pleading or a non-standard reading. None reaches its phase through ordinary definitions and direct statements without importing the desired result.
  2. Every case against the full moon requires the same—or simple denial. It survives by treating the converging words and relations as unrelated rather than providing a stronger explanation of them.
The weight of the evidence is not close. The dark and crescent readings survive only by adding a premise or changing the normal reading wherever the evidence points toward fullness. When every exception moves in the same direction, the most economical explanation is motivated reading: the desired calendar has been selected first, and each resistant passage is then handled separately.

If First Enoch Is Granted Scriptural Authority

One source does state the rival position more directly: the astronomical chapters of First Enoch. First Enoch 73:4 makes the moon's first visibility its first phase, and 78:12 says, “On the first day she is called the new moon, for on that day the light rises upon her.” That is a direct first-light or crescent claim, not a dark-conjunction claim. If First Enoch is Scripture, its wording is more explicit than the full-moon case assembled here.

But that changes the question. The contest is no longer between two equally natural readings of the same scriptural passages. It is between the cumulative case for the full moon from the books already accepted as Scripture and the case that First Enoch must also be accepted as Scripture and allowed to decide the calendar. First Enoch can function as a historical witness to a competing calendar without functioning as a divine witness. Before its calendar can overrule the pattern established here, its own claim to scriptural authority must carry greater weight than the full-moon case it contradicts.
