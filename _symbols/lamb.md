# Lamb

## Summary

**Jesus Christ — the unblemished Passover sacrifice, slain to redeem His people and now reigning.**
> *"And I beheld, and, lo, in the midst of the $[throne] and of the four beasts, and in the midst of the elders, stood a **Lamb** as it had been slain, having seven horns and seven eyes, which are the seven Spirits of God sent forth into all the earth."* — Rev 5:6

---

## Definitions

### Symbolic Definition

**Jesus Christ — the unblemished Passover sacrifice, slain to redeem His people and now reigning.**

### Literal Biblical Definition

*Not yet stated separately. This is a research gap: the literal sense must be derived from ordinary biblical uses, not copied from Webster.*

### Webster's Definition

Webster's 1913 entry for **lamb** begins: “(Zoöl.) Defn: The young of the sheep.”

These definitions may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Key Insight

The Hebrew word for the sacrificial lamb — H3532 *kebes* — comes from an unused root meaning **to dominate**. The very word for the animal led to slaughter contains dominion in its root.

This is not an accident of etymology. It is the grammar of the whole Bible compressed into a single word: authority is *earned* by sacrifice, not seized by force. The lamb that gives its life *is* the one who rules.

Revelation makes this visible. In Rev 5, no one in heaven or earth can open the scroll of destiny. An elder says, "the Lion of the tribe of Judah hath prevailed." John turns to see the Lion — and sees a **Lamb as it had been slain** (Rev 5:6). The Lion *is* the Lamb. The one who conquers is the one who was killed. He stands — alive, resurrected — bearing the marks of slaughter, and He stands *in the midst of the throne*. Seven horns: complete power. Seven eyes: complete knowledge. All authority flows from the wound.

This is the pattern that separates every true claim to authority from every counterfeit. The $dragon gives the $beast his $throne and great authority (Rev 13:2) — power seized by violence and deception. But the beast that rises from the earth has "two horns like a **lamb**" while speaking "as a $[dragon]" (Rev 13:11). The counterfeit *imitates* the lamb's appearance but cannot replicate the slaying. It looks like sacrifice but speaks domination. That is the test: authority by sacrifice versus authority by seizure.

**Symbolizes:** Innocent life given as substitutionary sacrifice — which through self-giving earns all authority

**Opposite:** The $dragon / $beast — authority seized by violence and deception rather than earned by sacrifice (Rev 13:2 vs. Rev 5:6)

**Defining verses:** Rev 5:6, Jn 1:29, Gen 22:7–8, Exod 12:3–5, Isa 53:7

**The surprise:** Four different Hebrew and Greek words trace a progression from generic flock animal to enthroned ruler — and the sacrificial word (H3532 *kebes*) carries "dominion" in its root. The lamb was always destined for the $throne.

**Connected:** $blood, $throne, $wrath, $fire, $shepherd, $marriage, $firstborn

## Lamb Across Scripture

### "Where Is the Lamb?"

> *"And Isaac spake unto Abraham his father, and said, My father: and he said, Here am I, my son. And he said, Behold the **$[fire]** and the wood: but where is the **lamb** for a burnt offering?"* — Gen 22:7

> *"And Abraham said, My son, God will provide himself a **lamb** for a burnt offering: so they went both of them together."* — Gen 22:8

Isaac's question is the question of Scripture. Where is the lamb? The $fire is ready, the wood is laid — but the sacrifice is missing. Abraham's answer is not a deflection; it is prophecy: *God will provide himself a lamb*.

The Hebrew (H7716 *seh*) is the broadest term — any flock animal set apart. Isaac does not yet know the specific identity of the sacrifice. He knows only that fire requires a lamb, and no lamb is visible. The question hangs in the air for centuries.

It is heard again at the Passover altar, at every morning and evening sacrifice, in every trespass offering, every guilt offering, every unblemished yearling brought to the priest. "Where is the lamb?" And each time, the answer is provisional — this lamb, for now, for this breach, until the one God provides.

### The Passover Lamb — Blood That Exempts from Judgment

> *"Speak ye unto all the congregation of Israel, saying, In the tenth day of this month they shall take to them every man a **lamb**, according to the house of their fathers, a **lamb** for an house... Your **lamb** shall be without blemish, a male of the first year."* — Exod 12:3, 5

The Passover lamb is not merely killed. It is *selected* on the tenth day, *kept* until the fourteenth, and only then slain. For four days the family lives with the animal they will sacrifice. They know it. It knows them. The identification between household and lamb is made personal before the $blood is shed.

And the $blood — the life of the flesh made visible (Lev 17:11) — is not poured on the altar but displayed on the doorposts and lintel of each house. When the destroyer passes through Egypt, what he sees is not the family inside but the $blood outside. The lamb's life, visibly offered, exempts the $firstborn from death.

> *"And the $[blood] shall be to you for a token upon the houses where ye are: and when I see the $[blood], I will pass over you, and the plague shall not be upon you to destroy you."* — Exod 12:13

This is the defining equation: the lamb dies so the $firstborn lives. Life given, life spared. The destroyer cannot touch what the $blood covers. And 1 Peter brings the strands together:

> *"Forasmuch as ye know that ye were not redeemed with corruptible things, as silver and gold... But with the precious **$[blood]** of Christ, as of a **lamb** without blemish and without spot."* — 1 Pet 1:18–19

"Without blemish" (G299 *amomos*) and "without spot" (G784 *aspilos*) — the Passover requirements applied directly to Christ. The Passover was not a metaphor looking for a meaning. It was a pattern waiting for its fulfillment.

### Four Words, One Arc

Scripture uses four different words for "lamb," and they trace a progression that maps the entire story:

**H7716** ***seh*** — the broadest term: any flock member, sheep or goat. This is the word in Gen 22:7 ("where is the *seh*?") and Exod 12:3 ("take a *seh*"). It is the generic animal set apart from the flock for sacrifice or identity. In Isa 53:7, the Suffering Servant is "as a *seh* to the slaughter." The word appears when the lamb is still unidentified — known only as one drawn from the many.

**H3532** ***kebes*** — the sacrificial lamb specifically, the term used for the daily offerings (Exod 29:38–41, Num 28:3), for the purification rites (Lev 4:32, 14:10–25), and for the Sabbath and feast offerings (Lev 23:12, Num 28:3–13). This is the lamb on the altar, day after day, morning and evening. Its root — "to dominate" — encodes the paradox: the one appointed for slaughter carries dominion in its name.

**G286** ***amnos*** — the lamb identified and pointed to. This word appears only four times in the New Testament: twice from the mouth of John the Baptist (Jn 1:29, 1:36), once quoting Isaiah 53 (Acts 8:32), and once in Peter's epistle (1 Pet 1:19). Every occurrence is a moment of *recognition*: "Behold! THE Lamb of God." The definite article matters. Not *a* lamb. *The* Lamb. The one all other lambs anticipated.

**G721** ***arnion*** — the diminutive form, the "little lamb." This is the word John uses 29 times in Revelation — and *only* in Revelation — for Christ in glory. The diminutive emphasizes vulnerability: this is a lambkin, not a ram. Yet this lambkin has seven horns (complete power) and seven eyes (complete knowledge). He opens the seals. He receives worship. His $wrath makes mountains tremble. The smallest word for lamb carries the greatest authority.

The progression: *seh* (generic, set apart) → *kebes* (sacrificial, on the altar) → *amnos* (identified, pointed to) → *arnion* (enthroned, reigning). From flock to altar to recognition to $throne. The lamb's journey *is* the biblical story.

### "He Opened Not His Mouth"

> *"He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth: he is brought as a **lamb** to the slaughter, and as a sheep before her shearers is dumb, so he openeth not his mouth."* — Isa 53:7

The lamb's silence is not passive weakness. It is deliberate restraint. Isaiah emphasizes this by saying it *twice*: "he opened not his mouth... so he openeth not his mouth." The doubling is intentional — this silence is chosen.

The Ethiopian eunuch, reading this passage in his chariot, asks Philip the critical question: "Of whom speaketh the prophet this? of himself, or of some other man?" (Acts 8:34). Philip "began at the same scripture, and preached unto him Jesus" (Acts 8:35). The lamb identified.

What makes the silence significant is who is being silent. This is the one who could summon twelve legions of angels (Mat 26:53). The Lion of Judah who chooses to be the silent Lamb. The one with all power at his disposal who *refuses to use it for self-preservation*. The silence is not the absence of power — it is the supreme expression of it.

### "Behold the Lamb of God"

> *"The next day John seeth Jesus coming unto him, and saith, Behold the **Lamb** of God, which taketh away the sin of the world."* — Jn 1:29

> *"And looking upon Jesus as he walked, he saith, Behold the **Lamb** of God!"* — Jn 1:36

John the Baptist's declaration answers Abraham's prophecy. "God will provide himself a lamb" (Gen 22:8) → "Behold the Lamb of God" (Jn 1:29). The question asked on Mount Moriah receives its answer at the Jordan River.

The verb is G142 *airo* — to take up, to carry away, to *remove*. Not merely to cover sin (as the Levitical system did temporarily) but to take it away entirely. The definite article — *the* Lamb (*ho amnos*) — signals that this is the singular, final answer to the question "where is the lamb?" Every Passover lamb, every daily sacrifice, every morning and evening offering was a placeholder. Now the placeholder is replaced by the reality.

John says it twice — on consecutive days. In Jewish law, a matter is established by two witnesses (Deut 19:15). The double declaration is testimony: this is the one.

### The Lamb on the Throne

Revelation transforms the lamb from sacrifice to sovereign — not by abandoning the sacrifice but by *enthroning* it. The slain Lamb does not become something else. He remains slain. The wound marks stay. But the slain one now occupies the center of all authority.

> *"And I beheld, and I heard the voice of many angels round about the $[throne]... saying with a loud voice, Worthy is the **Lamb** that was slain to receive power, and riches, and wisdom, and strength, and honour, and glory, and blessing."* — Rev 5:11–12

Seven attributes — a complete endowment of authority. And the basis? "That was slain." The worthiness flows from the sacrifice. Power is *received* because life was *given*. This is the principle that H3532 *kebes* (dominion-root) embedded in the sacrificial word from the beginning.

> *"And said to the mountains and rocks, Fall on us, and hide us from the face of him that sitteth on the $[throne], and from the **$[wrath]** of the **Lamb**: For the great day of his **$[wrath]** is come; and who shall be able to stand?"* — Rev 6:16–17

"Wrath of the Lamb" is one of the most arresting phrases in Scripture. Lambs do not rage. Lambs do not terrify. Yet the $wrath — the settled covenantal response to sustained rebellion, the judicial consequence poured out when the measure of iniquity is full — belongs to the slain one. The sacrifice rejected becomes judgment executed. Those who refused the $blood that exempts must face the Lamb whose $blood they refused.

> *"And the **Lamb** shall overcome them: for he is Lord of lords, and King of kings: and they that are with him are called, and chosen, and faithful."* — Rev 17:14

The Lamb *overcomes*. The word here — G3528 *nikao* — is the same word used for the overcomers in the seven letters (Rev 2–3). The Lamb overcame by sacrifice; those with Him overcome by the same means:

> *"And they overcame him by the **$[blood]** of the **Lamb**, and by the word of their testimony; and they loved not their lives unto the death."* — Rev 12:11

The $blood of the Lamb — that life of the flesh made visible, offered rather than taken — becomes the weapon that defeats the $dragon. Not force against force, but sacrifice against violence. And the overcomers participate in the same pattern: "they loved not their lives unto the death." The Lamb's method becomes the method of His people.

### The Lamb Becomes the Shepherd

> *"For the **Lamb** which is in the midst of the $[throne] shall feed them, and shall lead them unto living fountains of waters: and God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes."* — Rev 7:17

The word "feed" is G4165 *poimaino* — to shepherd. The $shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep (Jn 10:11) is revealed to have been the Lamb all along. The one who was led to slaughter now leads to living waters. The one who was fed upon (sacrificed) now feeds His flock. The roles are not contradictory — they are sequential. The Lamb becomes the $shepherd *because* He was the Lamb first.

Hebrews makes the connection explicit:

> *"Now the God of peace, that brought again from the dead our Lord Jesus, that great $[shepherd] of the sheep, through the **$[blood]** of the everlasting covenant..."* — Heb 13:20

The great $shepherd is identified by the $blood of the everlasting covenant. Shepherd-authority flows from lamb-sacrifice. The $blood — the life given — ratifies the covenant. The risen Shepherd carries the lamb's $blood as His credential.

### The Lamb Slain Before the Foundation

> *"And all that dwell upon the earth shall worship him, whose names are not written in the book of life of the **Lamb** slain from the foundation of the world."* — Rev 13:8

"From the foundation of the world" (G2602 *katabole*) — before creation began, the Lamb was already slain. This is not retroactive accounting. It is pre-creation purpose. The sacrifice was not a contingency plan after the fall. It was the design before the foundation was laid.

This means every lamb offered from Abel forward was not building toward an unforeseen conclusion — it was enacting a pattern already settled in eternity. Abel's offering (Gen 4:4 — "the firstlings of his flock") was an echo of a reality that preceded it. The Passover lamb was a shadow cast backward from a cross that existed in the mind of God before time.

### The Marriage of the Lamb

> *"Let us be glad and rejoice, and give honour to him: for the $[marriage] of the **Lamb** is come, and his wife hath made herself ready."* — Rev 19:7

> *"Blessed are they which are called unto the $[marriage] supper of the **Lamb**."* — Rev 19:9

The story ends where $marriage always pointed: covenant consummation. The $marriage — the full covenant picture — reaches its fulfillment in the union of the Lamb and His bride. The Lamb who gave His life now receives His people. The one who purchased with $blood now celebrates with joy.

> *"Come hither, I will shew thee the bride, the **Lamb's** wife."* — Rev 21:9

And in the final city:

> *"And I saw no temple therein: for the Lord God Almighty and the **Lamb** are the temple of it. And the city had no need of the sun, neither of the moon, to shine in it: for the glory of God did lighten it, and the **Lamb** is the light thereof."* — Rev 21:22–23

> *"And there shall be no more curse: but the $[throne] of God and of the **Lamb** shall be in it."* — Rev 22:3

The Lamb *is* the temple. The Lamb *is* the light. The Lamb shares the $throne. The sacrifice has become the structure of eternal reality. There is no building to enter because the Lamb is the meeting place between God and man. There is no sun because the Lamb's glory illuminates everything. There is no curse because the one who bore the curse now reigns from the $throne.

The river of life flows "out of the $throne of God and of the Lamb" (Rev 22:1) — sustaining life eternally from the seat of sacrificial authority.

## Patterns

1. **Dominion through sacrifice, never through seizure.** H3532 *kebes* carries dominion in its root. Rev 5:12 grants all power to the Lamb *because* He was slain. Rev 13:11 exposes the counterfeit: looks like a lamb, speaks like a $dragon. Authority by sacrifice is the test that separates true from false.

2. **The lamb is always without blemish.** Exod 12:5 (H8549 *tamim*), 1 Pet 1:19 (G299 *amomos* + G784 *aspilos*). The sacrifice must be whole, complete, without defect. The offering's perfection is what gives the exchange its validity — unblemished life covers the blemished.

3. **The four-word progression maps the story.** H7716 *seh* (set apart from flock) → H3532 *kebes* (placed on altar) → G286 *amnos* (identified and pointed to) → G721 *arnion* (enthroned and reigning). Generic → sacrificial → recognized → glorified. The lamb moves from anonymity to glory through sacrifice.

4. **Silence is the lamb's chosen posture.** Isa 53:7, Acts 8:32 — the lamb does not protest, does not defend, does not retaliate. The silence is not weakness but the supreme exercise of power withheld. The one who could speak judgment chooses to absorb it.

5. **The slain marks remain in glory.** Rev 5:6 — "as it had been slain" (G4969 *sphazo*). The Lamb in heaven still bears the evidence of sacrifice. The wounds are not healed away. They are the credential of authority. What was done cannot be undone, and need not be — it is the source of all that follows.

6. **Lamb and $shepherd are the same figure.** Jn 10:11 — the good $shepherd gives His life for the sheep. Rev 7:17 — the Lamb shepherds the redeemed. Heb 13:20 — the great $shepherd operates through the $blood of the everlasting covenant. The one led to slaughter becomes the one who leads to living water.

7. **The lamb's $blood is a weapon.** Rev 12:11 — they overcame the $dragon by the $blood of the Lamb. Not physical force but covenant life offered — the visible life given by the sacrifice — defeats the accuser. The $dragon has no answer for voluntary sacrifice.

8. **The counterfeit cannot replicate the slaying.** Rev 13:11 — two horns *like* a lamb, but the voice of a $dragon. The false $beast can imitate the lamb's appearance (mild, gentle, religious) but cannot reproduce the actual self-giving death. The test is always: has this authority been purchased by sacrifice, or seized by deception?

## Relationship to Other Symbols
- **$blood** — The life of the flesh made visible. When the lamb's $blood is offered, it atones (Lev 17:11); when displayed, it marks what belongs to God (Exod 12:13); when applied, it purifies (Rev 7:14 — robes washed white in $blood). The lamb and its $blood are inseparable: the sacrifice is the offering, and the $blood is the evidence that the offering was made.

- **$throne** — The legitimate seat of governing authority. The Lamb stands *in the midst of* the $throne (Rev 5:6), shares the $throne with the Father (Rev 22:1, 3), and receives seven-fold ascription *because* He was slain (Rev 5:12). Who sits on the $throne defines the age — and at the consummation, it is the Lamb.

- **$wrath** — God's settled covenantal response to sustained rebellion. The paradox of Rev 6:16–17 is that $wrath belongs to the Lamb. The sacrifice that was refused becomes the judgment that falls. Saints are exempted from $wrath through the Lamb's $blood (Rom 5:9, 1 Thess 5:9); those who reject the $blood face the Lamb's $wrath directly.

- **$fire** — God's holy standard in active contact. The $fire and the lamb appear together from the beginning: "Behold the $fire and the wood: but where is the lamb?" (Gen 22:7). $fire consumes what violates covenant and refines what keeps it — the lamb is what stands between the worshipper and the consuming $fire. The daily lamb offering (Num 28:3) was placed on the altar of perpetual $fire.

- **$shepherd** — The covenant caretaker who feeds, leads, knows by name, and gives His life for the flock. The good $shepherd *is* the Lamb — the same figure in two phases. First He dies as the Lamb (Jn 10:11, 15); then He leads as the $shepherd (Rev 7:17). The root of shepherd-authority is lamb-sacrifice.

- **$marriage** — The covenant picture. The Lamb's $marriage (Rev 19:7, 9) is the consummation of God's covenant with His people. The bride is the Lamb's wife (Rev 21:9). The entire biblical arc — from promise to law to sacrifice to resurrection — moves toward the $marriage supper of the Lamb.

- **$firstborn** — The covenant heir. Egypt's $firstborn die because Pharaoh holds God's $firstborn (Israel) captive (Exod 4:22–23). The Passover lamb dies so that the $firstborn lives. Christ is both: the $firstborn of all creation (Col 1:15), the $firstborn from the dead (Col 1:18), *and* the Lamb whose $blood redeems every $firstborn. He is simultaneously the sacrifice and the one the sacrifice protects.

## For Further Study

- **$blood** — The life of the flesh made visible. The lamb's $blood atones, marks, and purifies — but $blood also cries from the ground when taken unjustly. How do these two modes of $blood (offered vs. taken) define the difference between sacrifice and murder?
- **$throne** — The seat of governing authority. The Lamb shares the $throne with the Father in the final vision. How does the $throne study illuminate the transfer of authority from the scroll-holder to the Lamb in Rev 5?
- **$shepherd** — The covenant caretaker. The Lamb becomes the $shepherd — but what happens when shepherds *become* wolves? The $shepherd study traces the contrast between the good $shepherd and the hireling.
- **$firstborn** — The covenant heir. The Passover lamb dies so the $firstborn lives — but Christ is both Lamb and $firstborn simultaneously. How does the $firstborn pattern of bypassed natural order (Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau) illuminate the Lamb's claim to the $throne?
- **$marriage** — The covenant picture. The story ends at the $marriage supper of the Lamb. How does the $marriage study unpack the implications of the bride "making herself ready" (Rev 19:7)?
- **$fire** — God's holy standard in active contact. Isaac saw the $fire and asked where the lamb was. How does the $fire study illuminate why sacrifice and $fire are inseparable in every covenant context?

## Dictionary Comparison

Webster's 1913 entry for **lamb** begins:

> “Lamb, n. Etym: [AS. lamb; akin to D. & Dan. lam, G. & Sw. lamm, OS., Goth., & Icel. lamb.] 1. (Zoöl.) Defn: The young of the sheep.”

The dictionary supplies the ordinary sense. The definition above is narrower or figurative only where Scripture's own cross-references require that transfer.

## Competing Definitions Tested

The definition has changed since the preserved independent judgment. Objections aimed at the earlier wording are historical, not evidence against this replacement. The current definition needs a fresh relationship judgment; persuasion is needed only if that new judgment still finds a divergent core.

## Conclusion

**Jesus Christ — the unblemished Passover sacrifice, slain to redeem His people and now reigning.**

This is the glossary conclusion; broader applications in the study remain subordinate to the defining texts and countertexts.

## Corpus and Method

This study checks the glossary's defining citations first, then compares them with every KJV verse that prints *lamb*, *lamb*, *lambkin*, *sheep*, *flock*. The English-form sweep found **491 verses**. It is exhaustive for those printed forms, but it is not a substitute for a Strong's-number sweep where several Hebrew or Greek words share one English translation.
