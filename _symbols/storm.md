### Webster's Definition

Webster's 1913 entry for **storm** begins: “A violent disturbance of the atmosphere, attended by wind, rain, snow, hail, or thunder and lightning; hence, often, a heavy fall of rain, snow, or hail, whether accompanied with wind or not.”

These definitions may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

Nations in turmoil, pictured as the raging sea that is stilled by Christ’s word “Peace, be still.”

> *“Which stilleth the noise of the seas, the noise of their waves, and the tumult of the people.”* — Psalms 65:7

> *“Woe to the multitude of many people, which make a noise like the noise of the seas; and to the rushing of nations, that make a rushing like the rushing of mighty waters!”* — Isaiah 17:12

> *“The nations shall rush like the rushing of many waters: but God shall rebuke them, and they shall flee far off, and shall be chased as the chaff of the mountains before the wind, and like a rolling thing before the whirlwind.”* — Isaiah 17:13

> *“And he arose, and rebuked the wind, and said unto the sea, Peace, be still. And the wind ceased, and there was a great calm.”* — Mark 4:39

> *“And there shall be signs in the sun, and in the moon, and in the stars; and upon the earth distress of nations, with perplexity; the sea and the waves roaring;”* — Luke 21:25

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Competing Definitions Tested

A common reading is: **divine judgment, and the trials of life which Christ has power to calm.**

- The entry partly transfers sea-and-waters symbolism to storm/tempest. Isaiah 17 and Psalm 65 directly connect roaring waters with peoples or nations, but Mark 4 only records Christ calming a literal storm, and Luke 21 may juxtapose national distress and roaring seas rather than explicitly equate them. Thus the national identification is strongest in the prophetic parallels, while the Christological synthesis is more inferential.

- Psalm 65:7 explicitly parallels the noise of the seas with 'the tumult of the people,' and Isaiah 17:12-13 directly likens rushing nations to rushing waters chased before the whirlwind, with Luke 21:25 pairing 'distress of nations' with 'the sea and the waves roaring' — so the sea/waters-as-nations equation is genuinely scriptural, and one could argue the book's reading is only a specification of the consensus's chaos/hostile-powers alternative rather than a full replacement. However, those texts establish the sea/waters trope more than the storm/tempest itself, and applying it to Mark 4:39 remains an interpretive leap the printed verses do not compel.

- Prophetic usage most often makes the storm the vehicle of judgment rather than the nations themselves, and popular Christian application of the Gospel pericopes most often treats personal “storms of life”; the book’s national focus, though textually grounded, may over-specify one strand.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
