# Lion

## Approved Definition

**A royal and devouring ruler or empire. Wicked rulers, Assyria, and Babylon bear the predatory figure; its rightful holder is the Lion of Judah.**
> *"And one of the elders saith unto me, Weep not: behold, the **Lion** of the tribe of $[judah], the Root of David, hath prevailed to open the book, and to loose the seven seals thereof. And I beheld, and, lo, in the midst of the $[throne] and of the four beasts… stood a **$[lamb]** as it had been slain"* — Rev 5:5–6

---

## Definition Layers

### Bible symbolic sense

**A royal and devouring ruler or empire. Wicked rulers, Assyria, and Babylon bear the predatory figure; its rightful holder is the Lion of Judah.**

### Bible literal sense

*Not yet stated separately. This is a research gap: the literal sense must be derived from ordinary biblical uses, not copied from Webster.*

### Webster's English sense

Webster's 1913 entry for **lion** begins: “(Zoöl.) Defn: A large carnivorous feline mammal (Felis leo), found in Southern Asia and in most parts of Africa, distinct varieties occurring in the different countries.”

These layers may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Key Insight

John hears "Lion." He turns and sees a $lamb.

This is not a bait-and-switch. It is the single most important statement Scripture makes about power. The elder announces the Lion of $judah — the one with sovereign authority to open the sealed book. But when John looks, he does not see a conquering predator. He sees a slaughtered Lamb standing upright. The Lion *is* the Lamb. True dominion is expressed through sacrifice, not domination.

This paradox is the interpretive key to every lion in Scripture. The lion is not a symbol of good or evil. It is a symbol of **authority itself** — the raw capacity to rule, to devour, to command obedience. What determines the outcome is *whose hand wields it*. Christ is the Lion of Judah. Satan prowls as a roaring lion. Wicked rulers are devouring lions over the poor. The same image, the same power — different wielders.

The $lamb is the lion's opposite, but not as weakness opposes strength. The $lamb reveals how the Lion *chose* to exercise His dominion: through self-giving rather than through devouring. The roar of the Lion is the silence of the Lamb on the altar.

**Symbolizes:** Sovereign dominion — authority exercised, whether in righteous rule or predatory oppression

**Opposite:** The $lamb — power expressed through sacrifice rather than domination (Rev 5:5–6). The Lion *is* the Lamb; they are not two different beings but two expressions of the same authority

**Defining verses:** Rev 5:5–6, Gen 49:9, 1 Pet 5:8, Prov 28:1, Prov 28:15, Dan 7:4

**The surprise:** Both Christ *and* Satan claim lion identity. The symbol is not inherently righteous — it is raw dominion. And the only true Lion in Scripture turns out to be a slaughtered Lamb.

**Connected:** $lamb, $bear, $eagle, $dragon, $beast, $sheep, $shepherd, $throne, $judah, $crown, $serpent

## Lion Across Scripture

### Judah's Lion — The King Who Lies Down

> *"$[judah] is a **lion's** whelp: from the prey, my son, thou art gone up: he stooped down, he couched as a **lion**, and as an old **lion**; who shall rouse him up?"* — Gen 49:9

Jacob's blessing over $judah is the first explicit identification of a person-as-lion in Scripture. The language is dense with Hebrew: H738 *ari* (lion) and H3833 *labiy* (old lion, lioness — the mature, fully realized predator). Judah is first a whelp, then a lion returning from the hunt, then a lion couched and at rest.

The critical phrase: **"who shall rouse him up?"** This is not a question about sleep. It is a declaration of unchallenged authority. The lion lies down when it chooses, and no one dares disturb it. The position of rest *is* the position of dominion.

The very next verse ties this lion directly to kingship: "The sceptre shall not depart from Judah, nor a lawgiver from between his feet, until Shiloh come" (Gen 49:10). The lion image flows immediately into the $throne image. Lion = the royal tribe. The line to David, to Christ, to the Lion of Rev 5:5 begins here.

Balaam's oracles pick up the same imagery for the whole nation:

> *"Behold, the people shall rise up as a great **lion**, and lift up himself as a young **lion**: he shall not lie down until he eat of the prey"* — Num 23:24

> *"He couched, he lay down as a **lion**, and as a great **lion**: who shall stir him up? Blessed is he that blesseth thee, and cursed is he that curseth thee."* — Num 24:9

When Israel is aligned with God, the nation *is* the lion. The blessing-curse formula that accompanies this image echoes Abraham's covenant (Gen 12:3). The lion-nature of Israel is a covenant reality — present when they walk in covenant, absent when they depart from it.

### The Roaring Lion — Satan's Counterfeit

> *"Be sober, be vigilant; because your adversary the devil, as a roaring **lion**, walketh about, seeking whom he may devour"* — 1 Pet 5:8

If Christ is the Lion of Judah, Satan claims the same title. The $dragon presents himself as a lion — a counterfeit sovereignty. But notice what Peter emphasizes: *roaring*. The roar is intimidation. The $serpent uses stealth and cunning (Gen 3:1); the lion uses terror and open force. Satan's lion-mode is not deception — it is the overwhelming display of power designed to make prey freeze.

This dual usage — Christ as Lion, Satan as lion — confirms that the symbol itself is not moral. It is *dominion*. The question Scripture asks is not "is this a lion?" but "whose lion is this?"

The same duality appears in the Psalms. David cries:

> *"Lest he tear my soul like a **lion**, rending it in pieces, while there is none to deliver"* — Ps 7:2

> *"Like as a **lion** that is greedy of his prey, and as it were a young **lion** lurking in secret places"* — Ps 17:12

And in the Messianic psalm of the cross:

> *"They gaped upon me with their mouths, as a ravening and a roaring **lion**… Save me from the **lion's** mouth"* — Ps 22:13, 21

Here the lions are the powers arrayed against the Suffering Servant — political, spiritual, imperial. The same dominion that Gen 49:9 assigns to Judah is turned *against* Judah's heir. The lion devours the Lion.

### The Wicked Ruler — Lion Over the Poor

> *"As a roaring **lion**, and a ranging $[bear]; so is a wicked ruler over the poor people."* — Prov 28:15

> *"The **righteous** are bold as a **lion**."* — Prov 28:1

These two proverbs sit in the same chapter, and together they frame the lion's dual nature. The righteous person has lion-boldness — confidence from right standing with God. The wicked ruler has lion-ferocity — devouring authority turned against those under his care.

The pairing with the $bear in Prov 28:15 is not incidental. The lion and the $bear appear together repeatedly in Scripture as a matched set of predatory powers:

- **David's proving:** "Thy servant slew both the lion and the bear" (1 Sam 17:34–37). The $shepherd who protects $sheep must overcome both predators before facing the empire (Goliath).
- **Wicked rulers:** "A roaring lion and a ranging bear" (Prov 28:15).
- **Empires:** Lion = Babylon (Dan 7:4), $bear = Medo-Persia (Dan 7:5).
- **God in judgment:** "He was unto me as a bear lying in wait, and as a lion in secret places" (Lam 3:10).
- **The final $beast:** "His mouth as the mouth of a lion, and his feet were as the feet of a bear" (Rev 13:2).

This lion-$bear pairing creates an escalating-threat pattern. The lion strikes first — visible, roaring, kingly. The $bear follows — slower, more crushing, relentless. Together they represent the full spectrum of predatory authority.

### The Lion's Throne — Kingship Made Visible

> *"The $[throne] had six steps, and the top of the $[throne] was round behind: and there were stays on either side on the place of the seat, and two **lions** stood beside the stays. And twelve **lions** stood there on the one side and on the other upon the six steps: there was not the like made in any kingdom."* — 1 Ki 10:19–20

Solomon's $throne is guarded by fourteen lions — two at the seat, twelve on the steps. The lions do not merely decorate; they *define* the throne. They are the visible sign that kingship and lion-dominion are one thing. "There was not the like made in any kingdom" — because no other kingdom had received the lion-promise of Gen 49:9.

In Revelation's throne room, the first of the four living creatures has "the face of a lion" (Rev 4:7). The lion-face is present before God's $throne itself — kingship and majesty represented at the highest level of heavenly reality. This is not metaphor. It is the foundational attribute of divine governance: sovereign dominion.

### The Lion as Judgment Agent

Two striking narratives reveal the lion as a *precise* instrument of divine judgment — not random predation:

> *"And when he was gone, a **lion** found him, and slew him… And he went and found his carcase cast in the way, and the **lion** standing by the carcase: the **lion** had not eaten the carcase, nor torn the ass."* — 1 Ki 13:24, 28

A disobedient prophet is killed by a lion — but the lion does not eat the body or attack the donkey. It simply stands guard beside the corpse. This is controlled violence, not animal hunger. The lion acts as a judicial executioner: precise, limited, purposeful. Its dominion is under God's command.

> *"And so it was at the beginning of their dwelling there, that they feared not the LORD: therefore the LORD sent **lions** among them, which slew some of them."* — 2 Ki 17:25–26

When foreigners settle in Samaria without knowing the God of the land, lions enforce the covenant boundary. The land itself, through its predators, rejects those who do not fear the LORD. The lion here is territorial authority — the covenant guardian of a space that belongs to God.

Isaiah reveals that God Himself assumes the lion-identity in judgment:

> *"For thus hath the LORD spoken unto me, Like as the **lion** and the young **lion** roaring on his prey, when a multitude of $[shepherd]s is called forth against him, he will not be afraid of their voice, nor abase himself for the noise of them: so shall the LORD of hosts come down to fight for mount Zion"* — Isa 31:4

A multitude of $shepherds cannot frighten the LORD. He is the lion roaring over His prey — and His prey is the defense of Zion. This is not predation; it is protection. The same lion-power that devours in 1 Pet 5:8 guards in Isa 31:4. The wielder determines the outcome.

### The Empires as Lions — Daniel's Vision

> *"The first was like a **lion**, and had $[eagle]'s wings: I beheld till the wings thereof were plucked, and it was lifted up from the earth, and made stand upon the feet as a man, and a man's heart was given to it."* — Dan 7:4

Babylon is the lion-empire — the first $beast in Daniel's vision. It has $eagle's wings (speed, soaring imperial reach), but the wings are plucked. It is forced to stand upright like a man and given a human heart. This mirrors Nebuchadnezzar's personal journey in Dan 4: the king driven to beastly madness, then restored to sanity with a human heart. The lion-empire is *humbled* — its predatory flight-power removed, its wild nature tamed into something human.

What happens to Daniel's four separate beasts — lion, $bear, leopard, iron-monster — is remarkable. In Rev 13:2, they are *compressed into one*:

> *"And the $[beast] which I saw was like unto a leopard, and his feet were as the feet of a **$[bear]**, and his mouth as the mouth of a **lion**: and the $[dragon] gave him his power, and his seat, and great authority."* — Rev 13:2

Four empires spanning centuries merge into a single composite $beast. The lion contributes its **mouth** — devouring speech, imperial decrees, the voice of authority. The $bear contributes its crushing feet. The leopard contributes its swift, spotted body. The $dragon supplies the power behind them all. History's predatory empires are not left behind — they accumulate into a final system that bears every mark of every previous one.

The lion's mouth in the composite $beast matters. Of all the lion's attributes, it is the *mouth* — the devouring, decreeing organ — that endures. Dominion exercised through speech. Commands that consume.

### The Lion Defanged — The Kingdom Restored

> *"The cow and the $[bear] shall feed; their young ones shall lie down together: and the **lion** shall eat straw like the ox."* — Isa 11:7

> *"The wolf and the $[lamb] shall feed together, and the **lion** shall eat straw like the bullock: and dust shall be the $[serpent]'s meat."* — Isa 65:25

In the restored kingdom, the lion's predatory nature is removed. It does not cease to exist — it eats straw. The power remains, but the devouring is gone. The lion is returned to its pre-Fall state, coexisting with the ox, the $lamb, the child.

Note the parallel in Isa 65:25: the lion eats straw, and the $serpent eats dust. Both predatory powers — the lion (open dominion) and the $serpent (hidden cunning) — are neutralized simultaneously. The kingdom does not destroy them; it *tames* them. Authority exists without predation. The lion lies down with the $lamb not because the lion has become weak, but because dominion has been purified of its violence.

### Samson's Riddle — Sweetness from the Strong

> *"And behold, a young **lion** roared against him. And the Spirit of the LORD came mightily upon him, and he rent him as he would have rent a kid, and he had nothing in his hand… And after a time he returned to take her, and he turned aside to see the carcase of the **lion**: and, behold, there was a swarm of bees and honey in the carcase of the **lion**."* — Judg 14:5–6, 8

> *"Out of the eater came forth meat, and out of the strong came forth sweetness."* — Judg 14:14

The Spirit empowers Samson to overcome the lion — raw dominion conquered by divine power. Then the dead lion becomes a vessel for honey — sweetness from the carcass of the strong. The riddle encodes a pattern: when predatory dominion is slain, it becomes a source of life. The devourer, defeated, yields food.

This foreshadows the lion-$lamb paradox of Revelation. The Lion's power, expressed through the $lamb's sacrifice, produces life from death. The strong one's carcass becomes sweetness.

## Patterns

1. **Dual lion — same power, different wielders.** Christ is the Lion of Judah (Rev 5:5). Satan walks about as a roaring lion (1 Pet 5:8). Wicked rulers are devouring lions (Prov 28:15). The righteous are bold as lions (Prov 28:1). The symbol does not belong to one side. It is raw authority — the question is always *who holds it*.

2. **Lion = Lamb.** Rev 5:5–6 announces a Lion and reveals a $lamb. The truest exercise of sovereign dominion is self-sacrifice. This is not weakness disguised as strength — it is strength expressed through self-giving. The Lion conquers *by being* the slain Lamb.

3. **Lion + Bear: the paired predators.** David kills both (1 Sam 17:34–37). Proverbs pairs them as wicked rulers (Prov 28:15). Daniel separates them as empires (Dan 7:4–5). Revelation merges them into one $beast (Rev 13:2). God Himself takes both forms in judgment (Lam 3:10). Together they represent the complete range of predatory authority — the lion's roaring strike and the $bear's crushing weight.

4. **The mouth of the lion.** When Daniel's beasts merge in Rev 13:2, the lion contributes its *mouth*. Paul is "delivered out of the mouth of the lion" (2 Tim 4:17). David pleads to be saved from "the lion's mouth" (Ps 22:21). The lion's primary weapon is not its claws but its devouring voice — decrees, accusations, consuming speech.

5. **Lions as territorial enforcers.** In 1 Ki 13:24–28, the lion kills precisely and no more. In 2 Ki 17:25–26, lions police the covenant land against those who do not know the LORD. In 1 Ki 10:19–20, lions guard the $throne. Lions enforce boundaries — of obedience, of territory, of royal authority.

6. **The defanged lion.** Isa 11:7 and 65:25 — the lion eats straw. Job 4:10 — the lion's teeth are broken. The kingdom does not destroy sovereign power; it removes its predatory nature. Authority remains. Violence does not.

## Relationship to Other Symbols
- **$lamb** — The lion's defining opposite and paradoxical identity. Rev 5:5–6 is the hinge: the Lion of Judah *is* the slaughtered $lamb. True sovereign dominion is exercised through covenant sacrifice, not through devouring. The lamb does not replace the lion; it reveals what the lion's authority was always meant to look like.

- **$bear** — The lion's consistent companion in predatory pairs. Wherever the lion represents the first wave of devouring authority (roaring, visible, kingly), the $bear follows as the second (crushing, relentless, overwhelming). Together they map the full threat of predatory rule — from David's proving ground to Daniel's empires to the final composite $beast.

- **$dragon** — The power behind the counterfeit lion. In Rev 13:2, the $dragon gives the beast (with its lion-mouth) its power, seat, and authority. Satan *roars* as a lion (1 Pet 5:8) but his true form is the $dragon — the ancient $serpent who supplies the authority that false empires wield.

- **$beast** — Empire as devouring lion. Dan 7:4 presents Babylon as a winged lion. Rev 13:2 merges all Daniel's $beasts into one composite with a lion's mouth. The $beast is what happens when lion-dominion serves the $dragon instead of God.

- **$eagle** — Paired with the lion in Dan 7:4 (lion with eagle's wings) and Rev 4:7 (lion-face and eagle-face among the four living creatures before the $throne). The $eagle adds swiftness and height to the lion's earthbound dominion.

- **$serpent** — Where the lion represents *open* predatory authority (roaring, visible), the $serpent represents *hidden* predatory cunning (subtle, deceptive). Both are neutralized in the kingdom: the lion eats straw, the $serpent eats dust (Isa 65:25). Dan, remarkably, receives both symbols — lion's whelp (Deut 33:22) and $serpent (Gen 49:17).

- **$sheep** — The lion's prey. David the $shepherd kills the lion to protect $sheep (1 Sam 17:34–37). The righteous $shepherd must overcome lion-dominion to guard the flock. The good $shepherd *becomes* the $lamb — the Lion of Judah who lays down His life for the $sheep.

- **$shepherd** — The one who stands between the lion and the $sheep. In Isa 31:4, a multitude of $shepherds cannot frighten the LORD when He roars as a lion. The divine $shepherd is the greater Lion.

- **$throne** — The lion guards the $throne (1 Ki 10:19–20), represents the $throne (Rev 4:7), and inherits the $throne (Rev 5:5). Lion-dominion and $throne-authority are inseparable throughout Scripture.

- **$judah** — The lion's tribe. Gen 49:9 assigns the lion to $judah; Rev 5:5 completes it. The Lion of $judah is the rightful king — the one whose dominion was promised in Jacob's blessing and realized in Christ.

- **$crown** — The lion wears the $crown of legitimate authority. The counterfeit lion (Satan, the $beast) seeks the $crown that belongs to the Lion of Judah.

## Dictionary Comparison

Webster's 1913 entry for **lion** begins:

> “Li”on, n. Etym: [F. lion, L. leo, -onis, akin to Gr. Chameleon, Dandelion, Leopard.] 1. (Zoöl.) Defn: A large carnivorous feline mammal (Felis leo), found in Southern Asia and in most parts of Africa, distinct varieties occurring in the different countries. The adult male, in most varieties, has a thick mane of long shaggy hair that adds to his apparent size, which is less than that of the largest tigers. The length, however, is sometimes eleven feet to the base of the tail. The color is a tawny yellow or yellowish brown; the mane is darker, and the terminal tuft of the tail is black. In one variety, called the maneless lion, the male has only a slight mane.”

The dictionary supplies the ordinary sense. The approved definition above is narrower or figurative only where Scripture's own cross-references require that transfer.

## Conclusion

**A royal and devouring ruler or empire. Wicked rulers, Assyria, and Babylon bear the predatory figure; its rightful holder is the Lion of Judah.**

This is the approved glossary conclusion; broader applications in the study remain subordinate to the defining texts and countertexts.

## Corpus and Method

This study checks the glossary's defining citations first, then compares them with every KJV verse that prints *lion*, *lions*, *lions whelp*, *young lion*, *old lion*, *lioness*. The English-form sweep found **119 verses**. It is exhaustive for those printed forms, but it is not a substitute for a Strong's-number sweep where several Hebrew or Greek words share one English translation.

## Competing Definitions Tested

- Because the consensus makes Christ the paradigmatic primary referent while the book makes devouring rulers and empires primary, one could classify the entries as divergent at the level of central identification. Against that, the consensus expressly recognizes the book’s referents as common lion applications, and the book expressly retains Christ, making boundary reclassification the better fit. The printed texts strongly support wicked rulers, princes, Assyria, Babylon, and Christ as lion figures, but they do not by themselves establish the claim that the lion is tamed in Christ’s kingdom.

- One could argue DIVERGENT: the book replaces the consensus's central referent (Christ) with a different primary referent (the beast-kingdom/wicked ruler), and 'the figure's rightful holder' framing subordinates rather than affirms the Christological reading; if primary identification is the core, the cores differ. Additionally, the closing claim that 'in His kingdom the lion is tamed' has no printed citation (Isa 11:6-7 is absent), weakening the entry's self-supported completeness.

- The consensus emphasizes 1 Peter 5:8 (Satan as a prowling, roaring lion) as a major, equally prominent negative usage in Christian teaching. The book's entry does not explicitly include this, unless Satan is implicitly subsumed under the 'beast-kingdom' ruler concept.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
