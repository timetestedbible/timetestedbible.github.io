# Sleep

**Death — and the spiritual unawareness that resembles it.**

> *"Wherefore he saith, Awake thou that **sleepest**, and arise from the $[dead], and Christ shall give thee $[light]."* — Eph 5:14

---

## The Key Insight

Paul says three things in a single sentence — and the middle one reveals the symbol:

1. Awake thou that **sleepest**
2. Arise from the **dead**
3. Christ shall give thee **$light**

The sleeper is told to *arise from the dead*. The equation is not subtle. Sleep and death are placed in synonymous parallelism — one consciousness, one command, one condition. To sleep is to be dead. To awake is to arise. And the result of arising is $light — visible awareness, the opposite of everything sleep represents.

This is not a metaphor unique to Paul. When Jesus stood before a dead girl, He said, "The maid is not dead, but **sleepeth**" (Mat 9:24). They laughed at Him — they knew she was dead. When Lazarus lay in a tomb, Jesus told His disciples, "Our friend Lazarus **sleepeth**; but I go, that I may awake him out of sleep." The disciples thought He meant natural rest. John adds the gloss: *"Howbeit Jesus spake of his death"* (Jn 11:11–13).

Scripture uses sleep for death because death, for those in covenant, is *temporary unconsciousness*. A sleeper is not annihilated — only unresponsive. And a sleeper can be awakened. This is the logic that runs from Genesis through Revelation: the dead are not gone; they are asleep. And one day, they wake up.

But the symbol does not stop at the grave. If the physically dead are "asleep," then the spiritually dead are "asleep" too — unresponsive to God, unconscious of the times, unaware that the $night is ending and the $day is about to break.

**Symbolizes:** Death — both physical (temporary unconsciousness awaiting resurrection) and spiritual (unawareness of God's reality and timing)

**Opposite:** Awake / Watch — alertness to spiritual reality, readiness for Christ's return

**Defining verses:** Eph 5:14, 1 Thess 5:6–7, Jn 11:11–13, Dan 12:2, Rom 13:11

**The surprise:** The call to "awake" is not directed at unbelievers. In every epistle where it appears, the audience is *believers* — those already in covenant but living as though they were dead.

**Connected:** $light, $darkness, $night, $day

## Sleep Across Scripture

### The Dead Are Sleeping

The oldest and most pervasive use of sleep in Scripture is as a name for death. Dozens of times in Kings and Chronicles, the death of a king is recorded with a single phrase: "he **slept** with his fathers." This is not poetic decoration — it is a theological claim. Death is not an ending. It is a state of unconsciousness from which one can be called back.

Daniel makes the claim explicit, reaching all the way to the resurrection:

> *"And many of them that **sleep** in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt."* — Dan 12:2

The sleepers are in the *dust* — the language of burial, of Gen 3:19 ("unto dust shalt thou return"). But they do not remain there. They *awake*. The dead are not beyond reach; they are merely unconscious, waiting for the voice that will rouse them.

The psalmist grasps the same equation from the other direction — not from hope but from dread:

> *"Consider and hear me, O LORD my God: lighten mine eyes, lest I **sleep** the **sleep** of death."* — Ps 13:3

"Lighten mine eyes" — give me $light, give me awareness — *lest I fall into that unconsciousness*. Sleep is what happens when the eyes close. Death is what happens when they close permanently. The prayer is: keep me conscious, keep me responsive, keep me awake to You.

### "Not Dead, but Sleepeth"

Three gospels record the same scene. Jairus' daughter is dead. The mourners are weeping. Jesus arrives and makes a statement that produces laughter:

> *"He said unto them, Give place: for the maid is not dead, but **sleepeth**. And they laughed him to scorn."* — Mat 9:24

> *"And he said unto them, Why make ye this ado, and weep? the damsel is not dead, but **sleepeth**."* — Mk 5:39

> *"And all wept, and bewailed her: but he said, Weep not; she is not dead, but **sleepeth**."* — Lk 8:52

They laughed because they knew she was dead. Jesus was not correcting their medical diagnosis. He was redefining the category. In His presence, death is not death — it is sleep. He can wake her. And He does.

The Lazarus account makes the identification even more deliberate:

> *"These things said he: and after that he saith unto them, Our friend Lazarus **sleepeth**; but I go, that I may awake him out of **sleep**. Then said his disciples, Lord, if he **sleep**, he shall do well. Howbeit Jesus spake of his **death**: but they thought that he had spoken of taking of rest in **sleep**."* — Jn 11:11–13

John adds the authorial clarification because the disciples misunderstand. But the misunderstanding *is the point*. The word is the same. The condition looks the same — an unresponsive body, eyes closed, unable to hear. The only difference is who is speaking the wake-up call.

### "Fallen Asleep in Christ"

Paul carries the same usage into the epistles, and it becomes his standard term for the believing dead:

> *"After that, he was seen of above five hundred brethren at once; of whom the greater part remain unto this present, but some are **fallen asleep**."* — 1 Cor 15:6

> *"Then they also which are **fallen asleep** in Christ are perished. If in this life only we have hope in Christ, we are of all men most miserable."* — 1 Cor 15:18–19

The logic of 1 Cor 15:18 is telling. If there is no resurrection, then those "asleep in Christ" have *perished* — they are truly gone. But because resurrection is real, sleep is exactly the right word: temporary, reversible, awaiting the morning.

The pastoral heart of the metaphor appears in 1 Thessalonians, where believers are grieving their dead:

> *"But I would not have you to be ignorant, brethren, concerning them which are **asleep**, that ye sorrow not, even as others which have no hope. For if we believe that Jesus died and rose again, even so them also which **sleep** in Jesus will God bring with him."* — 1 Thess 4:13–14

"Sorrow not as others which have no hope." The distinction is not between sorrow and no sorrow, but between sorrow *with* hope and sorrow *without* it. Those who sleep in Jesus will be brought with Him. The sleep has an end. The alarm is set.

### The Spiritual Dead Are Sleeping Too

If death is sleep, then spiritual death is spiritual sleep — and this is precisely how the epistles treat it. What makes this striking is that the audience in every case is *believers*:

> *"And that, knowing the time, that now it is high time to awake out of **sleep**: for now is our salvation nearer than when we believed."* — Rom 13:11

Paul is writing to believers in Rome — people who have already believed. Yet he tells them to *awake out of sleep*. They are not physically dead. They are not unbelievers. They are covenant people who have become spiritually unconscious — unaware of the times, living as if the $day were not approaching.

> *"Therefore let us not **sleep**, as do others; but let us watch and be sober. For they that **sleep sleep** in the **$[night]**; and they that be drunken are drunken in the **$[night]**."* — 1 Thess 5:6–7

Here the full symbolic cluster comes into view. Sleep belongs to $night. Drunkenness belongs to $night. The two conditions — unconsciousness and impaired judgment — are the defining characteristics of those who live in the season of hiddenness and unawareness.

The opposite is not merely "being awake" in some vague sense. It is *watching* (G1127 *grēgoreō* — to be vigilant, alert, on guard) and being *sober* (G3525 *nēphō* — clear-minded, not intoxicated). Sleep is to spiritual life what $night is to $day: the season when eyes close, when perception fails, when the thief comes unnoticed.

### The Full Chain: Sleep → Death → Awake → Light

Ephesians 5:14 compresses the entire symbol into one command:

> *"Wherefore he saith, Awake thou that **sleepest**, and arise from the $[dead], and Christ shall give thee **$[light]**."*

Three movements:

1. **Awake** — stop being unconscious
2. **Arise from the dead** — sleep *is* death; to awake is to be resurrected
3. **Receive $light** — the result of waking is visibility, awareness, example

This verse connects sleep to the $light / $darkness cluster explicitly. The preceding verses describe the contrast:

> *"For ye were sometimes **$[darkness]**, but now are ye **$[light]** in the Lord: walk as children of **$[light]**."* — Eph 5:8

> *"And have no fellowship with the unfruitful works of **$[darkness]**, but rather reprove them."* — Eph 5:11

The chain runs: $darkness → sleep → death on one side. $light → awake → life on the other. Sleep is the *condition* of those in $darkness — unconscious, unresponsive, hidden from accountability. Waking is the transition from $darkness to $light, from death to life, from unawareness to visible obedience.

### The Profile of the Sleeper

Taken together, these passages sketch a profile of the spiritually asleep — and it is not a portrait of the irreligious. It is a portrait of believers who have drifted into unconsciousness:

They are **unaware of the times** — "knowing the time, that now it is high time to awake" (Rom 13:11). Like the turtledove of Jer 8:7 who knows her appointed times while God's people do not, the sleeper has lost track of the calendar.

They are **unresponsive to God's voice** — "awake thou that sleepest" is a command that implies someone is *being spoken to* and not hearing. The sleeper is not absent. He is present but unconscious.

They are **unprepared for the $day** — "the $[day] of the Lord so cometh as a thief in the $[night]" (1 Thess 5:2). The sleeper is the one caught off guard. The watcher is not.

They are **living in the $night** — "they that sleep sleep in the $[night]" (1 Thess 5:7). The sleeper's natural habitat is the season of hiddenness. Not because sleep causes $night, but because $night is where sleep happens.

## Patterns

1. **Sleep = temporary death.** Every use of sleep for physical death carries an implicit promise: what sleeps can be awakened. "Slept with his fathers" is not a permanent goodbye — it is a waiting room. Dan 12:2 and 1 Thess 4:14 confirm the sleep ends at resurrection.

2. **The call to awake is always directed at covenant people.** Rom 13:11 (believers), Eph 5:14 (the church), 1 Thess 5:6 (brethren). Scripture does not tell the world to "wake up." It tells God's people to wake up. The sleeper is inside the house, not outside it.

3. **Sleep clusters with $night and drunkenness.** 1 Thess 5:7 places all three together: "they that sleep sleep in the $night; and they that be drunken are drunken in the $night." Unconsciousness, hiddenness, and impaired judgment form a single condition.

4. **The opposite of sleep is not merely wakefulness but watchfulness.** G1127 *grēgoreō* — to watch, to guard — is the specific antonym. The awake person is not just conscious; they are *on duty*, alert to what is coming, ready.

5. **Sleep makes the $day come as a thief.** "The $[day] of the Lord so cometh as a thief in the $[night]" (1 Thess 5:2). The thief does not come because it is $night — the thief succeeds because the household is *asleep*. Wakefulness removes the surprise: "But ye, brethren, are not in $[darkness], that that $[day] should overtake you as a thief" (1 Thess 5:4).

6. **Eph 5:14 encodes the full symbolic chain.** Sleep → death → awake → alive → $light. One verse connects the death-metaphor, the spiritual-unawareness sense, and the $light / $darkness cluster into a single movement of transformation.

## Connections

- **$light** — The gift that comes to the one who wakes. "Awake thou that sleepest… and Christ shall give thee $light" (Eph 5:14). Sleep is the state that has no $light; waking is how $light is received. Those who sleep cannot see, cannot be seen, cannot be an example.

- **$darkness** — The environment of sleep. "Ye were sometimes $darkness, but now are ye $light" (Eph 5:8) precedes the call to awake (Eph 5:14). $darkness conceals; sleep makes one unconscious to the concealment itself. $darkness is the state; sleep is the condition of the person in that state.

- **$night** — The season of sleep. "They that sleep sleep in the $night" (1 Thess 5:7). $night is the time-frame of hiddenness; sleep is the unconsciousness that belongs to it. When the $night ends and the $day comes, the sleeper is caught unprepared.

- **$day** — The coming exposure that sleep renders catastrophic. "The $day of the Lord cometh as a thief in the $night" (1 Thess 5:2). The sleeper misses the $day's arrival. The watcher does not. The call to "awake out of sleep" (Rom 13:11) is specifically grounded in the nearness of $day.

## Hebrew & Greek Reference

| Strong's | Word | Meaning | Notes |
|----------|------|---------|-------|
| H3462 | *yashen* | to sleep, to be asleep | Used for both natural sleep and death ("slept with his fathers") |
| H8639 | *tardemah* | deep sleep | The divinely imposed sleep of Gen 2:21 (Adam) and Gen 15:12 (Abraham) |
| H8142 | *shenah* | sleep | "Lest I sleep the sleep of death" (Ps 13:3) |
| G2518 | *katheudō* | to sleep, to be asleep | Used by Jesus for death (Mat 9:24, Mk 5:39) and by Paul for spiritual unawareness (1 Thess 5:6–7, Eph 5:14) |
| G2837 | *koimaomai* | to fall asleep, to sleep | Paul's standard term for death of believers: "fallen asleep" (1 Cor 15:6, 18; 1 Thess 4:13–15) |
| G1127 | *grēgoreō* | to watch, be vigilant | The direct opposite of sleep — "let us not sleep… but let us watch" (1 Thess 5:6) |
| G3525 | *nēphō* | to be sober, clear-minded | Paired with watching as the opposite of sleep and drunkenness (1 Thess 5:6, 8) |

KJV translates G2837 *koimaomai* as "fallen asleep," "asleep," "sleep." G2518 *katheudō* appears as "sleep," "sleepeth," "sleeping." The two Greek words divide roughly along the death/unawareness line — *koimaomai* predominantly for the dead, *katheudō* for both death and spiritual sleep — though the symbolic meaning is unified.
