### Webster's Definition

Webster's 1913 entry for **garden** begins: “A piece of ground appropriates to the cultivation of herbs, fruits, flowers, or vegetables.”

These definitions may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

Garden signifies a planted collection of trees and, symbolically, a grouping of nations.

> *“I have made him fair by the multitude of his branches: so that all the trees of Eden, that were in the garden of God, envied him.”* — Ezekiel 31:9

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Dictionary Comparison

## Competing Definitions Tested

A common reading is: **paradise and fellowship with God — Eden lost and restored.**

- Ezekiel 31:9 depicts multiple personified trees within the garden of God and thus supports a collective-tree image, but the quoted verse alone does not identify the garden itself as nations; that inference depends on the wider chapter’s political-tree allegory.

- Ezekiel 31 demonstrably uses tree imagery for nations and places those trees 'in the garden of God,' so one could argue the book is only isolating a legitimate context-specific usage (as Response B notes, garden meaning is context-dependent), making this an unlisted complementary layer (NOVEL or even REFINED) rather than a replacement. But the entry offers the nations reading as the definition of the symbol and explicitly contrasts it with the commonly taught paradise view, which is displacement, not layering. The single cited verse supports trees-as-nations more directly than garden-as-grouping-of-nations, and cannot by itself carry the generalization across Genesis, Song of Songs, and Revelation — hence only partial citation support.

- Ezek 31:9 still embeds the trees inside ‘the garden of God’ and ‘trees of Eden,’ preserving the paradise frame; the trees (not the garden) are the nations, so the verse does not redefine garden itself as a national grouping.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
