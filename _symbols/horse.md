### Webster's Definition

Webster's 1913 entry for **horse** begins: “(Zoöl.) Defn: A hoofed quadruped of the genus Equus; especially, the domestic horse (E. caballus), which was domesticated in Egypt and Asia at a very early period.”

These definitions may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

The mount of an executing spirit; the conqueror who wins the nations by the bow (citing only Rev 6:2).

> *“And I saw, and behold a white horse: and he that sat on him had a bow; and a crown was given unto him: and he went forth conquering, and to conquer.”* — Revelation 6:2

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Dictionary Comparison

Webster's 1913 entry for **horse** begins:

> “course, current Cf. Walrus.] 1. (Zoöl.) Defn: A hoofed quadruped of the genus Equus; especially, the domestic horse (E. caballus), which was domesticated in Egypt and Asia at a very early period. It has six broad molars, on each side of each jaw, with six incisors, and two canine teeth, both above and below. The mares usually have the canine teeth rudimentary or wanting. The horse differs from the true asses, in having a long, flowing mane, and the tail bushy to the base. Unlike the asses it has callosities, or chestnuts, on all its legs. The horse excels in strength, speed, docility, courage, and nobleness of character, and is used for drawing, carrying, bearing a rider, and like purposes.”

The dictionary supplies the ordinary sense. The definition above is narrower or figurative only where Scripture's own cross-references require that transfer.

3. **Boundary:** the sense applies where the passage activates the same relationship; ordinary literal uses remain literal.

## Competing Definitions Tested

- Revelation 6:2 directly supports the bow, crown, and conquest, but it does not identify the rider as a spirit or explicitly state that the conquered objects are nations. Those elements require interpretation beyond the printed citation.

- One could argue MATCH: 'conqueror by the bow' is simply Rev 6:2 restated, and 'executing spirit' merely echoes the Zechariah agent background the consensus itself cites, so the differences are wording. Against this, the claim that the horse is specifically the mount of an executing spirit (tied to the four winds) is a definite doctrinal specification the consensus does not settle on, and the entry's restriction away from Rev 19 and general war symbolism is a real change of extension, not mere phrasing.

- The definition entirely omits the consensus’s most widely cited white horse (Rev 19 Christ) and supplies ‘executing spirit’ plus ‘wins the nations’ without direct warrant in the single printed verse.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
