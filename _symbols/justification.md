# Justify, justification

## Approved Definition

**To judge, declare, or demonstrate a person, word, deed, or cause right against a charge; the opposite is to condemn. God justifies the sinner through forgiveness in Christ, not by declaring wrongdoing right.**

The fuller book and notebook trail is available in [Uncommon Sanctification, Justification, and Cleanliness](/books/symbolic-language/uncommon-sanctification-justification-and-cleanliness/), [The End of the Law](/books/symbolic-language/the-end-of-the-law/).

## Definition Layers

### Bible symbolic sense

*No separate symbolic sense is asserted. This entry recovers a biblical word meaning rather than interpreting an image.*

### Bible literal sense

**To judge, declare, or demonstrate a person, word, deed, or cause right against a charge; the opposite is to condemn. God justifies the sinner through forgiveness in Christ, not by declaring wrongdoing right.**

### Webster's English sense

Webster's 1913 entry for **justify** begins: “To prove or show to be just; to vindicate; to maintain or defend as conformable to law, right, justice, propriety, or duty.”

These layers may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

To justify is to judge, declare, or demonstrate a person, claim, deed, or cause right against a charge. In salvation, God answers the sinner’s charge by gracious forgiveness and non-imputation; works can vindicate professed faith but cannot earn mercy.

> *“If there be a controversy between men, and they come unto judgment, that the judges may judge them; then they shall justify the righteous, and condemn the wicked.”* — Deuteronomy 25:1

> *“He that justifieth the wicked, and he that condemneth the just, even they both are abomination to the LORD.”* — Proverbs 17:15

> *“The Son of man came eating and drinking, and they say, Behold a man gluttonous, and a winebibber, a friend of publicans and sinners. But wisdom is justified of her children.”* — Matthew 11:19

> *“And all the people that heard him, and the publicans, justified God, being baptized with the baptism of John.”* — Luke 7:29

> *“Being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus:”* — Romans 3:24

> *“Whom God hath set forth to be a propitiation through faith in his blood, to declare his righteousness for the remission of sins that are past, through the forbearance of God;”* — Romans 3:25

> *“To declare, I say, at this time his righteousness: that he might be just, and the justifier of him which believeth in Jesus.”* — Romans 3:26

> *“Now to him that worketh is the reward not reckoned of grace, but of debt.”* — Romans 4:4

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Competing Definitions Tested

A common reading is: **God's once-for-all declaration that Christ's righteousness is imputed to a believer through faith apart from works.**

- Romans 4:6 explicitly says that God “imputeth righteousness,” which weakens the entry’s contrast between forgiveness and declaring or reckoning righteousness. Moreover, the consensus’s forensic declaration need not mean falsely relabeling intrinsic ungodliness; it may denote a legal status grounded in Christ, so the book may be rejecting a caricature of the common view.

- One could argue the book's remark that God forgives 'rather than renaming ungodliness righteousness,' combined with Proverbs 17:15 (justifying the wicked is abomination), amounts to a rejection of the imputation of Christ's righteousness — the very mechanism the primary consensus reading names — and thus contradicts rather than refines it. However, since the consensus itself flags the mechanism as contested (Responses B and C mark no settled consensus) and the book retains gracious forensic acquittal by faith apart from works, this is best read as a boundary dispute within a shared core rather than a replaced referent.

- The book's 'forgiving rather than renaming ungodliness righteousness' can be read as rejecting the very imputation of righteousness that consensus (and Rom 4:5-6 among the printed citations) treat as definitional, potentially elevating the difference from boundary refinement to core contradiction.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
