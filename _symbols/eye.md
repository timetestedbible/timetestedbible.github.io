### Webster's English sense

Webster's 1913 entry for **eye** begins: “(Zoöl.) Defn: A brood; as, an eye of pheasants.”

These layers may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

The eye is the lamp of the body (an inner burning light), a measure of generosity or withholding ('good' vs. 'evil' eye), and a representation of God's all-seeing presence running throughout the earth.

> *“The light of the body is the eye: if therefore thine eye be single, thy whole body shall be full of light.”* — Matthew 6:22

> *“But if thine eye be evil, thy whole body shall be full of darkness. If therefore the light that is in thee be darkness, how great is that darkness!”* — Matthew 6:23

> *“He that hath a bountiful eye shall be blessed; for he giveth of his bread to the poor.”* — Proverbs 22:9

> *“Eat thou not the bread of him that hath an evil eye, neither desire thou his dainty meats:”* — Proverbs 23:6

> *“For who hath despised the day of small things? for they shall rejoice, and shall see the plummet in the hand of Zerubbabel with those seven; they are the eyes of the LORD, which run to and fro through the whole earth.”* — Zechariah 4:10

> *“His head and his hairs were white like wool, as white as snow; and his eyes were as a flame of fire;”* — Revelation 1:14

> *“And I beheld, and, lo, in the midst of the throne and of the four beasts, and in the midst of the elders, stood a Lamb as it had been slain, having seven horns and seven eyes, which are the seven Spirits of God sent forth into all the earth.”* — Revelation 5:6

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Competing Definitions Tested

- One could argue REFINED on the ground that the book makes the generosity/stinginess idiom definitional (part of the core entry) rather than one debated alternative among several, and that the 'shining as the stars shine' linkage to stellar symbolism is a material addition not found in any consensus response. However, since the consensus already lists the generosity reading as a common interpretation of the very same verses, elevating its prominence does not change the extension of cases covered, and the star comparison is imagery rather than a changed identification.

- It could be argued that the book's phrase 'shining as the stars shine' introduces a novel poetic association not explicitly stated in the consensus, though it is a minor elaboration on the 'burning light within.'

- The distinctive addition of 'shining as the stars shine' and the seamless fusion of the lamp metaphor with the generosity idiom may reflect the book's private symbolic system rather than mainstream consensus boundaries for those verses.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
