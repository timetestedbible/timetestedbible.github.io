# Fig leaves

### Webster's Definition

Webster's 1913 entry for **fig** begins: “(Bot.) Defn: A small fruit tree (Ficus Carica) with large leaves, known from the remotest antiquity.”

These definitions may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

Fig leaves signify self-made righteousness: a self-fashioned covering that has outward appearance but no genuine spiritual substance.

> *“And the eyes of them both were opened, and they knew that they were naked; and they sewed fig leaves together, and made themselves aprons.”* — Genesis 3:7

> *“And when he saw a fig tree in the way, he came to it, and found nothing thereon, but leaves only, and said unto it, Let no fruit grow on thee henceforward for ever. And presently the fig tree withered away.”* — Matthew 21:19

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Dictionary Comparison

Webster's 1913 entry for **fig** begins:

> “Fig, n. Etym: [F. figue the fruit of the tree, Pr. figa, fr. L. ficus fig tree, fig. Cf. Fico.] 1. (Bot.) Defn: A small fruit tree (Ficus Carica) with large leaves, known from the remotest antiquity. It was probably native from Syria westward to the Canary Islands.”

The dictionary supplies the ordinary sense. The definition above is narrower or figurative only where Scripture's own cross-references require that transfer.

## Competing Definitions Tested

- Genesis 3:7 describes improvised covering but does not explicitly identify it as righteousness or declare it inadequate, while Matthew 21:19 concerns a fruitless fig tree rather than Adam and Eve’s covering. The citations support the component images, but the unified doctrine of self-made righteousness is interpretive.

- One could argue the book slightly REFINES by promoting the hypocrisy/fruitlessness motif (a consensus 'alternative') into the primary definition, and that 'self-made righteousness' as opposed to mere shame-covering is an inference not explicit in Gen 3:7 alone (the standard contrast text Gen 3:21 is not printed). But since the consensus already treats the blended reading as the dominant homiletical usage, this does not materially change extension or boundary.

- It could be argued that the Matthew 21:19 reference primarily applies to the 'leaves' of the fig tree rather than the specific phrase 'fig leaves' as a constructed garment, but the consensus explicitly recognizes this New Testament motif as a common alternative meaning for the term.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
