# Clean, unclean; cleanse, cleansing

## Summary

**Clean means free from defilement and fit for permitted contact, approach, or use; to cleanse is to remove that defilement. Uncleanness may be physical, ceremonial, or moral and does not always imply guilt; sanctification concerns holy claim and use, not merely cleanliness.**

The fuller book and notebook trail is available in [Uncommon Sanctification, Justification, and Cleanliness](/books/symbolic-language/uncommon-sanctification-justification-and-cleanliness/), [research-tohar-clearness.md](https://github.com/timetestedbible/timetestedbible.github.io/blob/main/books/symbolic-language/research/research-tohar-clearness.md).

### Literal Biblical Definition

**Clean means free from defilement and fit for permitted contact, approach, or use; to cleanse is to remove that defilement. Uncleanness may be physical, ceremonial, or moral and does not always imply guilt; sanctification concerns holy claim and use, not merely cleanliness.**

### Webster's Definition

Webster's 1913 entry for **clean** begins: “Free from dirt or filth; as, clean clothes.”

These definitions may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

The book defines cleanness functionally as freedom from any defilement that prevents proper contact, approach, or use. It includes physical, ceremonial, and moral uncleanness, explicitly denies that all uncleanness entails guilt, and distinguishes cleansing from sanctification.

> *“And that ye may put difference between holy and unholy, and between unclean and clean;”* — Leviticus 10:10

> *“Speak unto the children of Israel, saying, If a woman have conceived seed, and born a man child: then she shall be unclean seven days; according to the days of the separation for her infirmity shall she be unclean.”* — Leviticus 12:2

> *“The woman also with whom man shall lie with seed of copulation, they shall both bathe themselves in water, and be unclean until the even.”* — Leviticus 15:18

> *“He that toucheth the dead body of any man shall be unclean seven days.”* — Numbers 19:11

> *“Wash me throughly from mine iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin.”* — Psalms 51:2

> *“Create in me a clean heart, O God; and renew a right spirit within me.”* — Psalms 51:10

> *“That he might sanctify and cleanse it with the washing of water by the word,”* — Ephesians 5:26

> *“But if we walk in the light, as he is in the light, we have fellowship one with another, and the blood of Jesus Christ his Son cleanseth us from all sin.”* — 1 John 1:7

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Dictionary Comparison

Webster's 1913 entry for **clean** begins:

> “Clean, a. [Compar. Cleaner (; superl. Cleanest.] Etym: [OE. clene, AS. cl; akin to OHG. chleini pure, neat, graceful, small, G. klein small, and perh. to W. glan clean, pure, bright; all perh. from a primitive, meaning bright, shining. Cf. Glair.] 1. Free from dirt or filth; as, clean clothes.”

The dictionary supplies the ordinary sense. The definition above is narrower or figurative only where Scripture's own cross-references require that transfer.

## Competing Definitions Tested

A common reading is: **hygiene or obsolete ritual regulation, or collapsed into moral purity and holiness.**

- The printed texts clearly establish both non-sinful ritual uncleanness and cleansing from sin, but they only indirectly establish the book’s comprehensive functional formula of fitness for contact, approach, or use and its sharp conceptual sequencing of cleansing before sanctification.

- One could argue the book's core is simply the consensus's listed alternative (non-moral ritual status) elevated to primary, so the relation is closer to MATCH-with-an-alternative than REFINED against the primary; alternatively, one could press that rejecting 'unclean as synonym for moral purity' contradicts the consensus primary and warrants DIVERGENT. Both objections are weakened because the book retains moral cleansing (Ps 51; 1 John 1) within its definition, making the disagreement a matter of scope and boundary rather than replaced referent.

- Dominant confessional use equates the terms to moral purity and Christ's sanctifying work; the book's multi-category functional emphasis (and cleansing/sanctification split) can appear to dilute or de-emphasize that primary spiritual reading.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
