### Webster's Definition

Webster's 1913 entry for **elder** begins: “Older; more aged, or existing longer.”

These definitions may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

The leaders of the assemblies, seated among the host of heaven — the court round about the throne, the named and brightest of the stars.

> *“And he said, Hear thou therefore the word of the LORD: I saw the LORD sitting on his throne, and all the host of heaven standing by him on his right hand and on his left.”* — 1 Kings 22:19

> *“Then the moon shall be confounded, and the sun ashamed, when the LORD of hosts shall reign in mount Zion, and in Jerusalem, and before his ancients gloriously.”* — Isaiah 24:23

> *“And round about the throne were four and twenty seats: and upon the seats I saw four and twenty elders sitting, clothed in white raiment; and they had on their heads crowns of gold.”* — Revelation 4:4

> *“And Jesus said unto them, Verily I say unto you, That ye which have followed me, in the regeneration when the Son of man shall sit in the throne of his glory, ye also shall sit upon twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel.”* — Matthew 19:28

> *“And the heavens shall praise thy wonders, O LORD: thy faithfulness also in the congregation of the saints.”* — Psalms 89:5

> *“For who in the heaven can be compared unto the LORD? who among the sons of the mighty can be likened unto the LORD?”* — Psalms 89:6

> *“God is greatly to be feared in the assembly of the saints, and to be had in reverence of all them that are about him.”* — Psalms 89:7

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Dictionary Comparison

> “Eld”er, a. Etym: [AS. yldra, compar. of eald old. See Old.] 1. Older; more aged, or existing longer. Let the elder men among us emulate their own earlier deeds. Jowett (Thucyd. )”

The dictionary supplies the ordinary sense. The definition above is narrower or figurative only where Scripture's own cross-references require that transfer.

## Evidence by Sense

The entry currently states one controlling sense:

1. **Core sense:** Leaders and representatives of the assemblies, seated as a court around the throne — the constellations among the host of heaven and their [stars](/research/symbols/stars/).
2. **Defining witnesses:** 1 Kgs 22:19; Isa 24:23; Rev 4:4; Matt 19:28; Ps 89:5-7
3. **Boundary:** the sense applies where the passage activates the same relationship; ordinary literal uses remain literal.

## Competing Definitions Tested

- The cited passages support a heavenly court, elders around the throne, and enthroned apostolic judges, but they do not by themselves identify the elders as members of the heavenly host or as the ‘named and brightest’ stars. That final stellar equation depends on an unstated interpretive link or the cross-referenced chapter.

- One could argue DIVERGENT: by seating the elders 'among the host of heaven' as 'the court round about the throne,' the book effectively adopts the heavenly-council identification, which the consensus frames as an alternative that competes with (and for many interpreters excludes) the 'redeemed humanity' reading; and the astral identification could be called NOVEL since no response links elders to stars. The rebuttal is that the divine-council reading is explicitly listed as a common consensus alternative, the book still calls them 'leaders of the assemblies' (a human, consensus-compatible referent), and the star imagery is a symbolic overlay rather than a new referent — so the disagreement is at the boundary, not the center.

- The consensus generally treats redeemed human leaders and the angelic host/divine council as mutually exclusive interpretations of the twenty-four elders, whereas the book applies the titles of the heavenly host and stars directly to the leaders of the assemblies.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
