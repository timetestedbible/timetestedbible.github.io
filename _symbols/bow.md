### Webster's Definition

Webster's 1913 entry for **bow** begins: “To cause to deviate from straightness; to bend; to inflect; to make crooked or curved.”

These definitions may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

The bow signifies the covenant that binds a people into a kingdom together with the military might that outwardly expresses that covenant; breaking the bow therefore means dissolving or disabling the kingdom's cohesion.

> *“I do set my bow in the cloud, and it shall be for a token of a covenant between me and the earth.”* — Genesis 9:13

> *“And the bow shall be in the cloud; and I will look upon it, that I may remember the everlasting covenant between God and every living creature of all flesh that is upon the earth.”* — Genesis 9:16

> *“Thus saith the LORD of hosts; Behold, I will break the bow of Elam, the chief of their might.”* — Jeremiah 49:35

> *“All thy rulers are fled together, they are bound by the archers: all that are found in thee are bound together, which have fled from far.”* — Isaiah 22:3

> *“And it shall come to pass at that day, that I will break the bow of Israel in the valley of Jezreel.”* — Hosea 1:5

> *“And in that day will I make a covenant for them with the beasts of the field, and with the fowls of heaven, and with the creeping things of the ground: and I will break the bow and the sword and the battle out of the earth, and will make them to lie down safely.”* — Hosea 2:18

> *“He maketh wars to cease unto the end of the earth; he breaketh the bow, and cutteth the spear in sunder; he burneth the chariot in the fire.”* — Psalms 46:9

> *“But turned back, and dealt unfaithfully like their fathers: they were turned aside like a deceitful bow.”* — Psalms 78:57

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Competing Definitions Tested

- The entry may conflate distinct uses merely because Hebrew qesheth denotes both a weapon and a rainbow. Genesis directly supports a covenant-token, and Jeremiah and Hosea support military might being broken, but the printed texts do not by themselves establish that military might is the outward face of a kingdom's covenant or that breaking a bow generally 'unstrings' the kingdom. Isaiah 22:3's English wording about being 'bound' by archers especially does not independently prove that synthesis.

- One could argue this is merely a MATCH with Response A, which already records the tradition linking war-bow and covenant-rainbow (the unstrung bow) and lists both senses; on that view the book's 'unification' is an interpretive gloss, not a changed identification. Against this, the consensus link runs one direction (rainbow explained via the war-bow), whereas the book makes covenant the governing sense of weapon texts too — e.g., reading Hosea 1:5 and Jeremiah 49:35 as covenant-unstringing — which the consensus nowhere does. The citation support is only partial: Hosea 2:18 (covenant plus breaking the bow) and Psalm 78:57 (unfaithfulness as a deceitful bow) are suggestive, but the printed verses never state that might is the covenant's outward face; that identity is inferential.

- The synthesis relies on interpreting the shared Hebrew word (qesheth) for weapon and rainbow as evidence of a shared underlying symbolism (covenant). Interpreters may argue that this is a lexical overlap based on visual similarity, and that prophetic texts about breaking a nation's bow (like Elam or Israel) refer strictly to military defeat rather than the 'unstringing' of a national covenant.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
