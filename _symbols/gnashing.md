# Gnashing of teeth

## Approved Definition

**The rage of the wicked at the righteous, especially when they see the kingdom from which they are excluded. Their grief, hatred, gnashing, and destruction follow the pattern of the Psalms and Stephen's accusers.**

## The Short Case

The book identifies gnashing of teeth as the rage of the wicked against the righteous—the mixed sorrow and hatred of the excluded at the sight of the kingdom they forfeited—followed by their melting away, templated from the psalms and staged at Stephen's trial.

> *“The wicked shall see it, and be grieved; he shall gnash with his teeth, and melt away: the desire of the wicked shall perish.”* — Psalms 112:10

> *“The wicked plotteth against the just, and gnasheth upon him with his teeth.”* — Psalms 37:12

> *“There shall be weeping and gnashing of teeth, when ye shall see Abraham, and Isaac, and Jacob, and all the prophets, in the kingdom of God, and you yourselves thrust out.”* — Luke 13:28

> *“When they heard these things, they were cut to the heart, and they gnashed on him with their teeth.”* — Acts 7:54

> *“But the children of the kingdom shall be cast out into outer darkness: there shall be weeping and gnashing of teeth.”* — Matthew 8:12

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Competing Definitions Tested

A common reading is: **the anguish of the damned under conscious, everlasting torment.**

- Psalms 37 and Acts 7 clearly depict rage against a righteous person, but Luke 13 and Matthew 8 do not explicitly say that the excluded direct their gnashing at the righteous. The transfer of that hostile sense into every Gospel judgment occurrence—and especially the inference from Psalm 112 that the Gospel subjects ultimately perish rather than remain under punishment—is interpretive rather than directly established by the printed citations.

- One could argue DIVERGENT: the consensus's primary meaning is anguish of the damned under judgment (traditionally everlasting), and the book explicitly displaces that with rage-then-annihilation, denying the common teaching it quotes. If 'anguish under conscious everlasting torment' is taken as the consensus core rather than merely its traditional coloring, the book's 'then they melt away' is a direct contradiction, not a refinement.

- The consensus primary meaning of pure eschatological anguish under conscious torment is so dominant in Christian teaching that elevating the rage-and-perish reading may be viewed as a minority re-emphasis rather than a refinement of the same core.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
