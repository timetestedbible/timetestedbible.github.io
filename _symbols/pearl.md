## The Short Case

Pearl signifies “the one complete light,” identified with the full moon as governor of appointed times, and links wisdom, the bride, and the single-pearl gates of the heavenly city as the merchant’s royal entry.

> *“She is more precious than rubies: and all the things thou canst desire are not to be compared unto her.”* — Proverbs 3:15

> *“Who can find a virtuous woman? for her price is far above rubies.”* — Proverbs 31:10

> *“Blow up the trumpet in the new moon, in the time appointed, on our solemn feast day.”* — Psalms 81:3

> *“He hath taken a bag of money with him, and will come home at the day appointed.”* — Proverbs 7:20

> *“He holdeth back the face of his throne, and spreadeth his cloud upon it.”* — Job 26:9

> *“He appointed the moon for seasons: the sun knoweth his going down.”* — Psalms 104:19

> *“And the twelve gates were twelve pearls; every several gate was of one pearl: and the street of the city was pure gold, as it were transparent glass.”* — Revelation 21:21

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Competing Definitions Tested

A common reading is: **the kingdom worth everything, or the Church purchased by Christ.**

- The printed texts separately mention rubies, the moon and appointed seasons, a throne, and one-pearl gates, but they do not themselves establish that pearl means the full moon, that the cited Hebrew terms encode the proposed links, or that the gates constitute the merchant’s entry at the King’s gate.

- One could argue NOVEL rather than DIVERGENT: the book's lunar reading may be intended as complementary symbolism (the moon governing appointed times as an image of entry into the kingdom at the King's gate), not a denial that the parable's pearl means the kingdom; the identification is absent from consensus more than it strictly contradicts it. Additionally, the printed verses do support wisdom-above-pearls (given the peninim gloss) and the one-pearl gates, so the citation judgment could arguably be stronger than partial for that portion.

- The printed citations omit the dominant NT bases (Matt 13:45-46, 7:6) that ground every consensus reading, relying instead on an unparalleled synthesis of moon, appointed-time, and valuation texts that standard symbolism does not treat as defining the pearl.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
