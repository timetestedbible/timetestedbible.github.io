# Remnant

## Summary

**The faithful few preserved and gathered from what remains — like gleanings left at the [field](/research/symbols/field/)’s edge and gold separated from dross.**

The fuller book and notebook trail is available in [The Remnant](/books/symbolic-language/the-remnant/).

## Definitions

### Symbolic Definition

### Literal Biblical Definition

### Webster's Definition

Webster's 1913 entry for **remnant** begins: “Remaining; yet left. [R.] ”Because of the remnant dregs of his disease.” Fuller.”

These definitions may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

The remnant is the faithful few situated at society’s gleaning edge—especially the widow, fatherless, and stranger—deliberately kept small, purified through trial, and allegedly governed by a fixed biblical ratio.

> *“Yet I have left me seven thousand in Israel, all the knees which have not bowed unto Baal, and every mouth which hath not kissed him.”* — 1 Kings 19:18

> *“And it shall come to pass, that in all the land, saith the LORD, two parts therein shall be cut off and die; but the third shall be left therein.”* — Zechariah 13:8

> *“And I will bring the third part through the fire, and will refine them as silver is refined, and will try them as gold is tried: they shall call on my name, and I will hear them: I will say, It is my people: and they shall say, The LORD is my God.”* — Zechariah 13:9

> *“Even so then at this present time also there is a remnant according to the election of grace.”* — Romans 11:5

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Dictionary Comparison

Webster's 1913 entry for **remnant** begins:

> “Rem”nant (rm”nant), a. Etym: [OF. remanant, p. pr. of remanoir, remaindre. See Remanent, Remain.] Defn: Remaining; yet left. [R.] ”Because of the remnant dregs of his disease.” Fuller. And quiet dedicate her remnant life To the just duties of an humble wife. Prior.”

The dictionary supplies the ordinary sense. The definition above is narrower or figurative only where Scripture's own cross-references require that transfer.

## Evidence by Sense

The entry currently states one controlling sense:

1. **Core sense:** The faithful few preserved and gathered from what remains — like gleanings left at the [field](/research/symbols/field/)’s edge and gold separated from dross.
2. **Defining witnesses:** Ruth 2; 1 Kgs 19:18; Zech 13:8-9; Rom 11:5
3. **Boundary:** the sense applies where the passage activates the same relationship; ordinary literal uses remain literal.

## Competing Definitions Tested

- The printed references support faithful preservation, refinement, and gleaning separately, but they do not demonstrate that remnant status is defined by gleaning-edge social categories or that a fixed ratio runs from gleaning-law geometry to Elijah’s seven thousand.

- One could argue for MATCH: the entry's operative identification ('the faithful few... refined like gold') is exactly the consensus core, and the Ruth/gleaning material is illustrative imagery rather than a genuine boundary claim, with the 'fixed ratio' being rhetorical flourish deferred to chapter 15. Alternatively, one could argue the 'fixed ratio traceable from the gleaning law's geometry' is so foreign to any consensus or alternative that the entry verges on NOVEL. The REFINED verdict sits between these because the core is preserved while the ratio and gleaner-profile claims materially alter conditions the consensus leaves open.

- No consensus treatment links remnant to Ruth, gleaning statutes, or legal geometry; those elements re-center the motif on agricultural law in a way foreign to the prophetic-Pauline core.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
