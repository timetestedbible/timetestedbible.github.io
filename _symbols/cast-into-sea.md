# Cast into the Sea

## Approved Definition

**A ruling power irreversibly destroyed and sunk into the [sea](/research/symbols/sea/) of peoples, never to rise; especially the fall of [Babylon the Great](/research/symbols/babel/).**
> *"And a mighty angel took up a stone like a great **millstone**, and **cast it into the sea**, saying, Thus with violence shall that great city Babylon be thrown down, and shall be found no more at all."* — Rev 18:21

---

## Definition Layers

### Bible symbolic sense

**A ruling power irreversibly destroyed and sunk into the [sea](/research/symbols/sea/) of peoples, never to rise; especially the fall of [Babylon the Great](/research/symbols/babel/).**

### Bible literal sense

*Not yet stated separately. This is a research gap: the literal sense must be derived from ordinary biblical uses, not copied from Webster.*

### Webster's English sense

Webster's 1913 entry for **cast** begins: “To send or drive by force; to throw; to fling; to hurl; to impel.”

These layers may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Key Insight

This is a compound symbol. To read it, you have to know what the pieces mean separately — and then watch what happens when they combine.

A $rock or $mountain in Scripture represents a covenant or a kingdom — an established authority, something that *stands*. The $sea represents nations — chaotic, restless peoples (Rev 17:15 makes this explicit: "The waters which thou sawest… are peoples, and multitudes, and nations, and tongues").

When a stone is cast into the sea, a ruling authority is thrown into the mass of nations. It does not float. It does not wash ashore. It **sinks**. It is absorbed, overwhelmed, gone.

The action is always downward, always irreversible, and always final. Every time Scripture uses this image — from Exodus to Revelation — the pattern holds: a power that set itself against God's covenant is terminated by being swallowed into the chaos of peoples. What stood above the waters goes under them and does not come back up.

The opposite image confirms the meaning. In Daniel 2:35, a stone *becomes* a great $mountain that fills the whole earth — a kingdom *rising* from smallness to dominion. Cast into the sea is the reverse trajectory: a kingdom descending from dominion into oblivion.

**Symbolizes:** The irreversible destruction of a ruling power — a kingdom or authority absorbed into the chaos of nations, never to be restored

**Opposite:** A $mountain rising from the $sea — a kingdom established from among the nations (Dan 2:35, Rev 13:1)

**Defining verses:** Rev 18:21, Jer 51:63–64, Exod 15:5, Rev 8:8

**The surprise:** When Jesus says "this mountain shall be removed and cast into the sea" (Mat 21:21), He is standing on the Mount of Olives, facing the temple mount. He is not giving a lesson about prayer technique. He is pronouncing a kingdom-judgment: the ruling authority of Jerusalem will be thrown into the nations — which is exactly what happened in AD 70.

**Connected:** $mountain, $rock, $sea, $babylon, $fire, $earthquake

## Cast into the Sea Across Scripture

### Pharaoh's Army Sank as a Stone

The first occurrence of the pattern sets the template. After the Red Sea crossing, Moses and Israel sing:

> *"Pharaoh's chariots and his host hath he **cast into the sea**: his chosen captains also are drowned in the Red sea."* — Exod 15:4

> *"The depths have covered them: they **sank** into the bottom as a **stone**."* — Exod 15:5

> *"Thou didst blow with thy wind, the **sea** covered them: they **sank** as lead in the mighty waters."* — Exod 15:10

The ruling power of Egypt — its military apparatus, its chosen captains, its chariots — is cast into the waters and sinks *like a stone*. Not like driftwood. Not like something that might resurface. Like lead. Like rock. Gone.

Nehemiah later recalls this event in the same language:

> *"And their persecutors thou threwest into the deeps, **as a stone into the mighty waters**."* — Neh 9:11

The image was preserved across generations as the defining picture of what God does to a kingdom that opposes His covenant people: He throws it into the deep, and it goes down like a stone.

This is the Exodus prototype. Every later "cast into the sea" passage echoes it.

### Babylon Sinks and Shall Not Rise

Jeremiah's prophecy against historical Babylon closes with a dramatic symbolic act:

> *"And it shall be, when thou hast made an end of reading this book, that thou shalt bind a **stone** to it, and **cast it into the midst of Euphrates**: And thou shalt say, Thus shall **Babylon sink**, and **shall not rise** from the evil that I will bring upon her."* — Jer 51:63–64

The prophet ties a stone to the scroll of judgment and hurls it into the river. The stone carries the prophetic word — the weight of God's decree — and as it sinks, so shall Babylon. The key phrase is "shall not rise." The sinking is permanent. The destruction is irreversible.

Notice the elements: a stone (authority/weight), cast into waters (nations), and a kingdom that will never recover. This is Exodus repeated at a new scale — not Pharaoh's army but the entire Babylonian system.

Revelation 18:21 quotes this passage almost verbatim. The angel does not merely allude to Jeremiah — he *re-enacts* the prophetic gesture. The stone becomes a millstone, the Euphrates becomes the sea, and historical Babylon becomes "Babylon the Great." The pattern scales upward, but the meaning is unchanged: cast into the sea = a ruling power destroyed and never restored.

### The Burning Mountain — Revelation's Second Trumpet

> *"And the second angel sounded, and as it were a great **mountain burning with fire** was **cast into the sea**: and the third part of the sea became blood."* — Rev 8:8

Here all three components appear together: $mountain (kingdom), $fire (God's judgment), $sea (nations). A kingdom that is already burning with divine judgment is hurled into the mass of peoples — and the result is blood. The nations themselves are damaged by the impact.

This image combines two prophetic streams. Jeremiah calls Babylon a "destroying $mountain, which destroyest all the earth" and declares God will make it "a burnt mountain" (Jer 51:25). The burning mountain of Rev 8:8 evokes that oracle — a kingdom already under judgment, now thrown down into the peoples it once ruled. The destruction radiates outward: a third of the sea becomes blood, a third of the sea-creatures die, a third of the ships are destroyed.

The scale suggests this is not merely a single city falling but a systemic collapse — an entire governing structure crashing into the nations it dominated, producing catastrophic consequences for the peoples underneath.

### "This Mountain" — Jesus and the Temple

> *"Jesus answered and said unto them, Verily I say unto you, If ye have faith, and doubt not, ye shall not only do what is done to the fig tree, but also if ye shall say unto **this mountain**, Be thou removed, and be thou **cast into the sea**; it shall be done."* — Mat 21:21

> *"For verily I say unto you, That whosoever shall say unto **this mountain**, Be thou removed, and be thou **cast into the sea**; and shall not doubt in his heart, but shall believe that those things which he saith shall come to pass; he shall have it."* — Mk 11:23

These words are spoken on the Mount of Olives, overlooking the temple mount. Jesus has just cursed the fig tree (the fruitless symbol of Israel's religious establishment) and cleansed the temple (the seat of Jewish authority). He is asked about the withered fig tree. His response invokes the cast-into-the-sea pattern.

"This mountain" — the demonstrative pronoun points at what is in view: the temple mount, Jerusalem's ruling authority, the seat of Israel's religious and political power. "Be thou removed, and cast into the sea" — be terminated and swallowed up among the nations.

Reading this as a generic lesson about prayer and confidence misses the prophetic weight. Jesus is using the exact symbolic vocabulary of Exodus 15 and Jeremiah 51 — kingdom thrown into nations — and applying it to Jerusalem's authority. Within a generation, the temple was destroyed and the Jewish people were scattered among the nations. The mountain was cast into the sea.

Faith enters the equation because it takes faith to pronounce God's covenant judgment against what *looks* permanent. The temple looked eternal. The religious establishment looked immovable. Faith sees what God has decreed and speaks accordingly.

### The Millstone Around the Neck

> *"But whoso shall offend one of these little ones which believe in me, it were better for him that a **millstone** were hanged about his neck, and that he were drowned in the depth of the **sea**."* — Mat 18:6

> *"It were better for him that a **millstone** were hanged about his neck, and he **cast into the sea**, than that he should offend one of these little ones."* — Lk 17:2

The millstone saying applies the same pattern to an individual. The one who causes a "little one" to stumble is treated like Babylon: weighted with the instrument of his own authority and sunk into the depths. The millstone — the mechanism that grinds grain, that produces bread — becomes the instrument of drowning. The very thing that represented productive power becomes the weight that drags under.

This is not merely a graphic way of saying "it would be very bad." The imagery places the offender into the Babylon-judgment category: weighed down with the tools of authority and thrown irreversibly into the mass of peoples, never to rise. What was done to Pharaoh and to Babylon will be done to anyone who uses their position to destroy faith in the vulnerable.

### Mountains Carried into the Midst of the Sea

> *"Therefore will not we fear, though the earth be removed, and though the **mountains** be carried into the midst of the **sea**."* — Ps 46:2

Psalm 46 takes the cast-into-the-sea image and draws faith from it. If $mountain = kingdom, then "mountains carried into the midst of the sea" = ruling powers overthrown and swallowed among the nations. This is the *worst-case scenario* for anyone living under a government: the total collapse of authority into chaos.

And the psalmist says: we will not fear. Why? Because "God is our refuge and strength" (Ps 46:1). The covenant stands even when kingdoms fall. The $rock beneath the $mountain does not move. The mountains may be cast into the sea — every human authority may collapse — but the God of Jacob is still the refuge.

This is the positive application of the same pattern. For Pharaoh and Babylon, cast into the sea is judgment. For the covenant-keeper, watching kingdoms fall into the sea is not terror — it is confirmation that God shakes what can be shaken so that what cannot be shaken remains (Heb 12:27).

## The Sinking That Is Never Reversed

One detail unifies every occurrence: *nothing that is cast into the sea comes back up*.

Pharaoh's army "sank as lead" (Exod 15:10). Babylon "shall not rise" (Jer 51:64). Babylon the Great "shall be found no more at all" (Rev 18:21). The burning mountain produces blood but does not resurface. The millstone-weighted offender is "drowned in the depth" (Mat 18:6).

This irreversibility distinguishes cast-into-the-sea from other judgment images. $fire refines what is genuine while consuming what is false — some survive the fire (Dan 3, 1 Cor 3:15). $earthquake shakes to reveal what can and cannot be moved — some things remain (Heb 12:27). But the sea swallows completely. Nothing rises from the deep.

The opposite trajectory confirms this. Daniel's stone becomes a $mountain that fills the earth — rising, expanding, establishing (Dan 2:35). Beasts *rise from* the sea — kingdoms emerge out of the chaos of nations (Dan 7:3, Rev 13:1). The upward movement is establishment. The downward movement — cast *into* the sea — is termination. There is no passage where something thrown into the sea returns.

## Patterns

1. **Every era has its casting.** Pharaoh's army into the Red Sea (Exodus cycle). Babylon into the Euphrates (Exile cycle). "This mountain" into the sea (Roman cycle). The burning mountain and millstone into the sea (Consummation). The image scales across history, but the structure is identical: ruling power opposed to God → thrown into nations → gone.

2. **The object sinks — it does not float.** Stone, lead, millstone. Scripture chooses the heaviest images. The emphasis is on the impossibility of return. This is not exile (which implies potential restoration). It is absorption and termination.

3. **The weight carries significance.** In Jer 51:63–64, the stone bears the prophetic word — God's decree is the weight that sinks Babylon. In Mat 18:6, the millstone — the tool of one's own productive authority — becomes the drowning weight. What you wielded becomes what drags you down.

4. **The sea is damaged by the impact.** Rev 8:8 — the sea becomes blood. The falling kingdom does not simply disappear; its collapse harms the nations it crashes into. This is the collateral devastation of systemic judgment.

5. **The compound symbol requires all three elements.** A stone alone = $rock / covenant. A mountain alone = kingdom / authority. The sea alone = nations / peoples. Only when the stone or mountain is *thrown into* the sea does the full pattern appear: a ruling authority irreversibly destroyed by absorption into the chaos of nations.

6. **Psalm 46 turns judgment into comfort.** The same image that terrifies kingdoms reassures the faithful. If God is your refuge, the sight of mountains falling into the sea is not catastrophe — it is the removal of what stood between you and God's direct rule.

## Relationship to Other Symbols
- **$mountain** — The most frequent object cast into the sea. $mountain = kingdom / ruling authority. When a mountain is cast into the sea, a kingdom is thrown into the chaos of nations. Rev 8:8 (burning mountain), Mat 21:21 (this mountain), Ps 46:2 (mountains carried into the sea). The mountain that *fills* the earth (Dan 2:35) is the opposite trajectory.

- **$rock** — The stone that sinks. Exod 15:5 ("sank as a stone"), Neh 9:11 ("as a stone into mighty waters"), Jer 51:63 (stone cast into Euphrates), Rev 18:21 (millstone). The $rock normally represents the unchanging covenant — but here, a *false* authority is given the weight of stone and thrown down. The genuine $rock cannot be moved (Ps 62:6); the counterfeit stone sinks.

- **$sea** — The destination. $sea = nations / chaotic peoples (Rev 17:15, Isa 17:12). The sea does not merely receive the stone — it swallows it. The kingdom is not relocated; it is dissolved among the peoples. Rev 21:1 ("no more sea") suggests the ultimate resolution: when there are no more chaotic, rebellious nations, there is nothing left to swallow kingdoms into.

- **$babylon** — The primary subject. Jer 51:63–64 and Rev 18:21 explicitly link this image to Babylon's fall. The cast-into-the-sea pattern *is* the mechanism of $babylon's destruction: the world system thrown into the mass of peoples it once dominated, never to reconstitute itself.

- **$fire** — Combined in Rev 8:8: a mountain *burning with fire* cast into the sea. The kingdom is already under God's judgment ($fire = covenant standard in active contact) before it is thrown into the nations. The burning precedes the sinking — judgment ignites within before the final collapse.

- **$earthquake** — Co-occurring in the broader judgment sequences. When mountains are moved (Ps 46:2) and kingdoms collapse, $earthquake (the shaking of established order) is the mechanism. Heb 12:26–27 ties the shaking to the removal of what can be shaken — cast into the sea is the final destination of what the $earthquake dislodges.

This study checks the glossary's defining citations first, then compares them with every KJV verse that prints *cast into the sea*. The English-form sweep found **7 verses**. ## Competing Definitions Tested

- Jeremiah 51:63–64 and Revelation 18:21 strongly support Babylon’s irreversible fall, and Exodus 15:4–5 supports enemies sinking like stone. However, the printed texts alone do not establish that the sea universally symbolizes peoples, nor that every stone, mountain, or millstone cast into the sea refers specifically to Babylon or a ruling power.

- One could argue MATCH: the book's 'fall of Babylon' reading is exactly the dominant sense the consensus assigns to the key texts (Rev 18:21; Jer 51:63-64), and the consensus notes already acknowledge that judgment dominates for the stone/millstone passages, making the book's focus mere emphasis rather than material narrowing. Against this, the book's exclusive restriction to ruling powers and its distinctive 'sea of peoples' identification (the sea as nations, not mere oblivion) go beyond the consensus primary meaning and reclassify part of its extension, which fits REFINED better.

- By restricting this phrase-symbol entirely to 'the fall of Babylon / a ruling power,' the book neglects identical phrasing in the gospels regarding individuals (the millstone warning) and faith (casting mountains into the sea), which use the same imagery for different applications.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.

## Conclusion

**A ruling power irreversibly destroyed and sunk into the [sea](/research/symbols/sea/) of peoples, never to rise; especially the fall of [Babylon the Great](/research/symbols/babel/).**

This is the approved glossary conclusion; broader applications in the study remain subordinate to the defining texts and countertexts.
