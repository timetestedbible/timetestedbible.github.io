# Beast, wild animal

## Approved Definition

**A nation or kingdom organized under earthly rule, often shown in its wild or predatory character. Different beasts distinguish different kingdoms; clean and unclean beasts distinguish kinds of people.**

The fuller book and notebook trail is available in [Trees](/books/symbolic-language/trees/).

## Definition Layers

### Bible symbolic sense

**A nation or kingdom organized under earthly rule, often shown in its wild or predatory character. Different beasts distinguish different kingdoms; clean and unclean beasts distinguish kinds of people.**

### Bible literal sense

*Not yet stated separately. This is a research gap: the literal sense must be derived from ordinary biblical uses, not copied from Webster.*

### Webster's English sense

Webster's 1913 entry for **beast** begins: “Any living creature; an animal; -- including man, insects, etc. [Obs.] Chaucer.”

These layers may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

A beast symbolizes a nation or kingdom of the world, serving as the wild, predatory figure of a people, with unclean beasts specifically representing Gentile nations.

> *“These great beasts, which are four, are four kings, which shall arise out of the earth.”* — Daniel 7:17

> *“The ram which thou sawest having two horns are the kings of Media and Persia.”* — Daniel 8:20

> *“And the rough goat is the king of Grecia: and the great horn that is between his eyes is the first king.”* — Daniel 8:21

> *“Wherein were all manner of fourfooted beasts of the earth, and wild beasts, and creeping things, and fowls of the air.”* — Acts 10:12

> *“And he said unto them, Ye know how that it is an unlawful thing for a man that is a Jew to keep company, or come unto one of another nation; but God hath shewed me that I should not call any man common or unclean.”* — Acts 10:28

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Dictionary Comparison

Webster's 1913 entry for **beast** begins:

> “Beast, n. Etym: [OE. best, beste, OF. beste, F. bête, fr. L. bestia.] 1. Any living creature; an animal; -- including man, insects, etc. [Obs.] Chaucer.”

The dictionary supplies the ordinary sense. The approved definition above is narrower or figurative only where Scripture's own cross-references require that transfer.

## Competing Definitions Tested

- Daniel 7:17 and Daniel 8:20–21 directly identify the animals as kings or political powers, but they do not establish that every symbolic beast means any nation generally. Acts 10 supports applying unclean-animal imagery to Gentile persons, yet does not explicitly call them nations or establish the book’s universal beast symbolism, predatory characterization, or contrast with trees.

- One could argue MATCH: 'nation or kingdom of the world' is essentially the same identification as the consensus's 'kingdoms/empires,' and the Acts 10 Gentile-nations point already appears as a listed minority alternative in Response C, so nothing in the book is outside the consensus map. On this view the omission of 'God-opposing' is mere brevity of a glossary entry rather than a substantive boundary claim.

- The apocalyptic beasts in Revelation and Daniel are depicted specifically as demonic or fiercely anti-God powers, not merely as general representations of any worldly nation or the Gentiles.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
