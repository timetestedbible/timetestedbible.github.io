### Webster's Definition

Webster's 1913 entry for **dog** begins: “(Zoöl.) Defn: A quadruped of the genus Canis, esp. the domestic dog (C. familiaris).”

These definitions may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

Dogs symbolize the Gentile power—an unclean entity outside the covenant table that eventually crucified Christ and remains outside at the end.

> *“For dogs have compassed me: the assembly of the wicked have inclosed me: they pierced my hands and my feet.”* — Psalms 22:16

> *“But he answered and said, It is not meet to take the children's bread, and to cast it to dogs.”* — Matthew 15:26

> *“And she said, Truth, Lord: yet the dogs eat of the crumbs which fall from their masters' table.”* — Matthew 15:27

> *“Then the soldiers of the governor took Jesus into the common hall, and gathered unto him the whole band of soldiers.”* — Matthew 27:27

> *“And they stripped him, and put on him a scarlet robe.”* — Matthew 27:28

> *“And when they had platted a crown of thorns, they put it upon his head, and a reed in his right hand: and they bowed the knee before him, and mocked him, saying, Hail, King of the Jews!”* — Matthew 27:29

> *“And they spit upon him, and took the reed, and smote him on the head.”* — Matthew 27:30

> *“And after that they had mocked him, they took the robe off from him, and put his own raiment on him, and led him away to crucify him.”* — Matthew 27:31

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Evidence by Sense

The entry currently states one controlling sense:

1. **Core sense:** A wild [beast](/research/symbols/beast/) representing a kingdom or its citizens, especially Rome in the crucifixion pattern. Gentile dogs eat crumbs from Israel's table; Roman soldiers surrounded Jesus at the cross.
2. **Defining witnesses:** Ps 22:16; Matt 15:26-27; 27:27-35; Mark 7:28; Rev 22:15
3. **Boundary:** the sense applies where the passage activates the same relationship; ordinary literal uses remain literal.

## Competing Definitions Tested

- The Gospel citations clearly involve a Gentile woman and covenant-table imagery, but Psalm 22:16 identifies the dogs in parallel with an assembly of the wicked, while Revelation 22:15 lists morally condemned people outside the city. Neither text alone establishes that the dogs specifically represent Gentile power, much less a single historical progression from crumbs to the cross to final exclusion.

- One could argue for DIVERGENT: the consensus explicitly denies any single fixed referent ('no settled rule identifying dogs with one specific group in every text'), while the book asserts exactly such a rule, and applying 'Gentile power' to Ps 22:16 and Rev 22:15 arguably replaces the consensus identification of those passages (violent enemies; the wicked generally) rather than merely narrowing it. Additionally, the book's redemptive note that the dog is fed 'until brought in' introduces a positive trajectory the consensus classes as minority at best.

- In Revelation 22:15, 'dogs' are listed alongside 'sorcerers, and whoremongers, and murderers, and idolaters,' which heavily implies a category of moral wickedness or false teaching rather than an ethnic or political designation like 'the Gentile power.'



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
