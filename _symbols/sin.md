# Sin

## Summary

**The transgression of the law — Scripture's own definition: “sin is the transgression of the law”; where no law is, there is no transgression, and by the law is the knowledge of sin.**

The fuller book and notebook trail is available in [Grace, Mercy & the Gift](/books/symbolic-language/path-to-salvation/).

## Definitions

### Symbolic Definition

**The transgression of the law — Scripture's own definition: “sin is the transgression of the law”; where no law is, there is no transgression, and by the law is the knowledge of sin.**

### Literal Biblical Definition

*Not yet stated separately. This is a research gap: the literal sense must be derived from ordinary biblical uses, not copied from Webster.*

### Webster's Definition

Webster's 1913 entry for **sin** begins: “Sin, adv., prep., & conj.”

These definitions may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

Sin is transgression of the law; law identifies sin, and without law there is no transgression.

> *“Whosoever committeth sin transgresseth also the law: for sin is the transgression of the law.”* — 1 John 3:4

> *“Because the law worketh wrath: for where no law is, there is no transgression.”* — Romans 4:15

> *“Therefore by the deeds of the law there shall no flesh be justified in his sight: for by the law is the knowledge of sin.”* — Romans 3:20

> *“What shall we say then? Is the law sin? God forbid. Nay, I had not known sin, but by the law: for I had not known lust, except the law had said, Thou shalt not covet.”* — Romans 7:7

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Dictionary Comparison

Webster's 1913 entry for **sin** begins:

> “Sin, adv., prep., & conj. Defn: Old form of Since. [Obs. or Prov.Eng. & Scot.] Sin that his lord was twenty year of age. Chaucer.”

The dictionary supplies the ordinary sense. The definition above is narrower or figurative only where Scripture's own cross-references require that transfer.

## Corpus and Method

This study checks the glossary's defining citations first, then compares them with every KJV verse that prints *sin*. The English-form sweep found **535 verses**. It is exhaustive for those printed forms, but it is not a substitute for a Strong's-number sweep where several Hebrew or Greek words share one English translation.

## Evidence by Sense

The entry currently states one controlling sense:

1. **Core sense:** The transgression of the law — Scripture's own definition: “sin is the transgression of the law”; where no law is, there is no transgression, and by the law is the knowledge of sin.
2. **Defining witnesses:** 1 John 3:4; Rom 4:15; 3:20; 7:7
3. **Boundary:** the sense applies where the passage activates the same relationship; ordinary literal uses remain literal.

## Competing Definitions Tested

- The book may imply that law-transgression is an exhaustive definition, potentially excluding broader biblical presentations of sin as unbelief, omission, bondage, or a reigning power. However, the entry does not clearly reclassify those cases, and its central identification remains the consensus's dominant legal formulation.

- One could argue MATCH: the book merely quotes 1 John 3:4, which all four consensus responses cite in their primary basis, so the core identifications are equivalent and the omission of other metaphors is immaterial glossary brevity. However, the book's added citations (Rom 4:15 especially) and its framing as 'Scripture's own definition' signal an exclusive, law-dependent definition, which is a real precision claim beyond the consensus's inclusive treatment — hence REFINED rather than MATCH. Alternatively, one could argue the KJV's 'transgression of the law' mistranslates anomia (lawlessness as a disposition), weakening the book's citation support, but as printed the KJV verses do strongly support the entry's stated claim.

- Book omits the multifaceted metaphors and inherited-condition language that consensus regards as integral to a full biblical portrayal, potentially appearing incomplete even while correctly stating the core definition.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
