# Stranger

## Summary

**A non-citizen of God's commonwealth — one outside the [covenant](/research/symbols/covenant/), drawn in, or, once in, made a stranger to the world.**

The fuller book and notebook trail is available in [Orphans, Widows & the Fatherless](/books/symbolic-language/orphans-widows-and-the-fatherless/).

## Definitions

### Symbolic Definition

**A non-citizen of God's commonwealth — one outside the [covenant](/research/symbols/covenant/), drawn in, or, once in, made a stranger to the world.**

### Literal Biblical Definition

*Not yet stated separately. This is a research gap: the literal sense must be derived from ordinary biblical uses, not copied from Webster.*

### Webster's Definition

Webster's 1913 entry for **stranger** begins: “One who is strange, foreign, or unknown.”

These definitions may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

A non-citizen of God's commonwealth—one outside the covenant, drawn in, or, once in, made a stranger to the world.

> *“That at that time ye were without Christ, being aliens from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers from the covenants of promise, having no hope, and without God in the world:”* — Ephesians 2:12

> *“Now therefore ye are no more strangers and foreigners, but fellowcitizens with the saints, and of the household of God;”* — Ephesians 2:19

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Dictionary Comparison

Webster's 1913 entry for **stranger** begins:

> “Stran”ger, n. Etym: [OF. estrangier, F. étranger. See Strange.] 1. One who is strange, foreign, or unknown. Specifically: -- (a) One who comes from a foreign land; a foreigner. I am a most poor woman and a stranger, Born out of your dominions. Shak. (b) One whose home is at a distance from the place where he is, but in the same country. (c) One who is unknown or unacquainted; as, the gentleman is a stranger to me; hence, one not admitted to communication, fellowship, or acquaintance. Melons on beds of ice are taught to bear, And strangers to the sun yet ripen here. Granville. My child is yet a stranger in the world. Shak. I was no stranger to the original. Dryden.”

The dictionary supplies the ordinary sense. The definition above is narrower or figurative only where Scripture's own cross-references require that transfer.

## Corpus and Method

This study checks the glossary's defining citations first, then compares them with every KJV verse that prints *stranger*. The English-form sweep found **198 verses**. It is exhaustive for those printed forms, but it is not a substitute for a Strong's-number sweep where several Hebrew or Greek words share one English translation.

## Evidence by Sense

The entry currently states one controlling sense:

1. **Core sense:** A non-citizen of God's commonwealth — one outside the [covenant](/research/symbols/covenant/), drawn in, or, once in, made a stranger to the world.
2. **Defining witnesses:** Eph 2:12, 19
3. **Boundary:** the sense applies where the passage activates the same relationship; ordinary literal uses remain literal.

## Competing Definitions Tested

- A MATCH classification is plausible because the consensus explicitly includes both Ephesians 2 covenant alienation and believers’ pilgrim status. However, the book makes these citizenship relations exhaustive while omitting the prominent literal-hospitality application, creating a real narrowing of extension. Moreover, the printed Ephesians citations strongly support covenant exclusion and incorporation but do not by themselves establish the second clause about becoming a stranger to the world.

- One could argue MATCH: every element of the book's definition (covenant alienation and believer-as-stranger-to-the-world) appears in the consensus responses, and Response C even makes the Eph 2 reading primary, so the difference could be seen as mere emphasis rather than a material boundary change. Additionally, the printed citations fully support the covenant-alienation half, arguably making support STRONG for the entry's main claim.

- Printed citations (Eph 2:12, 19) support only the outside-the-covenant and 'drawn in' half; they reverse stranger status rather than establish the believer as 'stranger to the world,' and the entry omits the widely primary hospitality reading.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.

## Relationship to Other Symbols

The glossary names **Knowing.** as the reciprocal opposite. That relation must work in both directions; a merely related image is not an opposite.
