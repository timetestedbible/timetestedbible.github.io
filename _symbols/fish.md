# Fish

## Approved Definition

**A person in the [sea](/research/symbols/sea/) of nations, clean or unclean, gathered and sorted at the [harvest](/research/symbols/harvest/). Fishers of men draw the catch from the sea in the [net](/research/symbols/net/).**
> *"And makest men as the **fishes** of the $[sea], as the creeping things, that have no ruler over them?"* — Hab 1:14

---

## Definition Layers

### Bible symbolic sense

**A person in the [sea](/research/symbols/sea/) of nations, clean or unclean, gathered and sorted at the [harvest](/research/symbols/harvest/). Fishers of men draw the catch from the sea in the [net](/research/symbols/net/).**

### Bible literal sense

*Not yet stated separately. This is a research gap: the literal sense must be derived from ordinary biblical uses, not copied from Webster.*

### Webster's English sense

Webster's 1913 entry for **fish** begins: “A counter, used in various games.”

These layers may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Key Insight

God Himself makes the equation. In Habakkuk 1:14, He does not compare men to fish — He says He *makes* men as fish. And the defining quality is not that they swim, not that they are small, not that they multiply. It is that they have **no ruler over them**.

Fish in the $sea are leaderless. They swarm without a shepherd. They are exposed to every net, every hook, every predator. This is the condition of humanity scattered among the nations — people without covenant governance, vulnerable to whatever empire drags its $net through the waters.

When Jesus tells Simon and Andrew, "Follow me, and I will make you fishers of men" (Mat 4:19), He is not inventing a metaphor. He is invoking an equation that runs from Genesis to Ezekiel to Habakkuk: fish = people in the $sea, and fishermen = those sent to gather them.

**Symbolizes:** People living in the nations without covenant governance — and the fisherman as the agent sent to gather them

**Opposite:** Beasts of the field — land creatures under a ruler, in contrast to fish with "no ruler" (Hab 1:14)

**Defining verses:** Hab 1:14, Mat 4:19, Mat 13:47–48, Ezek 29:4–5, Ezek 47:10, Jer 16:16

**The surprise:** The very first thing named under human dominion in Gen 1:26 is "the fish of the sea." If $sea = nations, then the dominion mandate begins with authority over the peoples of the nations — and the rest of Scripture shows both God and empires exercising that authority through fishing.

**Connected:** $sea, $net, $snare, $sheep, $lamb, $bread, $babylon, $island

## Fish Across Scripture

### No Ruler Over Them

> *"And makest men as the **fishes** of the $[sea], as the creeping things, that have no ruler over them? They take up all of them with the angle, they catch them in their **$[net]**, and gather them in their drag: therefore they rejoice and are glad."* — Hab 1:14–15

Habakkuk's complaint is about Babylon. God has raised up the Chaldeans as an instrument of judgment, and the prophet is disturbed by the method: the wicked empire catches nations like fish, scooping them up indiscriminately. The people have no covenant shepherd, no king who answers to God — they are fish in open water, and $babylon drags its $net through them.

The next verse reveals something darker still: "They sacrifice unto their $[net], and burn incense unto their drag" (Hab 1:16). $babylon worships its own mechanism of capture. The empire deifies the system that gathers the leaderless fish-people. This is the $net as imperial dragnet — gathering that serves the gatherer, not the gathered.

The contrast with God's own fishing operations throughout Scripture is stark. When God sends fishermen, the catch is for life. When $babylon fishes, the catch is for consumption.

### Dominion Over the Fish

> *"And God said, Let us make man in our image, after our likeness: and let them have dominion over the **fish** of the $[sea], and over the fowl of the air, and over the cattle, and over all the earth."* — Gen 1:26

Fish are named *first* in the dominion mandate. Before birds, before cattle, before "all the earth" — the fish of the $sea. If the $sea represents the nations (as Rev 17:15 explicitly defines — "the waters which thou sawest are peoples, and multitudes, and nations, and tongues"), then authority over the fish of the $sea suggests authority over the peoples within those nations.

This is not a secondary implication. It is the order God chose. Psalm 8:8 echoes the creation mandate: "The fowl of the air, and the **fish** of the $[sea], and whatsoever passeth through the paths of the $[sea]s." The "paths of the seas" are the routes through the nations — and the fish moving along those paths are the people within them.

After the flood, the same dominion is restated: "The fear of you and the dread of you shall be upon every beast of the earth... and upon all the **fishes** of the $[sea]; into your hand are they delivered" (Gen 9:2). The fish are under human authority. The question the rest of Scripture answers is: *whose* authority, and to what end?

### Pharaoh's Fish — A Ruler's People

> *"I will put hooks in thy jaws, and I will cause the **fish** of thy rivers to stick unto thy scales, and I will bring thee up out of the midst of thy rivers, and all the **fish** of thy rivers shall stick unto thy scales."* — Ezek 29:4

Pharaoh is the great crocodile dwelling in the Nile — "the great dragon that lieth in the midst of his rivers" (Ezek 29:3). God promises to hook him out of his waters. And when Pharaoh is dragged out, his *fish* come with him, clinging to his scales.

The identification is unmistakable: the fish are Pharaoh's people. They cling to their ruler. When the ruler is extracted from his domain, the population follows.

> *"And I will leave thee thrown into the wilderness, thee and all the **fish** of thy rivers: thou shalt fall upon the open fields."* — Ezek 29:5

Ruler and people share the same fate. The fish cannot survive apart from their water — pull a nation's people out of their environment and they perish in the open field. Ezekiel's image makes the fish = people equation vivid through the mechanics of the metaphor itself.

### Fishers and Hunters — Two Phases of Gathering

> *"Behold, I will send for many **fishers**, saith the LORD, and they shall **fish** them; and after will I send for many hunters, and they shall hunt them from every mountain, and from every hill, and out of the holes of the rocks."* — Jer 16:16

God describes a two-phase gathering operation. First: fishers who fish broadly — casting into open water, drawing up people from the $sea of nations with nets. Second: hunters who pursue specifically — tracking the scattered from mountains, hills, and $rock-holes where they hide.

The context is Israel's removal from and return to the land (Jer 16:14–15). The fishers work the open waters; the hunters comb the terrain. One method is broad and indiscriminate (like the $net that catches "every kind"); the other is targeted and precise. Both serve the same purpose: God gathering His people from wherever they have been scattered.

This passage illuminates Jesus' commission. When He tells Peter and Andrew "I will make you fishers of men" (Mat 4:19), He is activating the first phase of Jeremiah's prophecy — the broad gathering from the $sea.

### Fishers of Men

> *"And Jesus, walking by the $[sea] of Galilee, saw two brethren, Simon called Peter, and Andrew his brother, casting a **$[net]** into the $[sea]: for they were **fishers**. And he saith unto them, Follow me, and I will make you **fishers** of men."* — Mat 4:18–19

The disciples are already fishermen — men of the $sea, working with $nets. Jesus does not change their vocation. He *transforms* it. The same skills, the same waters, the same labor — but the catch changes from fish to men. Or rather, Jesus reveals what the fish always were.

Mark adds that James and John were "mending their $[net]s" when Jesus called them (Mk 1:19). The fishermen who would gather people from the nations needed repaired equipment. The $net — the instrument of indiscriminate gathering — had to be whole before the kingdom catch could begin.

### The Kingdom Dragnet

> *"Again, the kingdom of heaven is like unto a **$[net]**, that was cast into the $[sea], and gathered of every kind: Which, when it was full, they drew to shore, and sat down, and gathered the good into vessels, but cast the bad away."* — Mat 13:47–48

This is the clearest parable of the fish-equation in action. The $net (G4522 *sagene* — a large seine or dragnet) sweeps through the $sea (the nations) and catches fish of *every kind* (G1085 *genos* — race, family, stock). The kingdom's gathering is indiscriminate. Everyone within the sweep of the $net is pulled in.

The critical detail: separation happens **after** the catch, on the shore — not in the water. The $net does not select. It gathers. Sorting is the work of the angels at "the end of the age" (Mat 13:49). Good fish go into vessels; bad fish are thrown away.

This parable confirms the fish = people equation and adds a warning. Not every fish in the kingdom $net is a "good" fish. Gathering and judging are two different operations, performed at two different times.

### Two Catches — Before and After Resurrection

Luke 5 and John 21 both describe miraculous catches of fish directed by Jesus, and the contrast between them is instructive:

**Before the resurrection** (Lk 5:4–6): Jesus tells Peter to let down his $nets. The catch is so vast that the $net begins to **break** — they must signal a partner boat, and both begin to sink under the weight. The old structures cannot hold what the kingdom is gathering.

**After the resurrection** (Jn 21:6–11): Jesus, standing on the shore, directs the cast to the right side of the boat. They catch 153 great fish — and the $net is **not broken**. Peter drags the catch to shore intact.

The pre-resurrection net breaks. The post-resurrection net holds. Whatever happened between these two catches — the death and resurrection of Christ — it transformed the gathering apparatus. The $net that could not contain the abundance before the cross can hold everything after it.

The number 153 has generated centuries of speculation. What the text emphasizes is not the number but the net: "and for all there were so many, yet was not the $[net] broken" (Jn 21:11). The integrity of the gathering instrument is the point.

### The Restoration River Teeming with Fish

> *"And it shall come to pass, that the **fishers** shall stand upon it from En-gedi even unto En-eglaim; they shall be a place to spread forth **$[net]s**; their **fish** shall be according to their kinds, as the **fish** of the great $[sea], exceeding many."* — Ezek 47:10

Ezekiel's temple vision includes a river flowing from beneath the threshold (Ezek 47:1), growing deeper as it runs until it reaches the Dead Sea and heals its waters (Ezek 47:8). Everywhere the river goes, life appears — and the formerly dead waters teem with fish "exceeding many."

The fishers line the banks with their $nets. The fish are "according to their kinds" — organized, distinguished, abundant. This is the positive vision of fishing in Scripture: not the imperial dragnet of $babylon that worships its own $net, but the kingdom gathering beside the river of life. The waters have been healed. The fish are alive. The fishers are ready.

The contrast with Hab 1:14–16 is deliberate. $babylon's fishing is exploitation; Ezekiel's fishing is restoration. Both use $nets. But the source of life makes all the difference. Where living water flows from God's presence, the fish multiply and the gathering is joyful.

### Fish as Provision — Loaves and Fishes

> *"And they say unto him, We have here but five loaves, and two **fishes**."* — Mat 14:17

In the feeding miracles (Mat 14:17–19, 15:36, Lk 9:13, Jn 6:9–11), fish are consistently paired with $bread. If $bread represents the Word — the covenant terms that sustain life (Deut 8:3, Jn 6:35) — then what do the fish represent when they appear alongside it?

The multitude being fed *is* the fish. Jesus feeds fish *to* the fish-people, multiplying provision for the gathered crowd from almost nothing. Five loaves ($bread — the Word) and two fish (provision drawn from the $sea) feed thousands with baskets left over.

The stitch-point comes in Mat 17:27, where Jesus tells Peter to cast a hook into the $sea: "take up the fish that first cometh up; and when thou hast opened his mouth, thou shalt find a piece of money: that take, and give unto them for me and thee." The provision — even the tax — comes from the $sea, drawn out through the fisherman's labor.

### Fish Given, Not Serpents

> *"Or if he ask a **fish**, will he give him a serpent?"* — Mat 7:10

> *"If he shall ask a **fish**, will he for a **fish** give him a $[serpent]?"* — Lk 11:11

The $bread / fish pairing appears again, set against its counterfeits: "If he ask $[bread], will he give him a stone? Or if he ask a fish, will he give him a $serpent?" (Mat 7:9–10). $bread opposes the stone (dead, inedible). Fish opposes the $serpent — the deceiver, the one whose cunning mimics nourishment but delivers poison.

The pattern suggests that what God gives (fish — people gathered into covenant care) and what the deceiver offers ($serpent — something that looks alive but brings death) are visually similar but opposite in nature. The father who gives good gifts gives fish. The $snare that captures the unwary looks like provision but turns out to be the fowler's trick.

### The Mourning of Fishers — When Waters Fail

> *"The **fishers** also shall mourn, and all they that cast angle into the brooks shall lament, and they that spread $[net]s upon the waters shall languish."* — Isa 19:8

When God judges Egypt, the Nile dries up (Isa 19:5–7). The fish die. The fishers mourn. The gathering fails because the waters fail.

If the waters represent peoples and the fish represent people within those waters, then the drying of the Nile is the collapse of Egypt's population base — and the fishers' mourning is the lament of those whose livelihood depended on drawing from those waters. The pattern mirrors Ezek 29:4–5: Egypt's ruler is extracted, and the fish clinging to his scales come out of the water to die.

Similarly, Hosea 4:3 includes fish in a comprehensive image of national collapse: "Therefore shall the land mourn... yea, the **fishes** of the $[sea] also shall be taken away." When judgment falls on a nation, its people — the fish — are removed.

## Patterns

1. **Fish = people without a shepherd.** Hab 1:14 is the anchor: men made as fish who have "no ruler." The fish's defining condition is leaderlessness — contrast $sheep, who are covenant people under a shepherd. Fish are pre-covenant; $sheep are in-covenant.

2. **The fisherman equation is explicit.** Jesus says it plainly: "fishers of men" (Mat 4:19). Jer 16:16 sends fishers before hunters. The symbolic role of the fisherman is to gather people from the $sea of nations into covenant.

3. **The $net gathers indiscriminately; separation comes later.** Mat 13:47–48 — every kind caught, sorted on shore. The kingdom $net does not pre-select. This distinguishes God's fishermen from $babylon's: both use $nets, but $babylon worships the $net itself (Hab 1:16), while the kingdom uses it for an end beyond the catching.

4. **Pharaoh's fish = his people.** Ezek 29:4–5 makes the equation concrete: the fish cling to the ruler, are dragged out with him, and die in the open field.

5. **Fish multiply where living water flows.** Ezek 47:10 — the river from the temple fills the Dead Sea with fish "exceeding many." Where God's presence goes, the $sea teems with life. Fishers spread their $nets in abundance, not desperation.

6. **Fish paired with $bread = provision from the Word and the $sea.** The loaves-and-fishes miracles consistently unite $bread (the Word) with fish (people/provision from the nations). Both are multiplied by Jesus to feed the gathered multitude.

7. **Two miraculous catches bracket the resurrection.** Lk 5 (net breaks) and Jn 21 (net holds). The cross transforms the gathering instrument. What could not be contained before can be held intact after.

8. **Fish die when waters fail.** Isa 19:8, Hos 4:3, Ps 105:29 — judgment on a nation dries or poisons its waters, and the fish are taken away. Without the $sea, the fish perish; without the social order, the people are destroyed.

## Relationship to Other Symbols
- **$sea** — The fish's native element. The $sea = nations / chaotic peoples (Rev 17:15). Fish in the $sea = people living within the turbulent, uncovenanted mass of humanity. Every fishing action in Scripture — whether by God, by $babylon, or by the disciples — occurs in or from the $sea.

- **$net** — The instrument of gathering that catches indiscriminately. Whether the kingdom dragnet of Mat 13:47, the imperial $net of Hab 1:15 that $babylon worships, or the restored $nets of Ezek 47:10, the $net is how fish-people are enclosed and drawn out. The $net does not select; separation happens after the catch.

- **$snare** — The $net's predatory counterpart at the individual level. Where the $net sweeps broadly through the $sea, the $snare is the concealed mechanism that captures by stealth (Ecc 9:12 pairs them directly: "fish in an evil $[net], birds in the $[snare]"). Both are instruments of seizure — but the $snare operates through hiddenness and suddenness.

- **$sheep** — The covenant counterpart to fish. $sheep = covenant people under a shepherd; fish = people without a ruler (Hab 1:14). Gathering fish out of the $sea and bringing them under a shepherd's care is, in effect, the transformation from fish to $sheep — from leaderless wanderers in the nations to covenant people who hear the shepherd's voice.

- **$lamb** — The offering paired with the fish in Levitical sacrifice. When the worshipper cannot afford a $lamb, the dove substitutes. But in the feeding miracles, it is fish that accompany $bread — provision for the multitude drawn from the $sea and multiplied by the one who is Himself the $lamb.

- **$bread** — Consistently paired with fish in the Gospels. $bread = Word / covenant terms; fish = provision from the $sea. Together they represent the full sustenance of the gathered people: God's Word and the abundance drawn from the nations.

- **$babylon** — The empire that fishes for its own glory. $babylon's $net catches nations-as-fish (Hab 1:14–16), but unlike God's fishermen, $babylon worships its own gathering mechanism. The imperial dragnet serves the empire, not the fish. This is the anti-fishing: capture without covenant, gathering without a shepherd.

- **$island** — Set-apart people distinguished from the $sea. If fish are people *in* the chaotic waters of the nations, $islands are people who have emerged *from* those waters — sanctified, standing on solid ground. The $island is what the fish becomes when drawn out of the $sea and placed on firm land.

## Dictionary Comparison

Webster's 1913 entry for **fish** begins:

> “Fish, n. Etym: [F. fiche peg, mark, fr. fisher to fix.] Defn: A counter, used in various games.”

The dictionary supplies the ordinary sense. The approved definition above is narrower or figurative only where Scripture's own cross-references require that transfer.

## Competing Definitions Tested

- Several consensus responses treat Christ via the Ichthys emblem as the dominant broad Christian meaning, whereas the book gives only the people/converts reading. However, every response recognizes the book’s identification as primary or common, and the book does not explicitly reject the Christological emblem.

- One could argue the book REFINES rather than matches, because it makes fish primarily denote all humanity in the nations (Hab 1:14) with the redeemed as a subset drawn out, whereas some consensus responses (B, D) treat fish more narrowly as converts/believers, and the book silently drops the ICHTHYS Christ meaning that two responses rank as primary. However, both elements (humanity as fish; the catch as converts) are explicitly present in the consensus responses, so this is emphasis, not a boundary change.

- It could be argued that the book completely ignores the widely recognized Christological meaning (ICHTHYS), which some consensus responses list as the absolute primary symbol, though they concede it is not derived from direct biblical exegesis.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.

## Conclusion

**A person in the [sea](/research/symbols/sea/) of nations, clean or unclean, gathered and sorted at the [harvest](/research/symbols/harvest/). Fishers of men draw the catch from the sea in the [net](/research/symbols/net/).**

This is the approved glossary conclusion; broader applications in the study remain subordinate to the defining texts and countertexts.

## Corpus and Method

This study checks the glossary's defining citations first, then compares them with every KJV verse that prints *fish*, *fishes*, *fishers*, *fishermen*. The English-form sweep found **65 verses**. It is exhaustive for those printed forms, but it is not a substitute for a Strong's-number sweep where several Hebrew or Greek words share one English translation.
