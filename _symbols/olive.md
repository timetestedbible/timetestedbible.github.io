### Webster's Definition

Webster's 1913 entry for **olive** begins: “(Bot.) (a) A tree (Olea Europæa) with small oblong or elliptical leaves, axillary clusters of flowers, and oval, one-seeded drupes.”

These definitions may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

Israel, the covenant people.

> *“The LORD called thy name, A green olive tree, fair, and of goodly fruit: with the noise of a great tumult he hath kindled fire upon it, and the branches of it are broken.”* — Jeremiah 11:16

> *“I found Israel like grapes in the wilderness; I saw your fathers as the firstripe in the fig tree at her first time: but they went to Baalpeor, and separated themselves unto that shame; and their abominations were according as they loved.”* — Hosea 9:10

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Dictionary Comparison

Webster's 1913 entry for **olive** begins:

> “Ol”ive, n. Etym: [F., fr. L. oliva, akin to Gr. Oil.] 1. (Bot.) (a) A tree (Olea Europæa) with small oblong or elliptical leaves, axillary clusters of flowers, and oval, one-seeded drupes. The tree has been cultivated for its fruit for thousands of years, and its branches are the emblems of peace. The wood is yellowish brown and beautifully variegated. (b) The fruit of the olive. It has been much improved by cultivation, and is used for making pickles. Olive oil is pressed from its flesh.”

The dictionary supplies the ordinary sense. The definition above is narrower or figurative only where Scripture's own cross-references require that transfer.

## Competing Definitions Tested

- The entry’s brevity could be read as making this identification universal, whereas the consensus emphasizes that olive, vine, and fig can have other meanings depending on context. Nothing in the entry, however, explicitly asserts exclusivity or rejects those alternatives.

- The two printed citations directly cover the olive (Jer 11:16, 'The LORD called thy name, A green olive tree' addressed to Israel) and the grapes/fig (Hos 9:10, 'I found Israel like grapes... as the firstripe in the fig tree'), so the vine element is supported only via 'grapes' rather than an explicit vine text like Psalm 80 or Isaiah 5; one could argue the vine specifically receives slightly indirect support, though the identification itself is uncontroversial.

- The printed citations cover only olive (Jer 11:16) and fig (Hos 9:10) as Israel, omitting an explicit vine reference, and the consensus notes that vine imagery can shift christologically in John 15.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
