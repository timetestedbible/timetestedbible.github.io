### Webster's Definition

Webster's 1913 entry for **door** begins: “An opening in the wall of a house or of an apartment, by which to go in and out; an entrance way.”

These definitions may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

The destination of the way — the entrance to the house of God, marked on the posts.

> *“And he was afraid, and said, How dreadful is this place! this is none other but the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven.”* — Genesis 28:17

> *“And they shall take of the blood, and strike it on the two side posts and on the upper door post of the houses, wherein they shall eat it.”* — Exodus 12:7

> *“And thou shalt write them upon the posts of thy house, and on thy gates.”* — Deuteronomy 6:9

> *“I am the door: by me if any man enter in, he shall be saved, and shall go in and out, and find pasture.”* — John 10:9

> *“And had a wall great and high, and had twelve gates, and at the gates twelve angels, and names written thereon, which are the names of the twelve tribes of the children of Israel:”* — Revelation 21:12

> *“On the east three gates; on the north three gates; on the south three gates; and on the west three gates.”* — Revelation 21:13

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

The dictionary supplies the ordinary sense. The definition above is narrower or figurative only where Scripture's own cross-references require that transfer.

## Competing Definitions Tested

- John 10:9 strongly supports Christ as the saving door, and Exodus 12:7 and Deuteronomy 6:9 support marked doorposts or gates. However, the printed excerpts do not explicitly call the door the destination of the way or the entrance to the house of God, so that fuller synthesis is only partially established by these citations alone.

- One could argue REFINED because the book narrows the symbol to 'the entrance to the house of God marked on the posts,' elevating a Passover/Shema typology the consensus treats as minority, and reframing the door as the destination of a 'way' schema rather than simply access to salvation; this could be read as a material reweighting of the symbol's center. But since the consensus's central referent (Christ as the saving entrance, John 10:9) is fully retained and explicitly cited, the reweighting is emphasis, not boundary change.

- It could be argued that the book's specific focus on the door being 'marked on the posts' (drawing from Ex 12:7 and Deut 6:9) narrows the symbol to covenantal or atoning contexts, whereas the consensus sees the New Testament 'access' motif as broader.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
