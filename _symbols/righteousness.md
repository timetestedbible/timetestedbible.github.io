# Righteousness

## Summary

**Right-living. God is Right-Living by definition; for those under His authority, righteousness is His commandments lived out.**

### Webster's Definition

Webster's 1913 entry for **righteousness** begins: “The quality or state of being righteous; holiness; purity; uprightness; rectitude.”

These definitions may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

Righteousness is actual right-living: God’s own right ways and, for those under his authority, obedience to his commandments. It may be graciously given and reckoned, but reckoning does not classify wrong-living as righteousness.

> *“He is the Rock, his work is perfect: for all his ways are judgment: a God of truth and without iniquity, just and right is he.”* — Deuteronomy 32:4

> *“And it shall be our righteousness, if we observe to do all these commandments before the LORD our God, as he hath commanded us.”* — Deuteronomy 6:25

> *“My tongue shall speak of thy word: for all thy commandments are righteousness.”* — Psalms 119:172

> *“The LORD is righteous in all his ways, and holy in all his works.”* — Psalms 145:17

> *“But which of you, having a servant plowing or feeding cattle, will say unto him by and by, when he is come from the field, Go and sit down to meat?”* — Luke 17:7

> *“And will not rather say unto him, Make ready wherewith I may sup, and gird thyself, and serve me, till I have eaten and drunken; and afterward thou shalt eat and drink?”* — Luke 17:8

> *“Doth he thank that servant because he did the things that were commanded him? I trow not.”* — Luke 17:9

> *“So likewise ye, when ye shall have done all those things which are commanded you, say, We are unprofitable servants: we have done that which was our duty to do.”* — Luke 17:10

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Dictionary Comparison

Webster's 1913 entry for **righteousness** begins:

> “Right”eous*ness, n. Etym: [AS. rihtwisnes.] 1. The quality or state of being righteous; holiness; purity; uprightness; rectitude. Note: Righteousness, as used in Scripture and theology, in which it chiefly occurs, is nearly equivalent to holiness, comprehending holy principles and affections of heart, and conformity of life to the divine law.”

The dictionary supplies the ordinary sense. The definition above is narrower or figurative only where Scripture's own cross-references require that transfer.

## Competing Definitions Tested

A common reading is: **primarily right standing before God — Christ's imputed righteousness received by faith apart from works — with right-living as its subsequent fruit and evidence.**

- Deuteronomy 6:25, Psalm 119:172, and 1 John 3:7 strongly support an ethical or obedience-based use, while Luke 17:7–10 supports the claim that obedience does not earn wages from God. However, Romans 4:5–8 and Philippians 3:9 appear to distinguish reckoned righteousness from works or law-based righteousness, and the printed citations alone do not firmly establish the book’s exclusive reinterpretation of Paul’s “without works” language as merely anti-wage rather than as a distinction between justificatory status and obedience.

- One could argue DIVERGENT: the consensus's minimum core is 'right standing before God received by faith apart from works,' and the book's core is 'right-living by commandment-keeping,' with an explicit denial that the gift renames wrong-living right — a direct contradiction of the imputed-status-to-the-ungodly identification that all three responses make primary. If the forensic status is treated as the consensus's central referent rather than one mechanism within a broader category, the book replaces it. The REFINED verdict depends on reading the consensus core broadly (standing + conformity), which the responses' own wording supports but does not compel. Additionally, the printed excerpts (Rom 4:5-6 'worketh not... without works') sit uneasily with the book's wage-work-only reading, weakening the entry's standalone support.

- Key Pauline texts printed (Rom 4:4-8; Phil 3:9) are classically read as forensic imputation of status apart from works, so the book's prioritization of commandment-keeping (Deut 6:25; 1 John 3:7) and duty (Luke 17) may conflate or subordinate justification to sanctification against the consensus primary.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
