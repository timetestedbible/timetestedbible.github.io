# Idolatry

**Self-rule — rejecting God's word as the standard and substituting one's own.**

> *"For rebellion is as the sin of witchcraft, and stubbornness is as iniquity and **idolatry**. Because thou hast rejected the word of the LORD, he hath also rejected thee from being king."* — 1 Sam 15:23

---

## The Key Insight

Saul never bowed to a statue.

He was told to destroy every animal of the Amalekites. He kept the best sheep and oxen alive. When confronted, he explained his reasoning: he saved them to sacrifice to the LORD. A reasonable-sounding substitution — his own judgment in place of the explicit command.

Samuel's verdict is devastating: "Stubbornness is as iniquity and **idolatry**. Because thou hast *rejected the word of the LORD*."

The equation is explicit. Idolatry is not defined here by what Saul worshipped but by what he *rejected*. He rejected God's word. He substituted his own standard. That substitution — regardless of how pious the reasoning — *is* the idolatry.

This reframes every subsequent appearance of the word. The golden calf, the high places, the household gods, Babylon's great image — these are not the *definition* of idolatry but its *expressions*. The root is something far more elemental: the human will displacing the divine command.

**Symbolizes:** Self-rule — substituting one's own standard for God's word

**Opposite:** Submission to God — $faith (trusting obedience to God's standard)

**Defining verses:** 1 Sam 15:23, Col 3:5, Exod 20:3, Ezek 14:3, Isa 44:16–17

**The surprise:** Paul equates *covetousness* — simple wanting — with idolatry (Col 3:5). No temple, no statue, no ritual. The desire itself, when it overrides God's ordinance, is the idol. The ultimate idol has no shape. It is the self.

**Connected:** $truth, $faith, $believe, $fornication, $virgin, $harlot, $wickedness, $bread, $name

## Idolatry Across Scripture

### The Man Who Makes His Own God

> *"He burneth part thereof in the fire; with part thereof he eateth flesh; he roasteth roast, and is satisfied: yea, he warmeth himself, and saith, Aha, I am warm, I have seen the fire: And the residue thereof he maketh a god, even his graven image: he falleth down unto it, and worshippeth it, and prayeth unto it, and saith, Deliver me; for thou art my god."* — Isa 44:16–17

Isaiah's mockery of the idol-maker is among the sharpest satire in Scripture. A man cuts down a tree. Half of it he burns for warmth, cooks his dinner over it, and is satisfied. The other half he carves into a god and asks it to save him.

The absurdity is the point. The worshipper *created* the thing he now begs for deliverance. He shaped it with his own hands, from material he selected, in a form he designed. The "god" is his own projection — a standard he invented, a master he built to serve his own purposes.

> *"The **idols** of the heathen are silver and gold, the work of men's hands."* — Ps 115:4

"The work of men's hands" — this phrase appears again and again (Deut 4:28, Ps 115:4, Ps 135:15, Isa 2:8, Jer 1:16). The Hebrew roots for idol reinforce this: H6091 *atsab* (from "to shape, fashion") = something man-made; H1544 *gillul* (from "to roll") = worthless thing, sometimes translated with connotations of dung. Idols are, at their linguistic root, man-fabricated replacements for the living God.

This is why the mockery works: the idol-maker hasn't found a superior god. He has manufactured one to his own specifications. He has become his own standard-setter — which is to say, he has made *himself* the ultimate authority and given the resulting product a religious veneer.

### Covetousness — The Idol Without a Shape

> *"Mortify therefore your members which are upon the earth; fornication, uncleanness, inordinate affection, evil concupiscence, and covetousness, **which is idolatry**."* — Col 3:5

Paul's equation strips idolatry of every external feature. No image. No altar. No temple. Covetousness — the desire for what is not yours, the wanting that overrides God's ordinance — *is* idolatry.

This only makes sense if idolatry is not fundamentally about objects of worship but about the orientation of the will. Covetousness places self-desire above God's word. It says, in effect, "what I want matters more than what God has said." That posture — self as standard-setter — is the root that connects the golden calf to the boardroom, the Asherah pole to the quiet interior of the heart.

The pattern holds across the testimony of Scripture: wherever idolatry is diagnosed at its deepest level, the diagnosis is not "you worshipped the wrong statue" but "you followed your own will instead of God's command."

### Idols of the Heart

> *"Son of man, these men have set up their **idols** in their heart, and put the stumblingblock of their iniquity before their face: should I be enquired of at all by them?"* — Ezek 14:3

The elders of Israel come to inquire of God through Ezekiel. They are sitting before the prophet. There is no visible idol in the room. But God sees what is invisible to Ezekiel: they have erected idols *in their heart*.

The internal location is decisive. Whatever these elders are serving — their own political calculations, their own religious preferences, their tribal loyalties — it has been installed where God's word should govern. They come to the prophet's door asking for divine guidance while the seat of authority in their inner life is already occupied.

God's response is not to answer their question. He asks His own: "Should I be enquired of at all by them?" The inquiry itself is fraudulent. You cannot ask God for His standard while yours is already enthroned.

This passage moves idolatry permanently past the realm of temples and statues. A person can sit in the right place, before the right prophet, asking the right questions — and be an idolater. The idol is in the heart, and its name is self-rule.

### The First Commandment — What "Other Gods" Actually Means

> *"Thou shalt have no other gods before me."* — Exod 20:3

The commandment does not say "no other statues." It says "no other *gods*." A god, in the biblical framework, is whatever you serve, obey, trust, and look to as ultimate authority. The Hebrew word *elohim* is applied not only to the Creator but to judges (Exod 21:6, Ps 82:6) — those who exercise governing authority. To have "other gods" is to submit to other governing authorities in place of the one true God.

This is why idolatry is not merely a religious category but a *governance* category. It answers the question: whose word rules your life? Whose standard do you follow when it conflicts with God's? The thing that wins that contest — whether it is a carved image, a political alliance, a cultural consensus, or your own preference — that is the god you serve.

The first commandment establishes the framework that makes 1 Sam 15:23 intelligible. Saul's sin was not religious in the narrow sense. It was governmental. He ruled himself rather than letting God's word rule him. He had another god — himself — and that *is* idolatry.

### The Chain: Self-Rule to Fornication to Harlotry

The pipeline of co-occurring symbols reveals a consistent chain in Scripture:

$idolatry (the root condition: self-rule) → $fornication (the act: turning to other masters) → $harlot (the identity: the covenant-breaker)

Consider Hosea's marriage. God tells him to marry "a wife of whoredoms: for the land hath committed great whoredom, departing from the LORD" (Hos 1:2). The $fornication is not sexual sin — it is explicitly defined as "departing from the LORD." Israel has left the covenant and gone after other masters. The act of departure is $fornication. The resulting identity is that of the $harlot. But what drives the departure? Self-rule. Israel's own heart, setting up its own standard, choosing its own alliances.

> *"Ephraim is joined to **idols**: let him alone."* — Hos 4:17

> *"My people ask counsel at their stocks, and their staff declareth unto them: for the spirit of whoredoms hath caused them to err, and they have gone a whoring from under their God."* — Hos 4:12

The "spirit of whoredoms" is the internal drive — the self-rule that produces the outward act of turning to other masters. The stocks and staff (divination implements) are not the disease. They are the symptom. The disease is the heart that has already decided to govern itself.

### The Opposite: Faith as Submission to God's Word

If idolatry is self-rule, its opposite is not merely "worship of the true God" but submission to God's word as the governing standard — which is what $faith means in Scripture.

$faith is trusting obedience (Heb 11:1, Jas 2:17–26, Rom 1:5). $believe is the verb form: to commit oneself to obey (Jn 3:36 where "believeth not" = G544 *apeitheō*, literally "to disobey"). $truth is God's standard — His commandments (Jn 17:17, Ps 119:142).

The structure resolves into a clean opposition:

| | Idolatry | Faith |
|---|---|---|
| **Standard** | Self's word | God's word ($truth) |
| **Posture** | Autonomy | Submission |
| **Action** | Self-rule | Obedience ($believe) |
| **Covenant** | Broken ($name profaned) | Kept ($name honored) |
| **Identity** | $harlot | $virgin |

$wickedness (lawlessness — G458 *anomia*, literally "without law") is the behavioral expression of idolatry. When self replaces God's standard, the result is lawlessness — not because the person follows no rules at all, but because they follow their own. The "mystery of iniquity" (2 Thess 2:7) is *anomia* — lawlessness — and its final expression is "that Wicked" (G459 *anomos*, "the lawless one") who enthrones himself as god (2 Thess 2:4).

### Keep Yourselves from Idols

> *"Little children, keep yourselves from **idols**. Amen."* — 1 Jn 5:21

This is the last verse of 1 John. The entire letter has been about walking in $truth (keeping God's commandments, 1 Jn 2:4), loving the brethren, and abiding in Christ. There is no discussion of temples, statues, or pagan worship anywhere in the letter. Yet John closes with this: keep yourselves from idols.

The warning makes sense only if idolatry is the internal condition that the entire letter has been warning against — the drift from God's standard toward self-governance, from $truth toward the lie, from $faith toward the autonomy that John has been diagnosing throughout.

The same pattern governs 1 John's framing of "the world":

> *"Love not the world, neither the things that are in the world… the lust of the flesh, and the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life, is not of the Father, but is of the world."* — 1 Jn 2:15–16

Lust of the flesh, lust of the eyes, pride of life — these are not statues. They are the desires that assert themselves as governing authorities when God's word is displaced. They are, in Paul's language, the covetousness that *is* idolatry (Col 3:5).

"Keep yourselves from idols" is not a postscript about pagan temples. It is the summary of the whole letter: keep yourselves from self-rule.

## The Bread That Is Rejected

$bread represents God's word — His covenant terms (Deut 8:3, Mat 4:4, Jn 6:35). To receive $bread is to receive God's standard. To reject it is to set up your own.

This is the texture of 1 Sam 15:23. Samuel does not say "you worshipped another god." He says "thou hast *rejected the word of the LORD*." The $bread was offered. The covenant terms were spoken. Saul refused them and substituted his own. That refusal — that rejection of the $bread — is the essence of idolatry.

The connection runs through the prophets. In Amos 8:11, God threatens a famine "not of $[bread], nor a thirst for $[water], but of hearing the words of the LORD." When God's word is withdrawn, what fills the vacuum? Man's word. Self-rule. The famine of $bread *produces* idolatry, because a people without God's standard will inevitably manufacture their own.

## The Name That Is Profaned

$name represents covenant relationship (2 Sam 7:13, Acts 4:12). To take God's $name is to enter His covenant. To profane His $name is to treat that covenant as nothing.

The Third Commandment — "Thou shalt not take the $[name] of the LORD thy God in vain" (Exod 20:7) — is not a prohibition against pronunciation. If $name = covenant, then taking it "in vain" means entering the covenant relationship while living as though it doesn't govern you. This is precisely what Ezekiel's elders did (Ezek 14:3): they bore God's $name as covenant people while idols occupied their hearts.

This is also the substance of Jesus' warning in Mat 7:22–23: "Many will say to me in that day, Lord, Lord, have we not prophesied in thy $[name]?" — and He responds, "I never knew you: depart from me, ye that work $[wickedness]" (G458 *anomia* — lawlessness). They invoked the covenant $name while practicing lawlessness — self-rule. They bore the $name but rejected the standard. That is idolatry dressed in covenant language.

## Patterns

1. **Idolatry is diagnosed by what is rejected, not by what is worshipped.** 1 Sam 15:23 — "thou hast rejected the word of the LORD." The defining act is not the embrace of a false god but the refusal of the true standard.

2. **The ultimate idol is the self.** Col 3:5 — covetousness (self-desire) *is* idolatry. Isa 44:16–17 — man fashions his own god. Ezek 14:3 — idols set up in the heart. No external object is required.

3. **Idolatry is internal before it is external.** Ezek 14:3 — heart idols precede temple idols. The outward expression (statue, ritual, alliance) is always the symptom; the inward condition (self-rule) is the disease.

4. **The absurdity of idolatry is the absurdity of self-creation.** Isaiah's satire (Isa 44:16–17) reveals the circularity: man makes god, then asks god to deliver man. The creature creates the standard and then submits to it — but since the creature created it, the submission is to *himself*.

5. **Idolatry and $wickedness share a root.** $wickedness = lawlessness (G458 *anomia*). Idolatry = self-rule (rejecting God's law). Both describe the same displacement of God's standard — $wickedness from the behavioral side, idolatry from the authority side.

6. **The chain is consistent: self-rule → $fornication → $harlot.** The root condition (idolatry/self-rule) produces the act ($fornication — turning to other masters), which produces the identity ($harlot — covenant-breaker). The reverse chain is restoration: $harlot → repentance → $virgin ($faith restored).

7. **Idolatry cannot coexist with $faith.** $faith = trusting obedience to God's standard. Idolatry = substituting one's own. They are structurally incompatible. This is why the first commandment precedes all others — if the authority question is not settled, no other commandment can be rightly obeyed.

## Connections

- **$truth** — God's standard, His commandments (Jn 17:17, Ps 119:142). $truth is what idolatry *rejects*. Wherever $truth governs, idolatry is absent. Wherever $truth is displaced, idolatry is present. The two are mutually exclusive standards.

- **$faith** — Trusting obedience to God's word (Heb 11:1, Rom 1:5). The direct opposite of idolatry. $faith receives God's standard and acts on it; idolatry discards God's standard and acts on its own. Saul's disobedience was simultaneously a failure of $faith and an act of idolatry.

- **$believe** — The verb form of $faith: to commit to obey (Jn 3:36, where "believeth not" = G544 *apeitheō*, to disobey). To $believe is to submit to God's word as governing authority. To refuse is to retain self-rule — which is idolatry by definition.

- **$fornication** — Idolatry's outward act: turning to other masters, departing from the LORD (Hos 1:2, Deut 31:16). $fornication is what idolaters *do*. The relationship: idolatry is the root condition; $fornication is the covenant-breaking action it produces.

- **$virgin** — The faithful, exclusively devoted covenant partner (Rev 14:4, 2 Cor 11:2). The $virgin is the anti-idolater — one who has not gone after other gods, who follows the Lamb alone. Where the idolater divides allegiance, the $virgin gives undivided loyalty.

- **$harlot** — The idolater's identity: the covenant-breaker (Isa 1:21, Rev 17:1). Once the $virgin commits $fornication (idolatry), she becomes the $harlot. Babylon, the "Mother of Harlots" (Rev 17:5), is the global system of self-rule dressed in religious garments.

- **$wickedness** — Lawlessness, covenant transgression (Deut 17:2, 2 Thess 2:7). $wickedness is what idolatry *produces* in behavior. Self-rule yields lawlessness, because when God's standard is rejected, no standard remains except the self's — which Scripture calls *anomia*.

- **$bread** — God's word, His covenant terms (Deut 8:3, Jn 6:35). $bread is what idolatry *refuses to eat*. Saul rejected the $bread (God's spoken command) and substituted his own reasoning. The famine of $bread (Amos 8:11) creates the conditions for idolatry to flourish.

- **$name** — Covenant relationship (2 Sam 7:13, Acts 4:12). Idolatry profanes the $name by wearing the covenant externally while governing oneself internally. Taking the $name "in vain" is the covenantal expression of idolatry — bearing the relationship while rejecting its terms.

## For Further Study

- **$truth** — If idolatry is the rejection of God's standard, what *is* that standard? The $truth study traces the chain from God's word to His law to His commandments — the very thing idolatry displaces.

- **$faith** — Idolatry's direct opposite. How does Scripture define the trusting obedience that submits to God's word rather than substituting one's own?

- **$fornication** — The act that flows from idolatry's root. How does Scripture use the marriage metaphor to describe covenant-breaking, and why does it matter that $fornication is not primarily a sexual category?

- **$bread** — God's word as covenant terms — the $bread that idolatry refuses. How does the manna test (Exod 16:4) reveal the connection between receiving God's word and resisting self-rule?

- **$name** — The covenant relationship that idolatry profanes from within. What does it mean to bear God's $name while practicing self-governance?
