These definitions may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

The fatherless/orphan symbolizes God's covenant people left without their Father, identified as the remnant.

> *“We are orphans and fatherless, our mothers are as widows.”* — Lamentations 5:3

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Competing Definitions Tested

- Lamentations 5:3 supports a corporate people describing themselves as orphaned, but the printed verse alone does not identify them as “the remnant,” explicitly identify the absent Father as God, or establish this as the general symbolic meaning of fatherless/orphan.

- One could argue for DIVERGENT: the consensus's primary referent is literal vulnerable individuals whose care tests justice, while the book replaces that with a corporate-symbolic referent (the remnant), and treating 'fatherless' as code for the remnant arguably rejects the plain ethical sense rather than refining it. Additionally, the 'remnant' specification exceeds even the minority corporate alternative, which speaks of national desolation generally rather than the faithful remnant specifically.

- Restricting the definition solely to the covenant remnant ignores the overwhelming majority of biblical texts where the fatherless function literally and ethically to represent socially vulnerable individuals needing justice and provision.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
