### Webster's Definition

Webster's 1913 entry for **justice** begins: “The quality of being just; conformity to the principles of righteousness and rectitude in all things; strict performance of moral obligations; practical conformity to human or divine law; integrity in the dealings of men with each other; rectitude; equity; uprightness.”

These definitions may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

Justice is righteousness under a second English name, backed by one Hebrew word, consisting of the commandments done in situations where a neighbor can be wronged or righted.

> *“For I know him, that he will command his children and his household after him, and they shall keep the way of the LORD, to do justice and judgment; that the LORD may bring upon Abraham that which he hath spoken of him.”* — Genesis 18:19

> *“Justice and judgment are the habitation of thy throne: mercy and truth shall go before thy face.”* — Psalms 89:14

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Competing Definitions Tested

- Psalm 89:14 and Psalm 97:2 apply justice to the foundation of God’s throne, which may suggest a broader divine-governance sense than the book’s specifically neighbor-facing formulation. The printed references also do not, in English alone, establish the lexical claim that one Hebrew word lies behind both ‘righteousness’ and ‘justice.’

- One could argue MATCH: several consensus responses already note that dikaiosyne and the Hebrew vocabulary make 'justice' and 'righteousness' overlapping renderings, and the consensus core includes right relationships and neighbor-protection, so the book merely restates a recognized point. But the book's insistence on a single Hebrew word (identifying KJV 'justice' with tsedaqah specifically and excluding mishpat, which consensus responses fold into the term) and its restriction of justice to the neighbor-facing commandments is a material boundary move, not mere wording. The printed parallel pairs (Gen 18:19/Jer 22:3; Ps 89:14/97:2) plausibly show the same Hebrew word rendered 'justice' in one verse and 'righteousness' in the parallel, but only two KJV excerpts are actually provided, so the philological claim is supported only partially by what is printed.

- It could be argued that the book's definition reduces justice entirely to inter-human ethics ('where a neighbor can be wronged'), potentially downplaying the aspects of divine retributive justice or forensic justification noted by parts of the consensus.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
