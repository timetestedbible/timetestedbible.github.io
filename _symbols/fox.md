# Fox

## Summary

**The breach-dweller: the false prophet or usurper at home in the ruins he was sent to repair — "thy prophets are like the foxes in the ruins… ye have not gone up into the breaches." He spoils the vineyard at blossom, walks the desolate [mountain](/research/symbols/mountain/) while the true throne endures, and carries [fire](/research/symbols/fire/) tail-to-tail through the [field](/research/symbols/field/); the foxes have holes, and the Son of man nowhere to lay his head.**

The notebook trail is in [research-fox.md](https://github.com/timetestedbible/timetestedbible.github.io/blob/main/books/symbolic-language/research/research-fox.md). The word appears 9 times in all of Scripture — Hebrew *shu'al* 6 times, Greek *alōpēx* 3 — and the Greek Septuagint renders the Hebrew with *alōpēx* at every checked site, so both testaments are handling one word. Nine occurrences is a corpus small enough to read in full, and this study reads all of them.

## Definitions

### Symbolic Definition

**The breach-dweller: the false prophet or usurper at home in the ruins he was sent to repair.** The sections below derive it; the definition is proposed for the glossary and awaits approval.

### Literal Biblical Definition

A small den-dwelling canine of ruins and dry places — the Hebrew *shu'al* ranges over the jackal as well: it burrows in holes (Matt 8:20), scavenges the fallen (Ps 63:10), and raids ripening vineyards (Song 2:15). Every literal property is load-bearing in the symbol.

### Webster's Definition

Not yet quoted; the 1913 entry will be added verbatim from the source text. The dictionary supplies the ordinary animal; the definition above is figurative only where Scripture's own cross-references require the transfer.

## Corpus and Method

The sweep is Strong's-complete, not English-form-only: Hebrew *shu'al* (H7776) appears in 6 verses (Judg 15:4; Neh 4:3; Ps 63:10; Song 2:15; Lam 5:18; Ezek 13:4) and Greek *alōpēx* (G258) in 3 (Matt 8:20; Luke 9:58; Luke 13:32) — 9 verses, 9 printed occurrences, verified against kjv_strongs.txt and wlc.txt. The LXX renders *shu'al* with *alōpēx* at every checked site (Song 2:15; Lam 5:18; Ezek 13:4) and the Hebrew Gospels render *alōpēx* back as *shu'al* (Matt 8:20; Luke 13:32), so both testaments handle one lexeme. Place-names built on the word (Shual, Hazar-shual) are excluded; the distinct word *tannim* (H8577, KJV "dragons," the howler of desolations) is a separate study.

## The Claim

When Jesus says "Go ye, and tell that fox" (Luke 13:32), the modern reader hears *sly*. Craftiness is a Greek proverb about foxes, not a biblical definition — and Scripture does not leave the word to proverbs. It defines the fox out loud, in an oracle built for the purpose, and then uses it the same way at every one of its 9 appearances. The claim of this study: **the fox is the breach-dweller — the false prophet or usurper who makes his home in the ruin he was sent to repair.** Test every occurrence and see.

## Scripture States It Plainly

Ezekiel is sent against the prophets of Israel — "the foolish prophets, that follow their own spirit, and have seen nothing" (Ezek 13:3). Then comes the equation itself:

> "O Israel, thy prophets are **like the foxes in the ruins**." — Ezekiel 13:4

The KJV prints "deserts," but the Hebrew is *ba-choravot* — **the ruins**, the waste places of a broken-down land. The very next verse tells us why the fox is the right animal, by stating what these prophets failed to do:

> "Ye have **not gone up into the breaches**, neither made up the **hedge** for the house of Israel to **stand in the battle** in the day of the LORD." — Ezekiel 13:5

Here is the whole symbol in two verses. The fox is the creature *of* the breach — it dens in broken walls, gaps, and ruins. The prophet's calling is the exact opposite of a fox's instinct: "I sought for a man among them, that should **make up the hedge, and stand in the gap** before me for the land… but I found none" (Ezek 22:30). A true prophet stands in the breach; a false prophet *lives* in it. The gap is his habitat and his livelihood — mending it would evict him. Ezekiel finishes the portrait in the same oracle: these prophets build the counterfeit repair, a [wall](/research/symbols/wall/) daubed with untempered mortar, and the storm of God flattens it (Ezek 13:10-16).

## The Little Foxes and the Vineyard

Scripture has already told us what the vineyard is: "the **vineyard** of the LORD of hosts is the **house of Israel**" (Isa 5:7). Now watch what the fox does to it:

> "Take us the foxes, the little foxes, **that spoil the vines**: for our vines have **tender grapes**." — Song of Solomon 2:15

The Hebrew behind "spoil" is *mechablim* — ruin, corrupt — and the timing is the point: the vines are *semadar*, **in blossom**. The fox ruins the vine before fruit can set — false teaching does its damage at the tender stage, and even the *little* foxes do it. The command is not to study them but to **catch** them. The [grapes](/research/symbols/grapes/) that should have come are the loss.

## The Fox on the Ruined Mountain

Lamentations closes with Zion emptied, and the fox arrives on cue — followed immediately by the contrast that gives the picture its meaning:

> "Because of the **mountain of Zion, which is desolate, the foxes walk upon it**. Thou, O LORD, remainest for ever; **thy throne** from generation to generation." — Lamentations 5:18-19

Foxes pacing the ruined throne-mount; the enduring throne set against them in the next breath. Hold that picture and read the one time Jesus uses the word for a man:

> "Go ye, and tell **that fox**, Behold, I cast out devils, and I do cures to day and to morrow, and the third day I shall be perfected." — Luke 13:32

Herod — an Idumean holding Judah's throne, the killer of the Baptist. The identification is not a loose insult, because the same pericope ends with the fox's habitat being declared: "Behold, **your house is left unto you desolate**" (Luke 13:35). Lamentations painted a fox walking the desolation; Luke gives the fox a name inside an oracle of desolation. The usurper is at home exactly where the true throne lies in ruins.

## A Fox Against the Wall

When Jerusalem's wall is being rebuilt, the enemies of the work reach for this animal:

> "Now Tobiah the Ammonite was by him, and he said, Even that which they build, **if a fox go up, he shall even break down their stone wall**." — Nehemiah 4:3

The mocker assigns the fox its known power — breach-making — and invokes it against the gap-menders. The taunt fails; the wall stands. Notice who speaks fox: the adversaries of the [wall](/research/symbols/wall/). The sides in Nehemiah are the sides of Ezekiel 13 — breach-menders against breach-dwellers.

## The Liars' Portion

Psalm 63 sentences the king's enemies, and its last line names what kind of men they were:

> "Those that seek my soul, to destroy it, shall go into the lower parts of the earth. They shall fall by the sword: they shall be **a portion for foxes**. But the king shall rejoice in God… the mouth of **them that speak lies** shall be stopped." — Psalm 63:9-11

The fallen liars become fox-food. Ruin feeds the ruin-creature, and the false take their portion with the false — the psalm's own frame runs from the seekers of the king's life to the stopping of lying mouths, with the foxes collecting in between.

## Samson's 300 Torches

Judges hands us the strangest fox text in Scripture, and the symbols read it like a sentence:

> "And Samson went and caught 300 foxes, and took firebrands, and turned **tail to tail**, and put **a firebrand in the midst between two tails**." — Judges 15:4

The Hebrew is exact: *lappid echad bein-shnei ha-zenavot* — **one torch between two tails**. The [tail](/research/symbols/tail/) is already defined by Isaiah's own decode: "the prophet that **teacheth lies**, he is the tail" (Isa 9:15). Foxes joined tail to tail are false prophets joined lie to lie — one fire shared per pair. Scripture states the torch-and-liar figure directly: "as a mad man who casteth **firebrands**, arrows, and death, so is the man that **deceiveth** his neighbour" (Prov 26:18-19); "where no wood is, there the fire goeth out: so where there is no **talebearer**, the strife ceaseth" (Prov 26:20); "the tongue is a **fire**… and it is set on fire of hell" (Jas 3:6).

Loosed, the fire runs through the [field](/research/symbols/field/) — the world, sown with the word — and consumes the people's fruit at every stage:

> "He let them go into the **standing corn** of the Philistines, and burnt up both the **shocks**, and also the standing corn, with the **vineyards** and **olives**." — Judges 15:5

Stacked grain, ripening grain, the vine, the olive whose [oil](/research/symbols/oil/) should have been stored works lighting the Way — false prophets bound lie to lie set a whole people ablaze. The image carries its own verdict on the carriers: a fox with a burning tail does not survive its torch. Samson's act points the entire engine at Philistia — the judge sends the fox-fire into the enemy's field.

## Two Witnesses, One Tale

The pairing of Samson's foxes is courtroom arithmetic. The law establishes every matter "at the **mouth of two witnesses**" (Deut 19:15) — so the counterfeit case also arrives in pairs, two foxes sharing one torch, a false-witness pair sharing one tale. Scripture stages it twice.

> "Set **two men, sons of Belial**, before him, to bear witness against him, saying, Thou didst blaspheme God and the king." — 1 Kings 21:10

Jezebel writes the script; verse 13 delivers her words through two mouths verbatim; Naboth is stoned — and the prize is his **vineyard** (1 Kgs 21:16). Two foxes, one tale between them, one vineyard spoiled: Song 2:15 with names and an address. At the trial of Jesus the same machine misfires until it finds its pair: "many bare false witness against him, but their witness **agreed not together**" (Mark 14:56, 59) — tails not yet tied — until "at the last came **two false witnesses**" (Matt 26:60).

The mirror-image proves the geometry. True testimony sits at the mouth: "at the **mouth** of two witnesses shall the matter be established" (Deut 19:15); "I will make my words in thy **mouth fire**, and this people wood" (Jer 5:14); the two witnesses of Revelation — "**fire proceedeth out of their mouth**, and devoureth their enemies" (Rev 11:5). False testimony carries its fire at the other end, and Isaiah has already said which end that is: the liar is the **tail**. Two true witnesses, fire at the mouth; two false witnesses, one torch between two tails. Both pairs carry fire through the field — the difference is which end of the witness it burns from.

## The Foxes Have Holes

One saying remains, spoken to a scribe who volunteered to follow:

> "The foxes have **holes**, and the birds of the air have nests; but the Son of man hath **not where to lay his head**." — Matthew 8:20

The fox is *housed* in the land — the hole-dweller is at home precisely where things lie in ruins — and it stands paired with the fowls of the air, the seed-snatchers of the sower parable (Matt 13:4, 19). The squatters have dens and the heir has nowhere. The Hebrew Gospels render it *la-shu'alim chorim* — the foxes have holes — keeping the animal's own name-meaning, for *shu'al* is the hollow-creature, the burrower.

## The Name Itself

One wordplay is worth flagging, as wordplay and not as the definition. In the same oracle that defines the fox, Ezekiel plays its name: the prophets are *shu'alim* — foxes (13:4) — who pollute souls "for **handfuls** of barley," *b'sho'alei* se'orim (13:19), the same consonantal skeleton. The *shu'al* is the hollow-dweller; the *sho'al* is the hollow of a hand — the measure in which God holds the waters (Isa 40:12). The fox-prophets work for what a hollow hand can hold, and the Gospel saying preserves the resonance in translation: the foxes have holes.

## Competing Definitions Tested

- **"Crafty, sly"** — the common reading of Luke 13:32. Craft explains one verse by a Greek proverb and leaves the other 8 unexplained: cunning has nothing to do with ruins (Ezek 13:4), blossoming vines (Song 2:15), desolate Zion (Lam 5:18), broken walls (Neh 4:3), or the portion of the slain (Ps 63:10). The breach-dweller reading explains Herod *and* the rest — and it is the one Scripture states (Ezek 13:4-5).
- **Plain naturalism in Matt 8:20** — the saying works at the literal level, and this study does not force it. The fowls-pairing, the desolation frame, and the Hebrew name keep it fully inside the range without requiring it.
- **Judges 15 as mere stratagem** — at the plain level it still testifies: what foxes do to a land's produce is destroy it. The symbolic reading rests on terms Scripture defines elsewhere (tail, firebrand-deceiver, field), not on the fox alone.
- No occurrence casts the fox positively; none requires a second sense.

## Relationship to Other Symbols

No reciprocal opposite is assigned; the fox's opposite is a calling, not an image — the man who stands in the gap (Ezek 22:30). ## Conclusion and Status

**The breach-dweller: the false prophet or usurper at home in the ruins he was sent to repair.** Ezekiel states the equation and the reason in consecutive verses; the vineyard, the desolate mountain, the wall, the liars' portion, Samson's torches, and Herod's oracle all keep it; and the two-witness texts show why the foxes come in pairs. The definition is proposed for the glossary from [research-fox.md](https://github.com/timetestedbible/timetestedbible.github.io/blob/main/books/symbolic-language/research/research-fox.md) and awaits the author's approval; nothing in the occurrence register stands against it.
