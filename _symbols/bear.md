# Bear

**Crushing dominion that devours indiscriminately — empire as brute, consuming force.**

> *"And behold another beast, a second, like to a **bear**, and it raised up itself on one side, and it had three ribs in the mouth of it between the teeth of it: and they said thus unto it, Arise, devour much $[flesh]."* — Dan 7:5

---

## The Key Insight

Scripture pairs two predators again and again: the $lion and the bear. They appear together in David's proving ground (1Sam 17:34–37), in the proverb of wicked rulers (Prov 28:15), in the sequence of Daniel's empires (Dan 7:4–5), in God's judgment against His own people (Lam 3:10), in the inescapable judgment of Amos (Amos 5:19), and in the kingdom's restoration (Isa 11:7). This is not incidental. The two beasts represent two *modes* of dominating power — and the distinction between them reveals what the bear specifically contributes.

The $lion roars. It announces itself. It is sovereign, regal, recognizable — the king of beasts exercising authority through visible, declarative power. Its mouth speaks (Rev 13:2). Its presence intimidates (1Pet 5:8). It can be faced with boldness (Prov 28:1).

The bear does not roar to intimidate. It does not rule with majesty. It *ranges* (Prov 28:15). It *devours* (Dan 7:5). It *crushes* (Rev 13:2). Where the lion is the face of empire — the decree, the throne, the crown — the bear is the *weight* of empire: the slow, grinding, administrative machinery that tramples everything underfoot. You can stand before a lion. You cannot reason with a bear.

The Hebrew root confirms this: H1677 *dob* derives from H1680 *dabab* — "to move slowly, to glide." The bear is defined by **sluggish, unstoppable motion**. And the Greek G715 *arktos* carries the sense of "obstructing by ferocity" — blocking, immovable, overwhelming by sheer mass. Both roots converge on the same image: not speed, not cunning, not royal authority, but mass in relentless motion.

**Symbolizes:** Crushing, indiscriminate dominion — empire as consuming weight, devouring much flesh without discrimination

**Opposite:** The $lamb — gentle, yielding, given over. The bear is unstoppable and takes; the lamb is surrendered and gives.

**Defining verses:** Dan 7:5, Rev 13:2, 1Sam 17:34–37, Prov 28:15

**The surprise:** God Himself takes on the bear's image — twice (Lam 3:10, Hos 13:8). When He acts as a bear bereaved of cubs, He is not merely punishing; He is *exposing* — rending the covering of the heart to reveal what is inside.

**Connected:** $lion, $serpent, $dragon, $beast, $iron, $shepherd, $sheep

## Bear Across Scripture

### "Arise, Devour Much Flesh"

> *"And behold another beast, a second, like to a **bear**, and it raised up itself on one side, and it had three ribs in the mouth of it between the teeth of it: and they said thus unto it, Arise, devour much flesh."* — Dan 7:5

Daniel's vision unfolds four beasts rising from the $sea. The first is a $lion with eagle's $wings — traditionally Babylon, the empire of regal, visible authority. The second is the bear.

Notice what defines this bear. It is lopsided — raised on one side — suggesting an imbalanced dominion (traditionally read as Medo-Persia, where Persia was dominant over Media). It already has three ribs in its mouth: conquests already consumed, fueling further appetite. And it receives a command: *Arise, devour much flesh.*

The bear is not hunting. It is already eating. The ribs are not prey being pursued — they are nations already swallowed. The command is not to begin but to *continue*. The bear's defining characteristic is voracious, indiscriminate appetite — consumption as its mode of existence.

Where the $lion exercises authority by decree (its mouth speaks in Rev 13:2), the bear exercises dominion by consumption. It does not need to announce itself. It simply devours.

### The Bear's Feet — How Empire Moves

> *"And the $[beast] which I saw was like a leopard, and his feet were as the feet of a **bear**, and his mouth as the mouth of a $[lion]: and the $[dragon] gave him his power, and his seat, and great authority."* — Rev 13:2

In Revelation's composite $beast, the four empires of Daniel 7 merge into a single end-time system. Each beast contributes a specific feature: the leopard gives the body (agility, speed), the $lion gives the mouth (authoritative speech, decrees), and the bear gives the **feet**.

Feet are how something *moves and stands*. The beast's method of locomotion — how it advances, how it occupies territory, how it tramples — is bear-like. Slow, grinding, crushing everything underfoot. Not the flashy conquest of a leopard or the regal pronouncement of a lion, but the systematic, bureaucratic, administrative weight that grinds a population into submission.

The $iron of Daniel's fourth beast shares this quality: "forasmuch as iron breaketh in pieces and subdueth all things" (Dan 2:40). Iron crushes. Bear feet crush. The end-time system moves with the slow, immovable force of a bear — and the $iron teeth of Daniel 7:7 suggest the fourth beast inherits and intensifies the bear's consuming nature.

### The Shepherd's Proving Ground

> *"And David said unto Saul, Thy servant kept his father's $[sheep], and there came a $[lion], and a **bear**, and took a $[lamb] out of the flock: And I went out after him, and smote him, and delivered it out of his mouth."* — 1Sam 17:34–36

Before David faces Goliath — the champion of empire — he must first defeat two predators: the $lion and the bear. This is the $shepherd's training sequence. The two beasts represent the two modes of threat that every protector of God's $sheep must overcome.

Notice what the bear does: it takes a $lamb from the flock. Not the strongest ram, not the most visible target. The bear devours indiscriminately — whatever it can consume. This is what makes the bear's threat different from the lion's. The lion attacks with purpose; the bear simply takes what is nearest.

David's victory over both predators qualifies him to face the imperial giant. The pattern suggests that faithful shepherding requires overcoming *both* the royal, intimidating threat ($lion) and the crushing, consuming threat (bear) before confronting the full weight of empire itself.

### The Wicked Ruler: Lion and Bear

> *"As a roaring $[lion], and a ranging **bear**; so is a wicked ruler over the poor people."* — Prov 28:15

The proverb pairs the two predators as a single portrait of oppressive government. The lion *roars* — intimidation, threat, the public display of power. The bear *ranges* (H8264 *shaqaq* — to run greedily, to rush about, to roam in search of prey). The lion makes you afraid. The bear makes you unable to escape.

Together they describe the dual nature of tyranny: visible intimidation paired with relentless, ranging consumption. The wicked ruler announces his authority like a lion and then systematically devours like a bear — consuming the poor without discrimination. The poor have no defense against either mode, but the bear is perhaps worse: you can hide from a roar, but you cannot hide from something that ranges everywhere.

### Three Stages of Inescapable Judgment

> *"As if a man did flee from a $[lion], and a **bear** met him; or went into the house, and leaned his hand on the wall, and a $[serpent] bit him."* — Amos 5:19

Amos presents the bear as the *middle stage* in a three-part sequence of inescapable judgment. The progression is devastating:

1. **The $lion** — open, visible imperial threat. You flee.
2. **The bear** — crushing, consuming force. You escape the lion only to meet something worse.
3. **The $serpent** — hidden, personal deception. You reach the safety of your own house, lean on your own wall, and the bite comes from the place you trusted most.

The bear occupies the middle position between visible authority and hidden cunning. It is the brute force that remains when the royal threat is evaded — the grinding weight of oppression that cannot be outrun. And even if you survive the bear, the $serpent waits at home. There is no escape from God's judgment through any door.

This sequence mirrors the composite $beast of Rev 13:2: lion mouth, bear feet, and the $dragon (the old $serpent, Rev 12:9) behind it all. The three predators of Amos are the three components of the final system.

### The Bereaved Bear — Most Dangerous State

Three passages invoke the same image: a bear robbed of her cubs.

> *"Let a **bear** robbed of her whelps meet a man, rather than a fool in his folly."* — Prov 17:12

> *"They be chafed in their minds, as a **bear** robbed of her whelps in the field."* — 2Sam 17:8

> *"I will meet them as a **bear** that is bereaved of her whelps, and will rend the caul of their heart."* — Hos 13:8

The bereaved bear represents the *most dangerous* state of crushing power — the point where rage becomes measureless. In Proverbs, this fury is more *predictable* than a fool's folly; the fool is worse because his destruction has no logic. But the bear's fury, while terrible, follows from a comprehensible cause: something she possessed has been taken.

Hushai uses this image to describe David's seasoned warriors (2Sam 17:8) — men whose bitterness and ferocity in battle make them untouchable, like a mother bear whose cubs have been stolen. The comparison is not flattering. It is *fearsome*.

But it is God's own use of this image that carries the greatest weight.

### When God Becomes the Bear

> *"I will meet them as a **bear** that is bereaved of her whelps, and will rend the caul of their heart, and there will I devour them like a $[lion]."* — Hos 13:8

> *"He was unto me as a **bear** lying in wait, and as a $[lion] in secret places."* — Lam 3:10

In Hosea, God declares that He will meet Israel as a bereaved bear. Israel is the thing that belongs to Him — and Israel has been *taken* by its own idolatry. God's response is bear-like: He will rend the *caul* (the membrane covering) of their heart. The image is visceral. The bear does not merely strike — it tears away the protective covering to expose what is inside.

This is not random destruction. It is *exposure*. The bear strips away the exterior to reveal the hidden interior. When God judges as a bear, He exposes the heart's true condition.

In Lamentations, Jeremiah perceives God as a bear lying in wait — an ambush predator. This seems to contradict the bear's nature as a slow-moving creature, and that is precisely the point. Even the "slow" judgment arrives. The one you thought was lumbering and distant is suddenly upon you. God's patience (the bear's slowness) does not mean God's absence.

The lion+bear pairing holds in both passages: Hosea pairs the bereaved bear with the devouring lion. Lamentations pairs the waiting bear with the lion in secret places. God uses *both* modes of power in judgment — royal authority and crushing consumption. Neither can be evaded.

### The Bear Tamed — Kingdom Restored

> *"And the cow and the **bear** shall feed; their young ones shall lie down together: and the $[lion] shall eat straw like the ox."* — Isa 11:7

In the vision of the restored kingdom, the bear feeds alongside the cow. Predator and prey are reconciled. The bear's consuming nature — "devour much flesh" — is removed. It feeds on what the cow feeds on. Its young lie down with the cow's young.

This is not the bear's destruction but its *transformation*. The crushing, indiscriminate appetite that defines empire is replaced by peaceful coexistence. The same power that once ranged and devoured now rests beside its former prey. If the bear represents crushing dominion, then the bear feeding with the cow represents dominion stripped of its consuming impulse — power that no longer needs to devour.

### Mourning Like Bears

> *"We roar all like **bears**, and mourn sore like $[dove]s: we look for judgment, but there is none; for salvation, but it is far off from us."* — Isa 59:11

This is the only passage where the bear's sound is associated not with aggression but with frustrated longing. Israel roars like bears — powerful creatures unable to achieve what they seek. The parallel with the $dove's mourning is striking: the dove mourns from covenant grief; the bear roars from impotent power. Together they describe a people who are both devoted and forceful, yet neither devotion nor force can bring the justice they crave.

The bear's roar here is the sound of dominion that *cannot dominate* — crushing power rendered helpless before its own sin. Salvation is far off not because they lack strength but because their "iniquities have separated between you and your God" (Isa 59:2).

### Covenant Enforcement — The She-Bears of Bethel

> *"And he turned back, and looked on them, and cursed them in the name of the LORD. And there came forth two she **bears** out of the wood, and tare forty and two children of them."* — 2Ki 2:24

Two she-bears emerge from the wood and maul forty-two youths who mock Elisha — the newly anointed prophet who has just received Elijah's mantle. The incident is often read as disproportionate, but within the symbolic framework, every detail carries weight.

The number forty-two appears again in Rev 13:5 — the months the $beast is given to exercise authority. Two bears — like two witnesses — execute judgment. The youths mock the covenant prophet ("Go up, thou bald head" — dismissing the prophetic office). The bears are instruments of covenant enforcement: crushing power deployed against those who reject God's appointed authority.

The bears come from the *wood* — the wild, outside settled civilization — and they act with the indiscriminate force characteristic of the bear. They do not select targets. They maul forty-two.

## Patterns

1. **Lion + Bear = the complete predator pair.** Every context that mentions one eventually mentions the other: 1Sam 17:34–37, Prov 28:15, Dan 7:4–5, Lam 3:10, Hos 13:8, Amos 5:19, Isa 11:7, Rev 13:2. The lion represents royal, declarative authority; the bear represents crushing, consuming force. Together they describe the full spectrum of oppressive power.

2. **Lion → Bear → Serpent = the full judgment sequence.** Amos 5:19 gives the three stages: visible authority, crushing force, hidden deception. All three merge in the composite $beast of Rev 13:2. The bear is always the *middle* — between the open display of power and the concealed strike.

3. **The bear devours indiscriminately.** "Devour much flesh" (Dan 7:5), ranges over the poor (Prov 28:15), mauls forty-two (2Ki 2:24), takes a lamb from the flock (1Sam 17:34). The bear does not select; it consumes whatever it encounters. This distinguishes it from the lion, which targets with purpose, and the $serpent, which deceives with precision.

4. **The bereaved bear = the most dangerous state.** 2Sam 17:8, Prov 17:12, Hos 13:8. When the bear loses what it considers its own, its fury becomes measureless. God Himself uses this image — He is the one bereaved of Israel, and His response is to rend the caul of their heart.

5. **The bear's slowness is its defining quality.** H1677 *dob* from H1680 *dabab* = to move slowly, to glide. The bear is not fast, not cunning, not regal. It is *massive, slow, and unstoppable*. This is the nature of grinding bureaucratic empire: it does not strike with lightning but advances with inevitable, crushing weight.

6. **God uses the bear for exposure.** Hos 13:8 — rend the caul of their heart. The bear strips away protective covering. The bear's judgment is not merely destructive; it is *revealing*. What was hidden inside the heart is torn open.

## Connections

- **$lion** — The bear's constant partner. Lion = sovereign authority exercised through visible, declarative power. Bear = crushing dominion exercised through consuming force. The same pairing appears in David's proving ground, in the proverb of wicked rulers, in Daniel's empires, in Hosea and Lamentations. The lion speaks; the bear tramples.

- **$serpent** — The third predator in Amos 5:19. Lion → Bear → Serpent traces the full spectrum from open authority to crushing force to hidden deception. All three converge in the composite $beast (Rev 13:2) powered by the $dragon (the old serpent, Rev 12:9).

- **$dragon** — The $dragon gives the $beast its power (Rev 13:2). The bear's feet are the method by which the dragon-empowered system *moves* — the slow, crushing advance of empire animated by spiritual authority behind the scenes.

- **$beast** — The composite beast of Rev 13:2 absorbs the bear, taking its feet. Daniel's four separate beast-empires compress into a single system. The bear is not a standalone empire but a *quality* that persists in every oppressive system: the grinding, consuming weight.

- **$iron** — Dan 7:7's fourth beast has $iron teeth, echoing the bear's consuming mouth (Dan 7:5). Iron crushes and subdues (Dan 2:40); bear feet crush and trample (Rev 13:2). Iron is the bear's material — the substance of slow, unyielding, grinding force.

- **$shepherd** — David the $shepherd slew the bear before facing Goliath (1Sam 17:34–37). The shepherd must overcome the bear's indiscriminate consumption to protect the flock. Faithful shepherding requires defeating both modes of predation — royal (lion) and crushing (bear).

- **$sheep / $lamb** — The bear takes the $lamb from the flock (1Sam 17:34). The bear ranges over the poor (Prov 28:15). The bear's prey is the vulnerable, the covenant people, the humble. The $lamb is the bear's opposite: where the bear takes by force, the lamb gives by surrender.
