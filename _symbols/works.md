# Works (good works, evil works)

## Summary

**A work is a deed, not a synonym for obedience. It becomes obedience when it fulfills a legitimate command and disobedience when it conflicts with God's command; context separately identifies wages, duty, or faith made visible.**

**A work is a deed, not a synonym for obedience. It becomes obedience when it fulfills a legitimate command and disobedience when it conflicts with God's command; context separately identifies wages, duty, or faith made visible.**

### Webster's Definition

Webster's 1913 entry for **work** begins: “Exertion of strength or faculties; physical or intellectual effort directed to an end; industrial activity; toil; employment; sometimes, specifically, physically labor.”

These definitions may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

Works are deeds, but not inherently obedience. A deed counts as obedience or disobedience according to its relation to a legitimate divine command; even complete obedience remains duty rather than wage-earning merit.

> *“And thou shalt teach them ordinances and laws, and shalt shew them the way wherein they must walk, and the work that they must do.”* — Exodus 18:20

> *“The works of his hands are verity and judgment; all his commandments are sure.”* — Psalms 111:7

> *“For the statutes of Omri are kept, and all the works of the house of Ahab, and ye walk in their counsels; that I should make thee a desolation, and the inhabitants thereof an hissing: therefore ye shall bear the reproach of my people.”* — Micah 6:16

> *“But which of you, having a servant plowing or feeding cattle, will say unto him by and by, when he is come from the field, Go and sit down to meat?”* — Luke 17:7

> *“And will not rather say unto him, Make ready wherewith I may sup, and gird thyself, and serve me, till I have eaten and drunken; and afterward thou shalt eat and drink?”* — Luke 17:8

> *“Doth he thank that servant because he did the things that were commanded him? I trow not.”* — Luke 17:9

> *“So likewise ye, when ye shall have done all those things which are commanded you, say, We are unprofitable servants: we have done that which was our duty to do.”* — Luke 17:10

> *“Now to him that worketh is the reward not reckoned of grace, but of debt.”* — Romans 4:4

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Competing Definitions Tested

A common reading is: **human obedience and effort set against faith in Paul's salvation texts, with good works following salvation as evidence.**

- The blind consensus already defines works as deeds rather than as a strict synonym for obedience and explicitly recognizes disputes about merit. Thus the book's distinction could be viewed as an elaboration already compatible with consensus rather than a substantial refinement; its real target is chiefly the common-view note's broad equation of Pauline works with obedient human effort.

- One could argue this is merely a MATCH with terminological precision: all consensus responses already define works as deeds, none formally asserts that works and obedience are strictly synonymous, and the Luke 17 no-wage point is compatible with the mainstream Protestant evidential view; on this reading the book adds a scholastic distinction without materially altering extension. Against this, the book explicitly frames its distinction against what is 'commonly taught' about Paul's works vocabulary, indicating an intended substantive boundary correction rather than mere restatement.

- Consensus already defines works primarily as deeds rather than as a strict synonym for obedience, so the book's distinction may be only a minor clarifying nuance rather than a material change of boundary.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
