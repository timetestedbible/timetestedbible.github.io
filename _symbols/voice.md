# Voice

## Approved Definition

**The words of the one who speaks; the Shepherd's voice is His words.**

The fuller book and notebook trail is available in [Love, Knowing, Faith & Belief](/books/symbolic-language/knowing-faith-love-and-belief/).

## Definition Layers

### Bible symbolic sense

**The words of the one who speaks; the Shepherd's voice is His words.**

### Bible literal sense

*Not yet stated separately. This is a research gap: the literal sense must be derived from ordinary biblical uses, not copied from Webster.*

### Webster's English sense

Webster's 1913 entry for **voice** begins: “Sound uttered by the mouth, especially that uttered by human beings in speech or song; sound thus uttered considered as possessing some special quality or character; as, the human voice; a pleasant voice; a low voice.”

These layers may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Short Case

The words of the one who speaks; the Shepherd's voice is His words.

> *“My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me:”* — John 10:27

The definition stays with the relationship these passages establish. Literal appearances preserve the image; they do not require every occurrence to be symbolic.

## Dictionary Comparison

Webster's 1913 entry for **voice** begins:

> “Voice, n. Etym: [OE. vois, voys, OF. vois, voiz, F. voix, L. vox, vocis, akin to Gr. vac to say, to speak, G. erwähnen to mention. Cf. Advocate, Advowson, Avouch, Convoke, Epic, Vocal, Vouch, Vowel.] 1. Sound uttered by the mouth, especially that uttered by human beings in speech or song; sound thus uttered considered as possessing some special quality or character; as, the human voice; a pleasant voice; a low voice. He with a manly voice saith his message. Chaucer. Her voice was ever soft, Gentle, and low; an excellent thing in woman. Shak. Thy voice is music. Shak. Join thy voice unto the angel choir. Milton.”

The dictionary supplies the ordinary sense. The approved definition above is narrower or figurative only where Scripture's own cross-references require that transfer.

## Corpus and Method

This study checks the glossary's defining citations first, then compares them with every KJV verse that prints *voice*. The English-form sweep found **469 verses**. It is exhaustive for those printed forms, but it is not a substitute for a Strong's-number sweep where several Hebrew or Greek words share one English translation.

## Evidence by Sense

The approved entry currently states one controlling sense:

1. **Approved core:** The words of the one who speaks; the Shepherd's voice is His words.
2. **Defining witnesses:** John 10:27
3. **Boundary:** the sense applies where the passage activates the same relationship; ordinary literal uses remain literal.

## Competing Definitions Tested

- The book’s statement may be read as merely a concise paraphrase of the consensus: hearing the Shepherd’s voice naturally means receiving His words. On that reading, the distinction between communication and its verbal content could be too slight to justify REFINED. However, John 10:27 alone does not explicitly equate voice with words, and the book’s categorical generic definition is narrower in sense yet broader in speaker coverage than the consensus.

- One could argue the book REFINES by stripping the consensus's core down from 'divine self-revelation, presence, and authority' to a bare linguistic identification (voice = words), potentially excluding the theophanic-power and presence dimensions (Psalm 29, Sinai) that the consensus treats as central; and John 10:27 alone shows sheep hearing and following the voice without explicitly equating voice with 'words,' so the printed citation only partially establishes the definition.

- Reducing voice to 'words' flattens the consensus multi-sense motif (theophanic power and non-verbal senses) and the lone John 10:27 citation only partially underwrites the general equation.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.
