# Faith

## Summary

**Trusting faithfulness — reliance on God's unseen promise held steady and brought to life through obedience.**
> *"Even so **faith**, if it hath not works, is dead, being alone."* — Jas 2:17

---

## Definitions

### Symbolic Definition

*No separate symbolic sense is asserted. This entry recovers a biblical word meaning rather than interpreting an image.*

### Literal Biblical Definition

**Trusting faithfulness — reliance on God's unseen promise held steady and brought to life through obedience.**

### Webster's Definition

Webster's 1913 entry for **faith** begins: “Belief; the assent of the mind to the truth of what is declared by another, resting solely and implicitly on his authority and veracity; reliance on testimony.”

These definitions may agree, but they are independent evidence. A biblical passage may use the term literally, symbolically, or both at the same time; the literal properties remain part of the logic when Scripture uses the object as a symbol.

## The Key Insight

Hebrews 3:18–19 contains a parallelism that most readers pass over:

> *"And to whom sware he that they should not enter into his rest, but to them that **believed not**? So we see that they could not enter in because of **unbelief**."* — Heb 3:18–19

Read that alongside the verse that sets it up:

> *"And to whom sware he that they should not enter into his rest, but to them that **obeyed not**?"* — Heb 3:18 (ASV, rendering G544 *apeitheo*)

The Greek word translated "believed not" in the KJV (G544 *apeitheo*) literally means "to disobey, to be non-compliant." And the noun "unbelief" (G570 *apistia*) is placed in synonymous parallelism with disobedience. The writer of Hebrews treats these as interchangeable. Israel at Kadesh-barnea did not merely *doubt* the report — they refused to *go in*. Their unbelief was not an intellectual failure. It was a failure to act.

This equation — unbelief = disobedience — is the key that unlocks every occurrence of "faith" in Scripture. If the opposite of faith is disobedience, then faith itself is obedience. Not bare obedience detached from trust, and not bare trust detached from action — but the single, unified response of hearing God's word and *doing it*.

A secular reader using only a dictionary would define faith as "belief" or "confidence." Scripture defines it as the trusting action that *moves*.

**Symbolizes:** Trusting obedience — hearing God's word and acting on it

**Opposite:** Unbelief / disobedience — refusing to act on what God has spoken (Heb 3:18–19)

**Defining verses:** Heb 11:1, Jas 2:17–26, Rom 1:5, Hab 2:4, Heb 3:18–19

**The surprise:** The Hebrew behind "the just shall live by his faith" (Hab 2:4) is H530 *emunah* — which means faithfulness, steadfastness, firmness. Its root H539 *aman* means "to be firm, to support, to be faithful." In Hebrew thought, faith is not a feeling of confidence. It is the quality of *being steadfastly reliable* — acting consistently on what God has declared.

**Connected:** $truth, $light, $fruit, $bread

## Faith Across Scripture

### The Hebrew Root — Firmness, Not Feeling

The verse most quoted in the New Testament — quoted by Paul in Rom 1:17, Gal 3:11, and by the writer of Hebrews in Heb 10:38 — originates in Habakkuk:

> *"Behold, his soul which is lifted up is not upright in him: but the just shall live by his **faith**."* — Hab 2:4

The word is H530 *emunah*. It does not mean "belief" in the modern English sense. Its semantic range centers on **faithfulness, firmness, steadfastness**. The root H539 *aman* means to be firm, to support, to be reliable — the same root from which we get "Amen" (so be it, it is firm).

When Moses' hands were steady (*emunah*) until sunset (Exod 17:12), the word is the same. His hands were not "believing" — they were *steadfast*, held firm, not wavering. When Habakkuk says the just shall live by *emunah*, the idea is: the righteous person lives by steadfast, faithful action — firm obedience to God's word when the circumstances give every reason to quit.

This matters because the three New Testament quotations of Hab 2:4 carry this Hebrew freight with them. Paul is not introducing a new concept of "belief-only" faith. He is drawing on the Hebrew understanding: the righteous person lives by firm, trusting obedience.

### The Body Without the Spirit

James 2 provides the most sustained argument in Scripture about the nature of faith. It is structured as a logical chain, and each link tightens the same conclusion:

> *"What doth it profit, my brethren, though a man say he hath **faith**, and have not works? can **faith** save him?"* — Jas 2:14

> *"Even so **faith**, if it hath not works, is dead, being alone."* — Jas 2:17

> *"But wilt thou know, O vain man, that **faith** without works is dead?"* — Jas 2:20

> *"Seest thou how **faith** wrought with his works, and by works was **faith** made perfect?"* — Jas 2:22

> *"For as the body without the spirit is dead, so **faith** without works is dead also."* — Jas 2:26

The analogy in verse 26 is decisive. A body without a spirit is a corpse — it has the form of a person but no life. Faith without works is likewise a corpse: it has the form of trust but no life. The spirit does not *add* to the body from outside; it is what makes the body *alive*. Works do not *add* to faith from outside; they are what make faith *alive*.

James even addresses the counterargument before it can form:

> *"Thou believest that there is one God; thou doest well: the devils also believe, and tremble."* — Jas 2:19

Demons possess perfect theological information. They know God is one. They know it so well that they tremble. But no one would say demons have faith. Mental assent — even terrified mental assent — is not what Scripture means by the word. Faith that does not act is the same species as demonic belief: correct information with no covenant response.

### Obedience *of* Faith — Paul's Bookends

Paul frames the entire book of Romans — his most extended treatment of faith — between two nearly identical phrases:

> *"By whom we have received grace and apostleship, for **obedience to the faith** among all nations, for his name."* — Rom 1:5

> *"But now is made manifest, and by the scriptures of the prophets, according to the commandment of the everlasting God, made known to all nations for the **obedience of faith**."* — Rom 16:26

The letter opens with "obedience to the faith" and closes with "obedience of faith." This is not accidental. The Greek genitive construction (*hupakoe pisteos*) binds obedience and faith together so tightly that translators struggle with it — is it obedience *that comes from* faith? Obedience *that is* faith? The ambiguity is the point. Paul does not separate the two because, in his understanding, they are inseparable. Faith *is* obedient response to God's word. Obedience *is* the expression of trust. The entire argument of Romans — justification, sanctification, the law, the Spirit — sits between these two bookends.

### "Thy Faith Hath Made Thee Whole"

Jesus uses this phrase repeatedly, and in every case, the person addressed had *done something*:

> *"And he said unto her, Daughter, thy **faith** hath made thee whole; go in peace."* — Mk 5:34 (also Mat 9:22, Lk 8:48)

The woman with the issue of blood did not sit at home and believe. She pressed through a crowd, reached out, and touched the hem of His garment. Jesus calls that *faith*. The blind men cried out publicly and followed Him (Mat 9:27–29). The leper came to Jesus and knelt (Mk 1:40–42). The centurion came personally or sent messengers (Mat 8:5–10). In each case, belief became motion. Trust became action. And Jesus named that action *faith*.

Not one of these people is commended for the quality of their theological understanding. They are commended for *acting on what they believed* — for trusting obedience that moved their bodies toward the one they trusted.

### What Faith Is — Hebrews 11:1

> *"Now **faith** is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen."* — Heb 11:1

The word "substance" is G5287 *hypostasis* — a standing-under, a foundation, assurance. The word "evidence" is G1650 *elegchos* — proof, conviction, the kind of evidence that persuades. Faith is the firm ground under what we hope for and the proof of what we cannot yet see.

But Hebrews 11:1 is not a complete definition in isolation. It is a *chapter heading*. What follows — the entire "Hall of Faith" — is a catalog of people who *acted*: Abel offered, Noah prepared an ark, Abraham went out, Moses refused, Rahab received the spies. Every illustration in Hebrews 11 is an action verb. The chapter defines faith not by restating verse 1 in different words, but by showing what faith *looks like* — and it always looks like obedience.

The inverse confirms this. Hebrews 3–4 shows what the *absence* of faith looks like — Israel at Kadesh-barnea refusing to enter the land:

> *"So we see that they could not enter in because of **unbelief**."* — Heb 3:19

> *"They to whom it was first preached entered not in because of **unbelief**."* — Heb 4:6 (rendering G543 *apeitheia* — disobedience)

Faith enters. Unbelief stays put. Hebrews defines faith by its movement and unbelief by its refusal to move.

### Grace Through Faith — Ephesians 2:8

> *"For by grace are ye saved through **faith**; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God."* — Eph 2:8

This verse is often read as though faith is the opposite of obedience — as though grace and works are enemies and faith sides with grace against works. But the verse says *through* faith, not *instead of* obedience. Grace is the source; faith is the channel. And what flows through that channel, as James and Hebrews and Romans all demonstrate, is trusting action.

Paul himself clarifies in the very next verse what he is excluding: "not of works, lest any man should boast" (Eph 2:9). The works excluded are those performed to *earn* — to generate a debt God must repay. But Eph 2:10 immediately adds: "For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus *unto good works*, which God hath before ordained that we should *walk in them*." The trajectory moves from grace → through faith → into obedient action. Faith is not the absence of works but the *trust-filled doing* of them.

## The Chain: From God's Mouth to Visible Works

The co-occurring symbols trace a chain that illuminates how faith functions:

God speaks — His word is $bread, the covenant terms that sustain life (Deut 8:3: "man doth not live by bread only, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of the LORD"). That word constitutes $truth — God's commandments, His standard (John 17:17: "Thy word is truth"; Ps 119:142: "Thy law is the truth").

Faith is the human response: hearing that $truth and acting on it. The trusting obedience that receives God's $bread and walks according to His $truth.

That obedience produces $fruit — visible works and deeds (Gal 5:22, Mat 7:16–20). And those $fruit become $light — the visible example of commandment-keeping that others can see (Mat 5:16: "Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works").

The chain: **$bread (God speaks) → $truth (His standard) → faith (trusting obedience) → $fruit (works) → $light (visible example).**

When any link is missing, the chain breaks. Remove faith — hearing without obeying — and there is no $fruit, no $light. Remove $truth — obedience without a standard — and there is nothing to obey. Remove $bread — a famine of God's word (Amos 8:11) — and there is no $truth to hear. The entire chain depends on God initiating and the human responding with trusting obedience.

## Patterns

1. **Faith's opposite is always disobedience, not doubt.** Heb 3:18–19 equates unbelief with refusal to act. Heb 4:6 uses G543 *apeitheia* (disobedience) as a synonym for unbelief. Israel's failure was not intellectual uncertainty — they had seen the plagues, the Red Sea, the pillar of fire. They *refused to go in*.

2. **Every "hero of faith" performs a verb.** Abel offered. Enoch walked. Noah prepared. Abraham departed. Sarah conceived. Moses refused, chose, forsook, kept. Rahab received. The Hebrews 11 catalog contains no example of faith-as-passive-belief.

3. **Faith is completed by works, not replaced by them.** James 2:22 — "faith wrought with his works, and by works was faith made perfect (G5048 *teleioo* — brought to completion)." Works do not compete with faith. They bring it to its telos, its intended end.

4. **The Hebrew root is firmness.** H530 *emunah* = steadfastness. H539 *aman* = to be firm, reliable. The biblical concept of faith is closer to "firm reliability of action" than to "subjective inner confidence."

5. **Jesus identifies faith by observing action.** "Thy faith hath made thee whole" is never said to someone who merely believed internally. It is always spoken to someone who *did something* — touched, came, cried out, sent, returned.

6. **Demons believe and tremble.** James 2:19 eliminates mental assent as sufficient. Correct theological information without covenant obedience is not faith — it is the religion of demons.

7. **Grace and obedience are not opposed.** Eph 2:8–10 moves from grace → through faith → unto good works. Rom 1:5 and 16:26 frame all of Romans as being *about* the obedience of faith. The supposed tension between Paul and James dissolves when faith is understood as trusting obedience rather than intellectual assent.

## Relationship to Other Symbols
- **$truth** — $truth is God's commandment, His standard (Ps 119:142, Jn 17:17). Faith is the *response* to that standard — hearing God's commandment and obeying it. You cannot have faith without $truth to act upon. "He that doeth truth cometh to the light" (Jn 3:21) — doing $truth is what faith looks like in practice.

- **$bread** — $bread is the Word of God, the covenant terms that sustain life (Deut 8:3, Jn 6:35). Faith is what happens when a person receives that $bread and *lives by it*. Israel received manna (the Torah-test bread, Exod 16:4) and was expected to respond with obedience — "whether they will walk in my law, or no." Receiving the $bread without walking in it is unbelief.

- **$light** — $light is the visible example of commandment-keeping (Mat 5:14–16). Faith produces $light: trusting obedience generates visible works that others can see. "Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works" — those good works are the $fruit of faith made visible.

- **$fruit** — $fruit is works and deeds (Gal 5:19–22, Mat 7:16–20). Faith *produces* $fruit; without faith, there is no $fruit. "Faith without works is dead" (Jas 2:17) — the absence of $fruit proves the absence of living faith. "Bring forth fruits meet for repentance" (Mat 3:8) — the $fruit *is* the evidence of the internal change.

## For Further Study

- **$truth** — If faith is the obedient response, $truth is what we respond *to*. How does Scripture define God's standard, and what does it mean to "do" truth?
- **$bread** — The Word that faith receives. How does the manna-test in Exodus 16 establish the pattern of God speaking and people obeying — or refusing?
- **$light** — The visible result of faith. How does obedience become example, and why does Jesus connect the command to "let your light shine" directly to the law and the prophets?
- **$fruit** — The works that faith produces. How does Scripture distinguish living fruit from dead works, and what determines whether a tree bears or is barren?

## Dictionary Comparison

Webster's 1913 entry for **faith** begins:

> “Faith, n. Etym: [OE. feith, fayth, fay, OF. feid, feit, fei, F. foi, fr. L. fides; akin to fidere to trust, Gr. th is perhaps due to the influence of such words as truth, health, wealth. See Bid, Bide, and cf. Confide, Defy, Fealty.] 1. Belief; the assent of the mind to the truth of what is declared by another, resting solely and implicitly on his authority and veracity; reliance on testimony.”

The dictionary supplies the ordinary sense. The definition above is narrower or figurative only where Scripture's own cross-references require that transfer.

## Competing Definitions Tested

A common reading is: **confident trust in God and His promises — the sole instrument of justification.**

- The formulation may blur the distinction between faith itself and the works that evidence or complete it, whereas many mainstream Protestant accounts define justifying faith as trust alone and treat obedience strictly as its necessary fruit. The entry partly answers this by explicitly denying that works purchase the promise.

- One could argue the book edges toward DIVERGENT because the common-view note singles out 'the sole instrument of justification' and the entry then elevates obedience and works into the very definition of faith — a move that Reformation-minded readers might see as blurring the faith/works distinction rather than merely refining it, especially since the book makes 'faithfulness held through obedience' definitional rather than consequential.

- Fusing ‘trust’ and ‘faithfulness’ plus foregrounding works-maturation could be read as blurring the sharp Protestant ‘faith alone’ emphasis that the consensus treats as primary, even though the book explicitly denies purchase.



These tests identify the boundary the definition must explain. A shared phrase or nearby theme is not enough by itself; the cited passages must establish the proposed identity and account for the countertexts without changing senses whenever the reading becomes difficult.

## Conclusion

**Trusting faithfulness — reliance on God's unseen promise held steady and brought to life through obedience.**

This is the glossary conclusion; broader applications in the study remain subordinate to the defining texts and countertexts.
