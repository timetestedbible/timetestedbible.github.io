# Extra Chapter 06: The Pharaoh of the Exodus

## Introduction

Among the royal mummies that Egypt has kept for three and a half thousand years lies the man who stood before Moses, hardened his heart, buried his firstborn, and lost his chariotry in the sea. Which one is he? The question has been argued for a century, and most of the argument has run backward: choose a pharaoh who left impressive ruins, then bend the Scriptures to reach his reign. This chapter runs the other way. Scripture fixes the year of the Exodus. Egypt's own records, read at that year, name the king: **Amenhotep II**, son of Thutmose III.

The chapter proceeds in five steps. First, the date, from Scripture alone. Second, the Egyptian king list at that date, and why Scripture settles the one open question in it. Third, four requirements the Bible places on the Pharaoh of the oppression and the Pharaoh of the Exodus, tested against the record. Fourth, a hostile witness, the Egyptian priest Manetho, who preserved the king's name. Fifth, why the popular alternative, Ramesses II, fails.

---

## The Date Comes from Scripture

### 1 Kings 6:1

The plainest chronological statement in the Bible about the Exodus is the anchor for everything that follows:

> "And it came to pass in the **four hundred and eightieth year** after the children of Israel were come out of the land of Egypt, in the fourth year of Solomon's reign over Israel, in the month Zif, which is the second month, that he began to build the house of the LORD." (1 Kings 6:1)

The fourth year of Solomon is fixed at 967/966 BC by the Assyrian synchronisms and the regnal data of Kings, as laid out in [chapter 12, *32 AD Resurrection*](/reader/timetested/12_32_AD_Resurrection). The founding of the temple in the spring of 966 BC fell in the four hundred and eightieth year counted from the Exodus. Counting the year of the departure as the first, the Exodus falls in the spring of **1446 BC**, on the fifteenth day of the first month.

### Judges 11:26

Jephthah gives an independent check. Answering the king of Ammon's claim to the land east of the Jordan, he says:

> "While Israel dwelt in Heshbon and her towns, and in Aroer and her towns, and in all the cities that be along by the coasts of Arnon, **three hundred years**? why therefore did ye not recover them within that time?" (Judges 11:26)

Heshbon was taken from Sihon in the fortieth year after the Exodus, just before the Jordan crossing of 1406 BC. Three hundred years of Israelite occupation bring Jephthah to about 1106 BC, in the period of the judges, a generation before Samuel, exactly where the book of Judges places him. Try the same arithmetic with the late date. An Exodus under Ramesses II in about 1260 BC puts the taking of Heshbon at 1220 BC and Jephthah's speech at 920 BC, in the reign of Rehoboam, a full century after the judges had given way to kings. The late date does not merely strain Judges 11:26. It makes the verse impossible.

Two independent witnesses, five hundred years apart in the text, agree. The Exodus was in 1446 BC.

## Reading Egypt's Record at 1446 BC

### Two frameworks, twenty-five years apart

Egyptologists reconstruct the 18th Dynasty from king lists, from the reign lengths on monuments and papyri, from the letters exchanged with Babylon and Assyria, and from a handful of astronomical notices. The notices are the crux. A papyrus from the ninth year of Amenhotep I records the rising of Sirius (the Egyptian *Sopdet*) against the civil calendar. Where that observation was made, at Thebes in the south or at Memphis in the north, moves the whole dynasty by about twenty-five years. Two new-moon dates from the campaigns of Thutmose III must then be fitted to the same choice. The result is two frameworks:

| Framework | Thutmose III | Amenhotep II | Thutmose IV |
|---|---|---|---|
| Higher | 1504–1450 BC | 1453–1419 BC | 1419–1410 BC |
| Lower | 1479–1425 BC | 1427–1401 BC | 1401–1391 BC |

Reign lengths are the same in both. Only the starting point differs. Most reference works print the lower framework. Radiocarbon series published in 2010 (Bronk Ramsey and others, *Science* 328) fall on the early side, consistent with the higher. Neither framework is fixed by Egypt alone; the evidence permits both.

Scripture does not permit both. The requirements set out in the next section can be met only in the higher framework, in which Thutmose III dies four years before the Exodus. This is the same method the book uses for Herod in [chapter 13](/reader/timetested/13_Herod_the_Great): where the secular record leaves a choice open, the Bible's fixed points make it.

### The 18th Dynasty on the higher framework

| Pharaoh | Reign | Length | Place in the Exodus account |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ahmose I | c. 1570–1546 BC | 25 years | Expelled the Hyksos; founder of the dynasty |
| Amenhotep I | c. 1551–1524 BC | 21 years | |

| Thutmose II | c. 1518–1504 BC | 14 years | |
| Hatshepsut | c. 1504–1483 BC | 21 years | Coregent during her nephew's minority |

| **Amenhotep II** | **c. 1453–1419 BC** | **34 years** | **Pharaoh of the Exodus; 1446 BC falls in his seventh year of sole rule** |
| **Thutmose IV** | **c. 1419–1410 BC** | **9 years** | **Succeeded although not the firstborn** |

Amenhotep II was crowned as coregent in the last years of his father and ruled alone from 1450 BC. Moses was born in 1526 BC, in the reign of Thutmose I or II; fled Egypt in 1486 BC, when Thutmose III had held the throne eighteen years; and returned in 1446 BC, when Thutmose III had been dead four years.

## Four Requirements the Bible Sets

### First: the king Moses fled reigned through his exile and died before his return

> "Now when Pharaoh heard this thing, he sought to slay Moses. But Moses fled from the face of Pharaoh, and dwelt in the land of Midian." (Exodus 2:15)
>
> "And it came to pass in process of time, that **the king of Egypt died**: and the children of Israel sighed by reason of the bondage." (Exodus 2:23)
>
> "And the LORD said unto Moses in Midian, Go, return into Egypt: for **all the men are dead which sought thy life**." (Exodus 4:19)

Stephen supplies the lengths. Moses was forty when he fled (Acts 7:23) and forty years passed in Midian before the bush burned (Acts 7:30); he was eighty when he stood before Pharaoh (Exodus 7:7). One king sought his life at his flight, and the death of that king, after "process of time," is what opened the way home. The Pharaoh of the oppression therefore held the throne in 1486 BC and died in the years just before 1446 BC. That is a reign of forty years or more, ending on the eve of the Exodus.

Thutmose III meets it exactly. He reigned fifty-four years, the longest reign of the dynasty, from 1504 to 1450 BC. He was king when Moses fled, and he died four years before Moses returned. The lower framework fails here: with Thutmose III on the throne until 1425 BC, the king Moses fled would have outlived the Exodus by twenty-one years, and Exodus 2:23 would have no one to die.

### Second: Pharaoh lost his army, not his life

> "And the waters returned, and covered the chariots, and the horsemen, and all the host of Pharaoh that came into the sea after them; there remained not so much as one of them." (Exodus 14:28)

The text destroys the host that entered the sea; it does not record the king among them, and Psalm 136:15, "overthrew Pharaoh and his host in the Red sea," uses a word (*na'ar*, to shake off) that describes the rout, not a death certificate. The Exodus Pharaoh may well have gone home. If he did, Egypt's record should show a king who lost his chariotry and his labor force in one season.

It does. In his ninth year, the year after the Exodus on this reckoning, Amenhotep II marched into Canaan on a campaign whose own stele at Memphis tallies the captives brought back at **89,600** men, women and children (translated in Pritchard, *Ancient Near Eastern Texts*, 245–247). No other campaign of the dynasty took prisoners on that scale, and Amenhotep II never campaigned in Asia again. A king who had just lost the slave population of the eastern Delta had reason to replace it.

### Third: Pharaoh's firstborn died, so the heir who followed him was not his firstborn

> "And all the firstborn in the land of Egypt shall die, **from the firstborn of Pharaoh that sitteth upon his throne**, even unto the firstborn of the maidservant that is behind the mill." (Exodus 11:5)
>
> "And it came to pass, that at midnight the LORD smote all the firstborn in the land of Egypt, from the firstborn of Pharaoh that sat on his throne unto the firstborn of the captive that was in the dungeon." (Exodus 12:29)

The Exodus Pharaoh's successor cannot have been his firstborn son. Amenhotep II was succeeded by **Thutmose IV**, who was not. A son named Amenhotep, styled king's eldest son and priest of Ptah at Memphis, appears in the records of the reign and is then heard of no more. Another son, Webensenu, died young and was buried in his father's own tomb, where his canopic jars were found. The heir who lived was a younger prince.

Thutmose IV knew it. Between the paws of the Great Sphinx he set up a stele, in his first year, telling how as a prince he fell asleep in the Sphinx's shadow and the god promised him the kingship. Kings who are born to the throne do not need a dream to explain how they got it. The Dream Stele is the apology of a younger son for a succession nobody expected, which is what the tenth plague leaves behind.

### Fourth: Israel built for Pharaoh in the eastern Delta, and that royal presence ended in his reign

> "Therefore they did set over them taskmasters to afflict them with their burdens. And they built for Pharaoh treasure cities, Pithom and Raamses." (Exodus 1:11)
>
> "And the children of Israel journeyed from Rameses to Succoth." (Exodus 12:37)

The land of Rameses, where Joseph settled his brothers (Genesis 47:11) and from which Israel departed, is the eastern Delta around Avaris, modern Tell el-Dab'a. The Austrian excavations under Manfred Bietak uncovered there an 18th Dynasty palace precinct, the naval and military base the Egyptians called Peru-nefer, occupied from Ahmose through Thutmose III and Amenhotep II, and then abandoned (Bietak, "Peru-nefer: The Principal New Kingdom Naval Base," *Egyptian Archaeology* 34, 2009). The royal establishment in the very district Israel labored in went dark in the reign of Amenhotep II. The excavator himself places the Exodus elsewhere; his spade, not his conclusion, is the witness.

## A Hostile Witness: Manetho

Manetho was an Egyptian priest of Heliopolis who wrote a history of his country in Greek in the third century BC, working from temple records. His book is lost. It survives in the quotations of later writers, the earliest and fullest being Josephus, who says of him:

> "I shall set down his very words, as if I were to bring the very man himself into a court for a witness." (Josephus, *Against Apion* 1.14)

Josephus quotes Manetho twice on Israel. The first passage tells of shepherd kings who ruled Egypt, were driven out, and built a city in Judea they called Jerusalem; Josephus takes them for Israel's ancestors, but Manetho sets that departure under a king Tethmosis, and it belongs to the expulsion of the Hyksos a century before Moses. The second passage is the one that matters. Manetho tells of a king **Amenophis**, the Greek form of Amenhotep, in whose reign a despised people of Egypt, led by a priest of Heliopolis named Osarseph who "changed his name and was called Moses," left the country for Syria (*Against Apion* 1.26–27).

Josephus despised the story, and said so: the tale is malicious, its details are fable, and its Amenophis, he says, is "a fictitious king's name" set down without regnal years. Take Josephus's judgment of the details. Notice what survives it. An Egyptian priest, writing to disparage the Jews and with no interest in confirming their Scriptures, drew from Egyptian sources a tradition that the departure of Moses and his people happened under a king named Amenhotep. A hostile witness who agrees with you on the one point you can check is worth more than a friendly one.

Four pharaohs of the dynasty bore the name. Amenhotep I died in 1524 BC, before Moses was born. Amenhotep III came to the throne in 1410 BC, after Moses was dead. Amenhotep IV is Akhenaten, a generation later still. Only **Amenhotep II** reigned in 1446 BC.

## The Ramesses Alternative

Since the nineteenth century the popular candidate has been Ramesses II, who reigned from 1279 to 1213 BC. The case rests on one word, the store city "Raamses" of Exodus 1:11, matched to the ruins of Pi-Ramesses at Qantir, which Ramesses II built. Everything else in the late date is a consequence of protecting that match.

### The date must be rewritten

Ramesses II came to the throne 167 years after the Exodus. To reach him, 1 Kings 6:1 is reread: the 480 years are said to be twelve generations of a nominal forty years each, which are then shortened to twenty-five, yielding about 300 years and an Exodus near 1266 BC. The text says years, not generations. No reader supposed otherwise until a pharaoh needed the room. And Judges 11:26, as shown above, cannot be made to fit at all.

### The predecessor is too short

The king who sought Moses' life reigned from before Moses' flight until just before the Exodus. Ramesses II's predecessor Seti I reigned eleven years, from 1290 to 1279 BC. No king of the 19th Dynasty can fill Moses' forty years in Midian, except Ramesses II himself, and making him the oppressor pushes the Exodus to his son Merneptah, about 1210 BC, 236 years past the date of Scripture. Merneptah's own stele, from his fifth year, then finds "Israel" already a people in Canaan to be boasted over, with no forty years of wilderness and no conquest yet begun.

### The firstborn test does not save him, and does not by itself sink him

Ramesses II outlived his eldest son, Amun-her-khepeshef, and was succeeded by his thirteenth, Merneptah. On the firstborn requirement alone he passes, as Amenhotep II does. He fails on the date, twice over, and on the predecessor. Honesty about which tests exclude him is part of the case.

### "Raamses" is the narrator's name for the place

Scripture itself shows the habit. Abram pursued the kings "unto Dan" (Genesis 14:14), though "the name of the city was Laish at the first" and it became Dan only in the days of the judges (Judges 18:29). Moses wrote for readers who knew the later name. So with Rameses: Joseph settled Jacob's family "in the land of Rameses" (Genesis 47:11) some six centuries before any king of that name, and nobody dates Joseph by it. The Torah names the eastern Delta by the name its readers knew. Exodus 1:11 dates nothing.

### The tests side by side

| Requirement | Amenhotep II | Ramesses II | Merneptah |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reigning in 1446 BC (1 Kings 6:1; Judges 11:26) | Yes | No, 167 years late | No, 236 years late |
| Predecessor reigned from Moses' flight to the eve of the Exodus | Yes, Thutmose III, 54 years | No, Seti I, 11 years | Ramesses II, 66 years |
| Successor was not the firstborn | Yes, Thutmose IV | Yes, Merneptah | Seti II, his heir, succeeded |
| Named by Manetho | Yes, Amenophis | No | No |
| Royal presence at Avaris ended in his reign | Yes | No, Pi-Ramesses was his own new capital | No |

## Conclusion

Scripture gives the year. Egypt, read at that year, gives the man. Thutmose III, the longest-reigning king of his house, is the Pharaoh who sought Moses' life and died while he waited in Midian. Amenhotep II, his son, is the Pharaoh who heard "Let my people go," lost his firstborn at midnight and his chariots in the sea, and came home to a Delta emptied of its labor, which he tried to refill the next year with the largest haul of captives his dynasty ever recorded. Thutmose IV, a younger son, followed him and explained his crown with a dream. An Egyptian priest who wished Israel ill kept the name Amenhotep in the record, and the palace at Avaris fell silent in the same reign.

The mummy of Amenhotep II was found in his tomb in the Valley of the Kings and rests today in Cairo. When you look at it, you are looking at the man who dealt with Moses.

## Summary of Key Points

1. **1 Kings 6:1** places the Exodus 480 years before Solomon's fourth year (967/966 BC): the spring of **1446 BC**.
2. **Judges 11:26** confirms it; the late date would put Jephthah in the reign of Rehoboam.
3. Egyptian chronology offers two frameworks twenty-five years apart. **Scripture selects the higher**, because the Pharaoh of the oppression must die between Moses' flight and his return.
4. **Thutmose III** (1504–1450 BC) reigned fifty-four years and died four years before the Exodus, meeting Exodus 2:23 and Acts 7:30.
5. **Amenhotep II** (1453–1419 BC) is the only Amenhotep on the throne in 1446 BC, the king Manetho's tradition names.
6. His ninth-year campaign took **89,600 captives**, the largest of the dynasty, the year after the Exodus; he never campaigned in Asia again.
7. His heir **Thutmose IV** was not his firstborn, and pleaded a dream at the Sphinx to justify his crown.
8. The 18th Dynasty palace at **Avaris** was abandoned in Amenhotep II's reign.
9. **Ramesses II** fails the date twice and the predecessor test; his firstborn's death does not rescue him.
10. **"Raamses"** in Exodus 1:11 is the narrator's name for the district, as Genesis 47:11 and Genesis 14:14 show.
